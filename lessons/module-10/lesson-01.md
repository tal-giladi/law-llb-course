# 10.1 · Sources and structure of Jewish law (מקורות המשפט העברי)

By the end of this lesson you will be able to open a Jewish-law text you meet in a ruling or a rabbinical-court decision, say which layer of the tradition it comes from and how much weight it carries, and follow one civil-law rule from the Talmud through the great codes to the point where later authorities disagree about it.

## Why it matters

Jewish law (משפט עברי) matters to an Israeli law student for three different reasons, and it helps to keep them apart.

1. **It is the law the rabbinical courts apply** in marriage and divorce of Jews (lesson 10.3). A divorcing couple in Israel meets the Talmud and the Shulchan Arukh whether or not they are observant.
2. **It is a named source of Israeli law.** The Foundations of Law Act 1980 sends a judge facing a true gap to "the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage" (lesson 01.2, and lesson 10.2 for how judges use it).
3. **It is a legal system in its own right**, with three thousand years of reasoning about contracts, torts, property, evidence and procedure. Supreme Court justices cite it as comparative law and as a source of values, sometimes in the same ruling in which another justice rejects it (lesson 10.5).

You cannot use, or argue against, a Jewish-law passage without knowing what kind of text it is. A verse in the Torah, an opinion recorded in the Talmud that was not accepted, a ruling in the Shulchan Arukh and a modern responsum are not equal. This lesson gives you the map; the rest of the module puts it to work.

## The law

Here "the law" is the internal law of the Jewish legal tradition. These texts are not Israeli statutes; they bind the rabbinical courts in personal status and they are what Israeli judges quote when they consult Jewish law. Every text below is free on Sefaria.

### The claim to authority: Deuteronomy 17:11

> עַל־פִּי הַתּוֹרָה אֲשֶׁר יוֹרוּךָ וְעַל־הַמִּשְׁפָּט אֲשֶׁר־יֹאמְרוּ לְךָ תַּעֲשֶׂה לֹא תָסוּר מִן־הַדָּבָר אֲשֶׁר־יַגִּידוּ לְךָ יָמִין וּשְׂמֹאל׃

*Gist:* Act according to the instruction and the judgment the authorities of your time give you, and do not depart from it. [Deuteronomy 17:11 on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.17.11)

The tradition reads this verse as the source of the sages' power to interpret and to legislate. It is why the Oral Law, and not only the written text, is binding inside the system.

### The chain of tradition: Mishnah Avot 1:1

> משֶׁה קִבֵּל תּוֹרָה מִסִּינַי, וּמְסָרָהּ לִיהוֹשֻׁעַ, וִיהוֹשֻׁעַ לִזְקֵנִים, וּזְקֵנִים לִנְבִיאִים, וּנְבִיאִים מְסָרוּהָ לְאַנְשֵׁי כְנֶסֶת הַגְּדוֹלָה.

*Gist:* The law was received at Sinai and handed down through an unbroken chain of authorities. [Avot 1:1 on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_Avot.1.1)

This is "קבלה" (tradition) as a legal source: a rule is valid because it was received, not because a court reasoned its way to it.

### Civil law has its own chapter: Exodus 21:1

> וְאֵלֶּה הַמִּשְׁפָּטִים אֲשֶׁר תָּשִׂים לִפְנֵיהֶם׃

*Gist:* "These are the ordinances you shall set before them", the opening of the Torah's code of civil and criminal law (slavery, injury, damage, bailment, loans). [Exodus 21:1 on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.21.1)

Jewish law draws a line between civil law (דיני ממונות), where parties may make their own terms and courts may legislate, and ritual law (איסור והיתר), where they may not. The term "משפט עברי" as an academic field usually means the civil part. That line matters in Israel: the Foundations of Law Act and the Supreme Court's references to Jewish law are almost always about the civil part.

### A moral sanction where the law gives none: Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:2

> אֲבָל אָמְרוּ, מִי שֶׁפָּרַע מֵאַנְשֵׁי דוֹר הַמַּבּוּל וּמִדּוֹר הַפַּלָּגָה, הוּא עָתִיד לְהִפָּרַע מִמִּי שֶׁאֵינוֹ עוֹמֵד בְּדִבּוּרוֹ.

*Gist:* A buyer who paid but has not yet performed the act of acquisition may legally withdraw, but "He who punished the generation of the Flood will punish one who does not keep his word". [Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:2 on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Bava_Metzia.4.2)

Keep this text in mind for lesson 10.2: a Supreme Court justice used exactly this kind of source when asking whether a duty of good faith in negotiations can carry damages.

### The state's law inside Jewish law: Gittin 10b

> אָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל: דִּינָא דְּמַלְכוּתָא – דִּינָא.

*Gist:* Shmuel's rule that "the law of the kingdom is law": the civil law of the ruling state binds Jews in certain matters. [Gittin 10b on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Gittin.10b)

### And its limit: Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat 26:1

> אסור לדון בפני דייני עכו"ם ובערכאות שלהם [...] אפי' נתרצו ב' בעלי דינים לדון בפניהם אסור

*Gist:* It is forbidden to litigate before non-Jewish judges and courts, even if both parties consent. [Choshen Mishpat 26:1 on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Choshen_Mishpat.26.1)

Whether Israel's civil courts count as "ערכאות" for this rule is itself debated among halakhic authorities. It explains why some observant litigants prefer a private rabbinical tribunal for civil disputes, and why the state's rabbinical courts may not act as arbitrators outside their jurisdiction (Amir, lesson 01.4).

## Leading cases

This is a theory lesson; the key texts are the sources themselves and the standard scholarly framework.

### The literary sources (המקורות הספרותיים), in order

| Layer | What it is | Date | Free text |
|---|---|---|---|
| Written Torah | The Pentateuch; the "ultimate source" (Elon's term: המקור העליון) | ancient | [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.21.1) |
| Mishnah | Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi's code of the Oral Law, 6 orders; civil law in Nezikin (Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia, Bava Batra, Sanhedrin) | c. 200 CE | [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Bava_Metzia.4.2) |
| Talmud (Bavli) | The discussion of the Mishnah by the amoraim; records disputes and sometimes the ruling ("והלכתא") | c. 500 CE | [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.12b) |
| Codes | Maimonides' Mishneh Torah (12th c.); Arba'ah Turim (14th c.); Rabbi Joseph Karo's Shulchan Arukh (16th c.) with Rabbi Moses Isserles' glosses (הגה, the Rema) for Ashkenazi practice | 1180–1570s | [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Choshen_Mishpat.174.1) |
| Commentaries on the codes | Shakh, Sma, Ketzot ha-Choshen and others on Choshen Mishpat | 17th c. on | Sefaria (partial) |
| Responsa (שו״ת) | Authorities' written answers to real questions; the "case law" of Jewish law | 9th c. to today | partly on Sefaria; large paid databases |
| Rabbinical-court rulings | Israel's state rabbinical courts publish decisions (anonymised) | 1948 on | rabbinical courts' site (lesson 10.6) |

### The legal sources (המקורות המשפטיים): Elon's framework

Menachem Elon, professor of Jewish law and later Deputy President of the Supreme Court, organised the field in his three-volume המשפט העברי – תולדותיו, מקורותיו, עקרונותיו (1973; English translation 1994). His central move was to borrow general jurisprudence: alongside the *literary* sources (where you find the law), he described the *legal* sources (what makes a rule law), the ways the system creates and changes rules:

1. **קבלה (tradition)** — a rule received from earlier authorities (Avot 1:1).
2. **מדרש (interpretation)** — a rule derived by interpreting the Torah's text, the system's main tool of development.
3. **תקנה (legislation)** — enactments by sages or communities, such as the takkanot that protected the wife's ketubah or let communities set tax and commercial rules.
4. **מנהג (custom)** — practice that can create or even override a rule in civil law, especially commercial custom.
5. **מעשה (precedent)** — a decided case or an authority's conduct as a source of law.
6. **סברא (legal reasoning)** — a rule justified by reason alone ("סברא היא").

Two of Elon's points matter for Israeli practice. First, the civil part of Jewish law has always left room for legislation, custom and the parties' own agreement, so it can coexist with a modern state's statutes. Second, he argued that integrating Jewish law into Israeli law was the way to keep it alive. Critics, including other scholars and justices, answered that a religious tradition cannot be lifted into a secular legal system without changing its meaning, and that its use in Israeli courts should be limited to comparative inspiration (lesson 10.2). Both views are part of the field; you need to recognise each.

The book is not free online. The bibliographic entry is linked in this lesson's sources; university libraries hold it.

## How it works in practice

### Reading a Jewish-law reference in a ruling

When a judgment or a rabbinical-court decision cites a Jewish-law source, ask four questions:

1. **Which layer?** "בבא בתרא יב ע״ב" is Talmud; "רמב״ם, הל׳ שכנים יב, א" is Mishneh Torah; "שו״ע חו״מ קעד, א" is Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat (the civil-law part); "הגה" or "רמ״א" is Isserles' gloss; "שו״ת" plus a name is a responsum.
2. **Is it the ruling or one view?** The Talmud records views that were rejected. Look for "והלכתא" (and the law is) or for how the codes decided.
3. **Is there disagreement in the codes?** The Shulchan Arukh often records "ויש אומרים" (some say) and the Rema adds Ashkenazi practice. A rabbinical court may follow either, depending on community and on later authorities.
4. **Civil or ritual?** In civil law the parties' agreement, custom and "דינא דמלכותא" can change the result. In personal status (marriage, divorce) they mostly cannot.

### Finding the text for free

- **Sefaria** (sefaria.org) has the Torah, Mishnah, both Talmuds, Mishneh Torah, Shulchan Arukh and many commentaries, in Hebrew with English translations of most of them. Search by reference ("Bava Batra 12b") or by phrase ("מדת סדום").
- **Israeli rulings that discuss Jewish law** in English: the Cardozo Israeli Supreme Court Project tags them under "Jewish Law" (lesson 10.5).
- Paid tools (the Bar-Ilan Responsa Project, commercial databases) are what professionals use for responsa. You do not need them for this course.

### Citation

Israeli lawyers cite Jewish law in Hebrew abbreviations: `בבלי, בבא בתרא יב ע"ב`; `רמב"ם, הלכות שכנים יב, א`; `שולחן ערוך, חושן משפט קעד, א (ובהגה)`. The uniform citation rules (lesson 02.5) cover Israeli sources; for Jewish law follow the form the courts use.

## Worked example

Tamar Levi and her brother Yoni in Kfar Saba inherit a plot jointly. Yoni owns the adjacent plot and asks that, when they split the inheritance, his half be the strip next to his own land. Tamar refuses "on principle": both strips are equal in value. Yoni has read that Jewish law forces her to agree. Trace the rule.

1. **Talmud.** Bava Batra 12b: a man bought land next to his wife's family's field; when the brothers divided, he asked for the part bordering his. Rabbah said: "כגון זה – כופין על מדת סדום" (in such a case we compel against the trait of Sodom, refusing a benefit that costs you nothing). Rav Yosef objected that the brothers could say this land is now worth more to them, "והלכתא כרב יוסף": the law follows Rav Yosef in that case. ([Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.12b))
2. **Maimonides.** Hilkhot Shekhenim 12:1 states the rule for partners or brothers dividing a field of equal quality: if one asks for the part next to his other field, "שומעין לו וכופה אותו על זה שעכוב בדבר זה מדת סדום היא" (we grant it, because refusing is the trait of Sodom). If the parts differ in quality, they draw lots. ([Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Neighbors.12.1))
3. **Shulchan Arukh.** Choshen Mishpat 174:1 repeats Maimonides, then the Rema adds two dissenting views: some say the partners need not agree unless he pays more ("וי"א דאין שומעין לו אלא צריך להעלות בדמים"), and some say it is doubtful ("ספק"). ([Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Choshen_Mishpat.174.1))
4. **Result inside Jewish law.** Under Maimonides and Karo, Yoni is right if the strips really are equal. Under the views the Rema records, he may have to pay a premium. A rabbinical tribunal would ask which tradition the parties follow.
5. **Result in Israeli law.** The division of jointly owned land is governed by the Land Law 1969 and decided by a civil court; the rabbinical court has no jurisdiction unless all parties consent to it in a personal-status matter (lesson 10.3). Jewish law is not the governing law here. But the same principle, "כופין על מדת סדום", was argued in the Supreme Court as a guide to the remedy in a condominium dispute (Roker v. Salomon, lesson 10.5). Tamar and Yoni now know where the idea comes from, and that it is contested even inside Jewish law.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Sefaria (free, no account needed for reading).
- The table of literary sources above.
- 45 minutes.

### Steps

1. Open [Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat 174:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Choshen_Mishpat.174.1). Copy the main ruling (Karo) and the Rema's gloss into two columns.
2. Use the "Connections" panel or the commentaries to find the source the Shulchan Arukh cites. Confirm it leads back to Maimonides and to Bava Batra 12b.
3. On [Bava Batra 12b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.12b), find each case in which Rabbah says "כופין על מדת סדום" and Rav Yosef objects. For each, write one line: the facts, Rabbah's view, Rav Yosef's objection, and the ruling ("והלכתא").
4. Classify the rule by Elon's legal sources: is "כופין על מדת סדום" tradition, interpretation, legislation, custom, precedent or legal reasoning? Write two sentences defending your answer. (There is more than one defensible answer; the point is the reasoning.)
5. Repeat steps 1–3 briefly for "דינא דמלכותא דינא": [Gittin 10b](https://www.sefaria.org/Gittin.10b), then [Mishneh Torah, Gezela va-Aveda 5:11](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Robbery_and_Lost_Property.5.11), then [Choshen Mishpat 369:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Choshen_Mishpat.369.8). Note one limit the Rema records on the rule.
6. Write a one-paragraph "source note" for each rule, in the citation form above.

### Template

None. Use a table with columns: layer, reference, text (Hebrew), what it says (English), ruling or one view.

### How you know it worked

- Each rule has at least three layers with correct references.
- You marked which opinions the Talmud rejected and which views the Rema adds.
- Your classification under Elon's sources gives a reason, not just a label.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Reading Jewish-law sources is a research skill anyone may use. It shows up in practice in two places: in the rabbinical court, where your own case may be decided by these texts (lesson 10.6), and in civil courts, where a judge may cite them as values or comparative law (lessons 10.2 and 10.5).

### Where and how

- Free texts: Sefaria. Free English translations of Israeli rulings that discuss Jewish law: the Cardozo Israeli Supreme Court Project.
- In the rabbinical court you may argue from halakhic sources yourself; the dayanim know them far better than you, so use them to frame a point, not to lecture.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- A halakhic question decides your personal status (for example whether a marriage is valid, a child's status, or whether a get can be compelled). Get a lawyer or a licensed rabbinical pleader (טוען רבני) who practises in that court (lesson 10.6).
- You are asked to sign an arbitration deed (שטר בוררות) before a rabbinical tribunal in a civil dispute: you may be giving up the civil court and the right of appeal.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quoting a Talmudic opinion that was rejected | The argument collapses when the other side reads the next line | Check "והלכתא" and the codes; cite the ruling | Always read to the end of the passage and check the code |
| Citing only the Shulchan Arukh and missing the Rema | You present a contested rule as settled | Add the gloss and the dissenting views | Read the הגה and the main commentaries |
| Treating a verse as an operative rule | Courts and dayanim treat it as a source, not a ruling | Follow the verse into the Mishnah, Talmud and codes | Cite the code for the rule, the verse for the root |
| Assuming Jewish law governs a civil dispute in Israel | Wrong legal basis; claim dismissed or sent elsewhere | Find the Israeli statute; use Jewish law as values or comparison | Run the layers check in lesson 01.2 first |
| Mixing up civil and ritual rules | You argue that parties can contract out of a rule they cannot | Check whether the rule is דיני ממונות or איסור והיתר | Classify the rule before arguing |

## Review

- Jewish law has literary layers (Torah, Mishnah, Talmud, codes, commentaries, responsa) of different weight; the codes and the later authorities decide what the Talmud left open.
- Elon's framework separates literary sources from legal sources: tradition, interpretation, legislation, custom, precedent and legal reasoning.
- Civil law (דיני ממונות) is flexible: agreement, custom and "דינא דמלכותא דינא" can shape it. Ritual and personal-status law is not.
- One rule ("כופין על מדת סדום") runs from Bava Batra 12b through Maimonides to the Shulchan Arukh, where the Rema records dissent.
- These texts bind the rabbinical courts in personal status and inform, but do not govern, civil-court decisions.
