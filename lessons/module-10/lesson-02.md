# 10.2 · Jewish law in the Israeli legal system: Foundations of Law Act and the courts

By the end of this lesson you will be able to say, for a given dispute in a civil court, whether Jewish law can enter the decision at all, by which route (gap-filling, interpretation, values or comparison), and how much weight a judge is likely to give it, and you will be able to present the long-running debate about that role fairly.

## Why it matters

Lesson 01.2 showed that the Foundations of Law Act 1980 replaced English law with "the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage" as the source for true gaps. In practice that gap almost never opens, and yet Jewish law appears in hundreds of Supreme Court judgments. It enters by other doors: judges use it to interpret statutes, to give content to the phrase "a Jewish and democratic state", and as a comparative source alongside English, American or German law.

For a self-represented party this has two consequences. First, a Jewish-law argument in a civil court is a supporting argument, never your main one; your main argument is the Israeli statute and Israeli case law. Second, when the other side or the judge raises Jewish law, you need to know whether it is being used as binding law (rare), as an interpretive aid or as inspiration, because the answer tells you how to respond.

The role of Jewish law in Israeli courts is contested. Some lawyers and judges see it as the natural historical source of a Jewish state's law; others see its religious character, and its rules on women and non-Jews, as a reason to keep it at the level of values and comparison. This lesson presents both and decides neither.

## The law

### The gap-filler: Foundations of Law Act s. 1 (as amended 2018)

> ראה בית המשפט שאלה משפטית הטעונה הכרעה, ולא מצא לה תשובה בדבר חקיקה, בהלכה פסוקה או בדרך של היקש, יכריע בה לאור עקרונות החירות, הצדק, היושר והשלום של המשפט העברי ומורשת ישראל.

*Gist:* Only where statute, case law and analogy all fail does the court decide by the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage. [Full text, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט#סעיף_1)

The 2018 amendment (ס״ח 2715, 2 May 2018, p. 638) inserted the words "המשפט העברי ו" before "מורשת ישראל". [Amendment, Reshumot PDF](https://fs.knesset.gov.il/20/law/20_lsr_499371.pdf) It did not change the lacuna threshold. Read the section as four steps:

1. Is there a legal question that requires decision?
2. Does any statute answer it, directly or by interpretation?
3. Does any case law answer it?
4. Can it be answered by analogy from statute or case law?

Only a "no" at steps 2–4 opens the door, and then to *principles*, not to specific halakhic rules. A judge applying s. 1 asks what freedom, justice, equity and peace require in light of the tradition; he does not apply a paragraph of the Shulchan Arukh as such.

### The values route: Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty s. 1א

> חוק־יסוד זה, מטרתו להגן על כבוד האדם וחירותו, כדי לעגן בחוק־יסוד את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל כמדינה יהודית ודמוקרטית.

*Gist:* The Basic Law protects human dignity and liberty in order to anchor the values of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state. [Full text, s. 1א](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_1א)

The phrase "Jewish and democratic" appears in the Basic Laws on dignity and on occupation and in the limitation clause (module 6). Judges who want to give content to "Jewish values" often turn to Jewish-law sources. How abstractly to read "Jewish" is itself one of the central debates of Israeli constitutional theory: one reading looks to universal values that Judaism shares with democracy; another looks to the specific content of the Jewish legal tradition.

### What s. 2 kept

> (ב) אין בהוראות סעיף קטן (א) כדי לפגוע במשפט שנקלט בארץ לפני תחילת חוק זה.

*Gist:* English law absorbed before 1980 remains part of Israeli law. [Full text, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט#סעיף_2)

So s. 1 does not "Judaise" existing law; it only governs future gaps.

### The four routes in one table

| Route | Legal basis | Binding? | How often |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gap-filling | Foundations of Law Act s. 1 | Yes, but only principles, only for a true lacuna | Very rare |
| Interpreting a statute | Ordinary purposive interpretation; a statute that uses a Jewish-law term or concept | It shapes the meaning of the Israeli statute | Occasional |
| Values | "Jewish and democratic" in the Basic Laws; the legal system's general values | Part of the interpretive balance | Regular in constitutional cases |
| Comparison / inspiration | The court's general freedom to consult other systems | No | Common |

## Leading cases

### ע״א 6370/00 קל בנין בע״מ נ׳ ע.ר.מ. רעננה לבניה והשכרה בע״מ (2002)

A developer ran a private tender, negotiated a full agreement with the winning contractor, and then gave the work to someone who had not bid. The Supreme Court had held in an earlier round that this was a breach of good faith in negotiations (Contracts Law s. 12); the question now was the remedy. The court allowed expectation damages (lost profit), not only reliance damages. President Barak based this on ss. 12 and 39 of the Contracts Law as fundamental provisions of Israeli law. Justice Englard, concurring, considered whether, if s. 12 did not itself yield expectation damages, the answer could come from filling a lacuna under the Foundations of Law Act; he surveyed Jewish law on reneging before a formal act of acquisition (the "מי שפרע" sanction of Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:2, lesson 10.1), found halakhic support for reliance damages but only a *moral* sanction for expectation loss, and asked whether a civil court may "develop" a moral sanction into a legal one.

Why it matters: it is a rare, explicit judicial walk through the s. 1 procedure, and it shows the method: statute first, Jewish law only on the assumption of a gap, and an honest account of where Jewish law stops. [Cardozo translation (Jewish-law portions)](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/219)

### ע״א 6024/97 שביט נ׳ חברה קדישא גחש״א ראשון לציון (1999)

A daughter wanted her mother's tombstone to show the Gregorian dates as well as the Hebrew ones; the Jewish burial society refused. The Supreme Court held that burial societies have a public character and are subject to public-law norms, that the Alternative Burial Law had not overruled earlier rulings requiring them to allow non-Hebrew lettering, and that the family's dignity outweighed the offence to religious visitors, noting that the Jewish-law prohibition on foreign dates and letters is "not sweeping and comprehensive".

Why it matters: the court engaged with the *content* of Jewish law as one input in a constitutional balance. Jewish law was neither the governing law nor ignored; its own internal flexibility was part of the reasoning. [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/342)

### בג״ץ 153/87 שקדיאל נ׳ השר לענייני דתות (1988)

Leah Shakdiel, an observant teacher, was proposed for the Yerucham religious council and excluded because she is a woman. Justice Elon, the leading scholar of Jewish law on the bench, held for the court that a religious council is an administrative body that provides religious services but does not decide questions of religious law; its membership is governed by the general legal system and the principle of equality (Declaration of Independence, Women's Equal Rights Law), not by religious-law qualifications.

Why it matters: it draws the line that runs through this module. Where a body exercises state power, general Israeli law governs, even if the body is religious in character; religious law governs only where a statute gives it that role (personal status, lesson 10.3). [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/338)

### Preview: רע״א 6339/97 רוקר נ׳ סלומון (1999)

In a condominium dispute an expanded panel split on whether "כופין על מדת סדום" should shape the remedy for building on common property. You will read it in full in lesson 10.5. [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/316)

## How it works in practice

### The debate, in its two strongest forms

**For a strong role.** Jewish law is the historical legal tradition of the Jewish people; the Knesset chose it in 1980 as the source for gaps and strengthened the wording in 2018; a Jewish and democratic state should draw on its own tradition as other states draw on theirs; and the civil part of Jewish law is rich, flexible and often in harmony with modern values (good faith, protection of the weak party, limits on abuse of rights). On this view judges should consult it routinely, not only when a gap opens.

**For a limited role.** Israel's law serves citizens of every religion and of none; Jewish law is a religious system whose authority rests on revelation and on rabbinic institutions outside the state; some of its rules (on women's testimony, on non-Jews, on personal status) conflict with equality; and the s. 1 threshold shows that the Knesset intended a residual role. On this view judges should use Jewish law, like any foreign system, as optional inspiration, and choose its universal principles rather than its particular rules.

The current state of the law: s. 1 applies only to a true lacuna; courts may and do cite Jewish law as interpretive aid, value and comparison; no ruling treats a specific halakhic rule as binding in a civil court outside a statute that adopts it.

### When a self-represented party should use Jewish law

- **Rarely as your main argument.** In a civil court, lead with the statute and Israeli rulings.
- **Sometimes as support.** If your argument is about fairness, abuse of rights or good faith, a short reference to a Jewish-law principle that a Supreme Court justice already used (for example "מידת סדום" in Roker, or the moral duty to keep one's word discussed in Kal Binyan) can strengthen it. Cite the ruling, not only the Talmud.
- **Never to override a statute.** A Jewish-law rule cannot displace an Israeli statute in a civil court.

## Worked example

Nadia Haddad, a dentist in Nazareth, negotiated for two months with a contractor to renovate her clinic. They agreed every term by email, and the contractor asked her to cancel her other quote, which she did. The day before signing he withdrew because a larger job came up. She lost the deposit she had paid for equipment that only fits his plan and had to delay opening.

1. **Main legal basis.** Contracts (General Part) Law s. 12: a duty to negotiate in customary manner and in good faith; s. 12(ב) gives damages for loss caused "עקב המשא ומתן או עקב כריתת החוזה" (module 3).
2. **What damages?** Reliance (her wasted deposit and delay costs) is clearly available. For expectation damages she can rely on Kal Binyan, where the Supreme Court allowed them in a bad-faith tender.
3. **Does she need Jewish law?** No. There is a statute (s. 12) and case law (Kal Binyan). The Foundations of Law Act does not apply: there is no lacuna.
4. **Could Jewish law help?** Marginally, as support for the moral weight of keeping one's word, and only via Kal Binyan, where Justice Englard discussed it. Nadia, who is Christian, may well find that the Israeli statute and an Israeli ruling are more persuasive and more natural for her claim; she is under no obligation to argue from any religious tradition.
5. **Her letter and claim** cite s. 12, s. 12(ב) and Kal Binyan, itemise the reliance loss and, if she claims lost value, explain it.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The skill shows up in practice when you write a demand letter, a small claim or a response in a civil court and consider whether a Jewish-law argument adds anything. You may make any legal argument in your own case (reg. 170 of the Civil Procedure Regulations; small claims practice, module 8).

### Where and how

- In a small claim or magistrates' court case, put any Jewish-law reference in one or two sentences after your statutory argument, citing the Israeli ruling that used it.
- Free English translations of Supreme Court rulings that discuss Jewish law: the Cardozo project, tagged "Jewish Law". Hebrew originals: the Judiciary's decision search (module 2).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your case actually turns on whether there is a lacuna in Israeli law. That is a sophisticated argument and a sign the case needs professional help.
- The other side relies on Jewish law to claim a right a statute does not give, in a high-value dispute.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arguing the Foundations of Law Act when a statute applies | The court rejects the argument at step 2 | Rebuild the argument on the statute and case law | Run the four steps before writing |
| Quoting a halakhic rule as binding in a civil court | Your submission looks misinformed | Present it as a principle or comparison, via an Israeli ruling | Cite the Israeli ruling that used the source |
| Treating "Jewish and democratic" as a trump card | The court balances it with other values | Frame it as one interpretive consideration | Read the module 6 lessons on the limitation clause |
| Making the argument about identity or politics | Credibility lost; the judge disregards it | Restate it as a legal argument with a source | Write each side's legal reason, not its motive |
| Ignoring a Jewish-law point the other side raises | It goes unanswered in the judgment | Answer it: route, weight, and the statute that governs | Ask "binding, interpretive, value or comparison?" |

## Review

- Jewish law enters Israeli civil-court decisions by four routes: gap-filling (s. 1, very rare), interpretation, values ("Jewish and democratic") and comparison.
- Section 1 applies only after statute, case law and analogy fail, and then only to principles; the 2018 amendment added "המשפט העברי" but not a lower threshold.
- Kal Binyan shows the method: statute first, Jewish law only on the assumption of a gap, and honesty about where it stops.
- Shakdiel draws the line: a religious body exercising state power is governed by general law and equality.
- The debate between a strong and a limited role is live; know both arguments and the current state of the law.
