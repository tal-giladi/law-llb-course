# 10.4 · Muslim, Christian and Druze religious courts

By the end of this lesson you will be able to say, for a Muslim, Christian or Druze family in Israel, which court hears the marriage, the divorce, the maintenance and the children, where the Family Court offers an alternative, what happens when the spouses belong to different communities, and on what grounds the High Court of Justice will intervene in a religious court's decision.

## Why it matters

A large part of Israel's citizens are Muslim, Christian or Druze. For them, as for Jews, marriage and divorce run through a religious court applying the community's law: the Sharia courts for Muslims, the Druze religious courts, and the ecclesiastical courts of each recognised Christian community. The rules are not the same as for Jews. Since 2001 Muslims and Christians may take maintenance, custody and property to the Family Court instead; the Druze court has a narrower exclusive core; and a Christian community whose law does not allow divorce changes what "ending a marriage" means.

Treating every community with the same care also means noticing what is common: every religious court in Israel is a state court for this purpose, bound by statutes such as the Women's Equal Rights Law, required to follow the mandatory dispute-resolution stage of lesson 10.3, and subject to review by the High Court of Justice.

## The law

### Muslims: Order in Council art. 52

> לבתי הדין המושלמים הדתיים יהא שיפוט־יחיד בעניני המצב האישי של מושלמים שהם נתינים ישראליים [...]

*Gist:* The Sharia courts have exclusive jurisdiction over the personal status of Muslim Israeli citizens (and of foreigners subject to them under their national law); appeal lies to the Sharia Court of Appeals. [Full text, art. 52](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/דברי_המלך_במועצה_על_ארץ-ישראל#סעיף_52)

"Personal status" here is the full list of art. 51(1): marriage and divorce, maintenance, guardianship, legitimacy of minors and more. The Sharia courts apply Islamic law as codified for them, notably the Ottoman Family Rights Law 1917, together with the Israeli statutes that bind religious courts. Qadis are Muslims appointed by the President on the recommendation of a nine-member selection committee chaired by the Minister of Justice, after written examinations ([Qadis Law ss. 2–4](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הקאדים#סעיף_2)).

### Christians: Order in Council art. 54

> לבתי הדין של העדות הנוצריות השונות יהא:– (i) שיפוט יחיד בעניני נישואין וגטין, מזונות של חברים מבני עדתם שאינם נתינים נכריים [...] (ii) שיפוט בכל הענינים האחרים של מצבם האישי של אותם האנשים, כשכל הצדדים מסכימים לקבל את שיפוטם.

*Gist:* The courts of each recognised Christian community have exclusive jurisdiction over marriage, divorce and maintenance of their members, and jurisdiction over other personal-status matters only with all parties' consent. [Full text, art. 54](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/דברי_המלך_במועצה_על_ארץ-ישראל#סעיף_54)

The Second Schedule lists the recognised communities: Eastern (Orthodox), Latin (Catholic), Gregorian-Armenian, Armenian Catholic, Syrian Catholic, Chaldean (Uniate), Greek Catholic Melkite, Maronite and Syrian Orthodox, together with the Jewish community; later additions recorded on the consolidated text are the Evangelical Episcopal Church (1970) and the Baha'i faith (1971). A Christian whose church is not recognised (for example many Protestant denominations) has no religious court; their personal status goes to the civil Family Court. [Second Schedule](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/דברי_המלך_במועצה_על_ארץ-ישראל#תוספת_2)

### Druze: Druze Religious Courts Law 1962, s. 4

> עניני נישואין וגירושין של דרוזים בישראל, אזרחי המדינה או תושביה, יהיו בשיפוטו הייחודי של בית הדין; [...]

*Gist:* Marriage and divorce of Druze citizens and residents are exclusively for the Druze religious court, which also has exclusive jurisdiction over Druze religious endowments; other personal-status matters require all parties' consent (s. 5). [Full text, ss. 4–5](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בתי_הדין_הדתיים_הדרוזיים#סעיף_4)

The Druze are a recognised religious community ([1995 regulations, reg. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/תקנות_העדות_הדתיות_(ארגונן)_(העדה_הדרוזית))). The court applies the community's personal-status law, which prohibits polygamy and allows divorce by mutual consent declared before the qadi-madhab (unofficial Hebrew translation on Wikisource; it was not published in Reshumot).

### The Family Court alternative: Family Court Law s. 3(ב1)

> (ב1) על אף הוראות סעיף 25, בית המשפט לעניני משפחה יהא גם הוא מוסמך לדון בעניני משפחה של מי שנקבעה לגביו סמכות שיפוט ייחודית בסימנים 52 או 54 לדבר המלך במועצה על ארץ ישראל, 1922–1947, למעט עניני נישואין וגירושין.

*Gist:* Since the 2001 amendment, the Family Court may also hear the family matters of Muslims and Christians, except marriage and divorce themselves. [Full text, s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בית_המשפט_לעניני_משפחה#סעיף_3)

Note what the section does not cover: the Druze (s. 4 of the Druze law is not mentioned, so the general rules of lesson 10.3 apply to them, with jurisdiction by consent for matters outside the exclusive core).

### Mixed and non-community couples: the 1969 Law

> (א) עניני התרת נישואין שאינם בשיפוטו הייחודי של בית דין דתי יהיו בשיפוטו של בית המשפט לעניני משפחה [...]

*Gist:* Dissolution of a marriage that no religious court has exclusive jurisdiction over (for example spouses of different religions, or a spouse of no recognised community) is heard by the Family Court. [Full text, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_שיפוט_בעניני_התרת_נישואין_(מקרים_מיוחדים_וסמכות_בין-לאומית)#סעיף_1)

If either spouse belongs to a recognised community, the court first asks the head of that community's court whether a religious divorce is needed (and, for a Christian court, whether its law allows divorce); if so, that part goes to the religious court (s. 3). Separately, art. 55 of the Order in Council lets the President of the Supreme Court decide which court hears a personal-status case involving members of different communities.

### Criminal law around marriage

> נשוי הנושא אשה אחרת, ונשואה הנישאת לאיש אחר, דינם – מאסר חמש שנים.

> התיר איש את קשר הנישואין על כרחה של האשה, באין בשעת התרת הקשר פסק דין סופי של בית המשפט או של בית הדין המוסמך המחייב את האשה להתרה זו, דינו – מאסר חמש שנים.

*Gist:* Polygamy (s. 176) and divorcing a wife against her will without a final judgment of a competent court (s. 181) are offences, for every community. [Penal Law ss. 176, 181](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין#סעיף_176) Marrying a minor is also an offence ([Marriage Age Law s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_גיל_הנישואין#סעיף_2)).

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 187/54 בריה נ׳ קאדי בית הדין השרעי המוסלמי (1955)

After a Muslim father's death, his sister took his children and applied to the Sharia court for guardianship, relying on Sharia law; the mother relied on s. 3 of the Women's Equal Rights Law 1951, which makes both parents natural guardians. The HCJ held the petition premature, because the qadi had not yet decided, but Justices Goitein and Berinson stated the principle: if a religious court confines itself to religious law and refuses to apply the civil statute on women's equal rights, it acts without jurisdiction and the HCJ will intervene.

Why it matters: it established, early and for a Sharia court, the rule now in s. 7(ב) of the Women's Equal Rights Law: religious courts are bound by the statutes the Knesset directs to them. [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/81)

### בג״ץ 3856/11 פלונית נ׳ בית הדין השרעי לערעורים (27.6.2013)

In a Sharia "arbitration" divorce claim under s. 130 of the Ottoman Family Rights Law, the wife wanted to appoint a woman as her arbitrator. The Sharia Court of Appeals held that the Maliki interpretation behind s. 130 requires male arbitrators. The HCJ (Arbel, Hayut, Sohlberg) allowed the petition: the court had departed from s. 1א(א) of the Women's Equal Rights Law ("one law for women and men for every legal act"), and neither exception the court relied on (rules of prohibition and permission; appointment to a religious office) applied to a party's choice of arbitrator.

Why it matters: it shows the modern form of review. The HCJ does not sit as an appeal court over religious courts, but departure from a statute that binds them is a ground for intervention. [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/139)

### בג״ץ 143/62 פונק־שלזינגר נ׳ שר הפנים (1963)

A Christian Belgian woman married a Jewish Israeli in a civil ceremony in Cyprus. The Population Registry refused to register her as married. The HCJ held that the registration official's role is statistical: presented with a public certificate of marriage, he must register it and may not examine its validity. The ruling was later extended to same-sex couples married abroad (HCJ 3045/05 Ben-Ari, 2006).

Why it matters: it is the legal basis for registering interfaith and civil marriages contracted abroad. Registration is not a ruling on validity; the validity question can arise later, in divorce or inheritance, and then the 1969 Law and lesson 10.3 apply. [Cardozo translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/160)

## How it works in practice

### The map by community

| Issue | Muslims | Christians (recognised community) | Druze |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marriage and divorce | Sharia court, exclusive (art. 52) | Community court, exclusive (art. 54) | Druze court, exclusive (s. 4) |
| Maintenance | Sharia court, or Family Court (s. 3(ב1)) | Community court (exclusive under art. 54(i)) or Family Court (s. 3(ב1)) | Druze court by consent (s. 5); otherwise Family Court |
| Custody, guardianship | Sharia court or Family Court | Community court by consent, or Family Court | Druze court by consent, or Family Court |
| Property between spouses | Family Court (civil claim between family members) | Family Court | Family Court |
| Appeal | Sharia Court of Appeals | Each community's own appeal instance (art. 54(iv) also lets these courts hear appeals from courts abroad) | Druze Court of Appeals (s. 6) |
| Review | HCJ (Basic Law: The Judiciary s. 15(ד)(4)) | HCJ | HCJ |

Where both the religious court and the Family Court can hear a matter, the race and the request-to-settle stage of lesson 10.3 apply. The Dispute Resolution Law covers all four court systems (s. 2), and for requests filed in the Sharia courts the days of Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha are not counted (s. 6א(2)).

### Practical points

- **Sharia courts** sit in several cities and have online services and forms on gov.il (for example a woman's application for a marriage permit, or a maintenance claim in special cases; ⚠️ check on gov.il). Fees are set by the Sharia Courts (Fees) Regulations; amounts in [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).
- **Christian courts** are run by the churches themselves; their procedure, language and fees are set by each church; ask the court office for its rules before filing. Catholic communities generally grant annulment or separation rather than divorce; the Dispute Resolution Law expressly treats "separation under religious law that does not allow divorce" as divorce for its purposes.
- **Druze courts** have their own procedure and fee regulations, an appeals court, and a disciplinary process and ombudsman review similar to civil judges'.
- **Assistance units** of the Welfare Ministry operate at the religious courts as well as the Family Courts.

## Worked example

Three families in the Galilee.

1. **Samira and Khaled, Muslims from Sakhnin, married 9 years, two children.** Samira wants a divorce and maintenance for the children. The divorce must go to the Sharia court (art. 52). For child maintenance and custody she may choose the Sharia court or the Family Court (s. 3(ב1)). Before filing anything she files a request to settle the dispute (lesson 10.3); after the mahut meetings, as first filer she chooses where to file each issue. If the Sharia court refuses a request she makes on the basis of equality (for example her choice of arbitrator), she has HCJ 3856/11 on her side.
2. **George and Rania, Greek Catholics from Haifa.** Their marriage has broken down. Their church court can grant separation or annulment, according to its own law; maintenance can go to the church court or to the Family Court. Rania prefers the Family Court for maintenance and files the request to settle there.
3. **Wassim (Druze, Daliyat al-Karmel) and Noa (Jewish, Tel Aviv), married civilly in Cyprus and registered as married under Funk-Schlesinger.** No single religious court has exclusive jurisdiction over a Druze-Jewish couple, so dissolution goes to the Family Court (1969 Law s. 1). The court sends inquiries to the heads of the Druze court and of the rabbinical courts on whether a religious divorce is needed (s. 3) and proceeds according to their answers. Property and the children are in the Family Court.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes. You may appear in person in the Sharia, Druze and Christian courts and in the Family Court. The Bar Association Law's monopoly does not apply in religious courts (s. 21); each system has its own rules on who else may represent you (the Druze law, for example, authorises rules for representation by non-lawyers, s. 30(2)). Check the forum's rules before you bring anyone with you. See [Can I act without a lawyer?](../../references/can-i-act-without-a-lawyer.md).

### Where and how

- Sharia and Druze courts: gov.il service pages and the court secretariats (⚠️ check on gov.il). Christian courts: the community's court office.
- Request to settle the dispute: to the forum you choose, before any claim.
- Free help: assistance units at the religious courts; Legal Aid; women's organisations and legal clinics that work with Arabic-speaking clients ([where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md)).
- Fees: [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your spouse divorced you unilaterally or threatens to, or plans a second marriage (criminal offences under ss. 181 and 176; you may also need urgent relief).
- One spouse is abroad, or the marriage was contracted abroad and its validity is disputed.
- A child may be taken out of the country.
- The court applies a religious rule that seems to contradict an equality statute: an HCJ petition has strict timing and form (module 6).

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filing a Muslim or Christian divorce in the Family Court | Dismissed for lack of jurisdiction | Refile in the community's court | Remember s. 3(ב1) excludes marriage and divorce |
| Assuming the Druze have the s. 3(ב1) option | Wrong forum argument | Use s. 5 (consent) or the Family Court for non-core matters | Read which articles s. 3(ב1) names (52 and 54 only) |
| Belonging to an unrecognised church and filing in a church court | No jurisdiction | File in the Family Court | Check the Second Schedule |
| Filing maintenance before the request to settle | Not heard during the stay | File the request first | Lesson 10.3 applies to every community |
| Treating a Funk-Schlesinger registration as proof of validity | Surprise in divorce or inheritance | Raise validity in the right forum | Registration is statistical only |
| Petitioning the HCJ against the merits of religious law | Petition dismissed | Frame it as departure from a binding statute or natural justice | Read Barriya and HCJ 3856/11 |

## Review

- Sharia courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Muslims' personal status (art. 52); Christian community courts over marriage, divorce and maintenance (art. 54); Druze courts over Druze marriage and divorce (s. 4).
- Since 2001 the Family Court may also hear Muslims' and Christians' family matters except marriage and divorce (s. 3(ב1)); the Druze are not covered by that section.
- Couples of different communities, or of no recognised community, dissolve their marriage in the Family Court, with inquiries to the religious courts (1969 Law).
- Every religious court is bound by statutes directed to it (Women's Equal Rights Law s. 7) and reviewed by the HCJ for jurisdiction, natural justice and departure from such statutes (Barriya; HCJ 3856/11).
- Polygamy, unilateral divorce against the wife's will and child marriage are offences for all communities.
