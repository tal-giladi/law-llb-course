# 06.2 · The constitutional revolution: Bank Mizrahi and judicial review

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain how Israeli courts came to strike down laws passed by the Knesset, decide whether a particular law is open to that kind of review at all, and brief the ruling that started it, Bank Mizrahi. You will also be able to state the case for and against judicial review as its supporters and critics would.

## Why it matters

Until 1992 an Israeli court could protect your rights against the government but not against the Knesset. If a minister acted without legal authority, the High Court of Justice could cancel the decision; if the Knesset passed a law that harmed you, the court could only interpret it narrowly. In 1992 the Knesset passed Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty and Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation (re-enacted in 1994), and in 1995 the Supreme Court held in Bank Mizrahi that these Basic Laws outrank ordinary laws. Since then, a limited number of statutory provisions (roughly two dozen by most counts) have been declared void, wholly or partly.

President Barak called this the "constitutional revolution". The name is itself contested: supporters see the completion of a process the Knesset began; critics see the Court giving itself a power the Knesset never clearly granted. Both views shape how judges, ministers and Knesset members behave today, and the 2023 reform proposals (lesson 06.6) were in large part a response to it.

## The law

### Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty

> חוק־יסוד זה, מטרתו להגן על כבוד האדם וחירותו, כדי לעגן בחוק־יסוד את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל כמדינה יהודית ודמוקרטית.

*Gist:* The purpose is to protect human dignity and liberty in order to anchor the values of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state. [s. 1א](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_1א)

> אין בחוק־יסוד זה כדי לפגוע בתקפו של דין שהיה קיים ערב תחילתו של חוק־היסוד.

*Gist:* The Basic Law does not affect the validity of law in force before it began (March 1992). [s. 10](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_10)

> כל רשות מרשויות השלטון חייבת לכבד את הזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה.

*Gist:* Every governmental authority must respect the rights in the Basic Law. [s. 11](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_11)

The limitation clause (s. 8) is quoted in lesson 06.1 and analysed in lesson 06.3. Note again: this Basic Law has no entrenchment clause and no override clause.

### Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation and the override clause

> הוראת חוק הפוגעת בחופש העיסוק תהיה תקפה אף כשאינה בהתאם לסעיף 4, אם נכללה בחוק שנתקבל ברוב של חברי הכנסת ונאמר בו במפורש, שהוא תקף על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד זה; תוקפו של חוק כאמור יפקע בתום ארבע שנים מיום תחילתו, זולת אם נקבע בו מועד מוקדם יותר.

*Gist:* A law that infringes freedom of occupation is valid even if it fails the limitation clause, if passed by 61 members and expressly stated to be valid despite the Basic Law; it lapses after four years. [s. 8(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_חופש_העיסוק#סעיף_8)

The override has been used once, in the Meat and Meat Products Law 1994, which bans importing meat without a kashrut certificate:

> תוקפו של חוק זה הוא על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד: חופש העיסוק.

*Gist:* This law is valid despite Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation. [Meat Law, s. 5](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בשר_ומוצריו). Because it passed within a year of the Basic Law, the four-year lapse does not apply to it (s. 8(ב)).

Freedom of Occupation's own preservation clause (s. 10) kept older laws in force only until March 2002; after that, pre-1992 laws restricting occupation are open to review. Human Dignity and Liberty's s. 10 has no end date, but courts read old laws "in the spirit" of the Basic Law.

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 73/53 חברת "קול העם" בע״מ נ׳ שר הפנים (1953)

The Minister of the Interior suspended two communist newspapers under the Mandatory Press Ordinance for articles he thought endangered public peace. The Court, with no written bill of rights, read the Ordinance in light of freedom of expression drawn from the Declaration of Independence and Israel's democratic character, and held that the minister could suspend a paper only if publication created a "near certainty" (ודאות קרובה) of real danger. The suspensions were cancelled.

Why it matters: it is the founding case of Israel's judge-made bill of rights. It shows what the court could do before 1992: control the government through interpretation, but not strike down a statute. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/228)

### ע״א 6821/93 בנק המזרחי המאוחד בע״מ נ׳ מגדל כפר שיתופי, פ״ד מט(4) 221 (1995)

A 1993 amendment to a law rescuing indebted agricultural cooperatives (the "Gal Law") let a statutory rehabilitator write off debts owed to banks and guarantors. Creditors argued it took their property contrary to s. 3 of Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty. The Court held:

1. **Supremacy.** The 1992 Basic Laws on rights are constitutional norms superior to ordinary laws.
2. **Review.** An ordinary law that infringes a protected right without meeting the limitation clause may be declared void by the courts. President Barak drew on *Marbury v. Madison* and the common-law tradition that a court deciding a case must decide which norm prevails.
3. **On the facts.** The amendment did infringe property, but met the limitation clause (proper purpose, proportionate), so it was upheld.

The justices agreed that the amendment stood; most also agreed on supremacy and review, but not on the theory behind it (lesson 06.1). Justice Cheshin denied that the Knesset has constituent power, accepted only procedural self-limitation (such as a 61-member requirement), and would have reserved any power to void a Knesset law to the High Court of Justice alone. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/391)

### בג״ץ 6055/95 צמח נ׳ שר הביטחון (1999)

Military law allowed a soldier to be held for up to 96 hours before being brought before a judge. An expanded panel held that this infringed personal liberty (s. 5 of Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty) more than necessary, since civilian law required 48 hours, and declared the provision void, suspending the declaration for six months so the Knesset could legislate.

Why it matters: it shows two tools now standard in Israeli review: striking down a specific provision rather than a whole law, and suspending the declaration to give the Knesset time. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/390)

### בג״ץ 6298/07 רסלר נ׳ הכנסת (2012)

The Tal Law (2002) let yeshiva students defer military service. The Court held that it disproportionately infringed equality, which is part of human dignity, because in practice it had not achieved its own stated aims of gradual integration, and ruled that it could not be extended beyond August 2012.

Why it matters: an example of review based on how a law worked in practice, not only on its text, and of a long-running dialogue with the Knesset on the same subject (still unresolved in 2026, see lesson 06.4). [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/311)

## How it works in practice

### Is this law open to constitutional review? A four-question check

1. **Which right is affected?** It must be a right in Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty (life, body, dignity, property, liberty, leaving and entering Israel, privacy) or in Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, or a right the courts derive from dignity (equality, expression, family life, minimum subsistence; lesson 06.4).
2. **When was the provision enacted?** A provision in force before March 1992 is protected by s. 10 of Human Dignity and Liberty (it can only be interpreted narrowly). An amendment after that date is reviewable.
3. **Is there an override?** Only for Freedom of Occupation, only with 61 votes and an express statement.
4. **Is the infringement justified?** The limitation clause (lesson 06.3).

### Who decides, and where

President Barak, following the common-law tradition, accepted that any court deciding a case may hold that a provision cannot be applied because it is unconstitutional; Justice Cheshin would have reserved this to the High Court of Justice. In practice, almost all declarations of invalidity come from the Supreme Court sitting as the High Court of Justice, usually on a petition against the Knesset. The Court often prefers narrower routes: interpreting the law to fit the Basic Law, striking only one provision, or suspending the declaration.

### The strongest argument on each side

**For judicial review.** The Knesset itself adopted Basic Laws that say rights may be infringed only under the limitation clause; a limit no one can enforce is not a limit. Majorities sometimes harm minorities, suspects, migrants or unpopular groups who cannot win in the legislature. Israel has unusually weak structural checks (one house, no federalism, a government that controls the Knesset), so the courts are the main check. Most democracies have some form of review.

**Against (or for a narrower version).** The rights Basic Laws were passed by less than half of the Knesset's members, and their supporters did not present them as a transfer of power to the courts. Words like "proper purpose" and "proportionate" call for value judgments that elected representatives should make. Review in Israel is unusually broad because of relaxed standing and justiciability (lesson 06.6). Critics propose an override clause for all rights, a special majority of justices to strike down a law, or review only by a full panel. None of these has been enacted as of October 2026.

## Worked example

Rania Haddad, a pharmacist from Nazareth, reads that a new 2026 law limits pharmacies' opening hours on weekends in some cities. She asks: can this law be challenged?

1. **Right affected.** Freedom of occupation (s. 3 of Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation) — her ability to run her business; possibly equality, if the rule distinguishes between pharmacies by owner identity.
2. **Date.** Enacted 2026, so reviewable; the Freedom of Occupation preservation clause expired in 2002 anyway.
3. **Override.** She checks the law's final sections for "על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד: חופש העיסוק". None.
4. **Limitation clause.** The question becomes whether the law has a proper purpose and is proportionate (lesson 06.3). If the stated purpose is rest for workers or public order, it is likely proper; the fight will be over proportionality.
5. **Practical route.** A constitutional challenge is a High Court of Justice petition against the Knesset; usually a professional association brings it. Rania's realistic first step is to join or contact one, and to check whether the regulator (not the law itself) caused her specific problem, which would be an easier target (lesson 06.8).

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Partly. Anyone may raise a constitutional argument in their own case (for example, as a defence to a fine or in a claim against the state), and anyone may petition the High Court of Justice in their own name. A petition to strike down a law is complex and rarely succeeds without expert drafting.

### Where and how

- In your own case in any court, you may argue that a provision applied to you is unconstitutional; when the validity of a law is challenged, the court will usually direct that the Attorney General be notified so the State can take a position.
- A direct challenge to a law is a High Court of Justice petition (lesson 06.8); fees via [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).
- Public-interest organisations and university clinics bring most constitutional petitions; joining as an affected person is often more effective than filing alone.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your argument is that a law is void, and your livelihood or liberty depends on the outcome.
- The State Attorney's office has filed a response arguing the issue in depth.
- The court asks for a constitutional brief or for notice to the Attorney General.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Challenging a pre-1992 law under Human Dignity and Liberty | Dismissed: s. 10 preserves it | Argue interpretation in the spirit of the Basic Law, or attack a later amendment | Check the enactment date of the exact provision |
| Ignoring an override clause | Wasted challenge | Check the override's 61 votes and four-year lapse | Read the final sections of the law |
| Claiming a right the Basic Laws do not list without explaining its source | The court sees no constitutional right | Show how the courts derived it from dignity (lesson 06.4) | Use the rights table in lesson 06.4 |
| Asking only for the whole law to be voided | Court prefers narrower remedies; petition seen as overreaching | Ask for a specific provision, or for interpretation | Plan alternative remedies |
| Calling the opposing view illegitimate | Weak, partisan brief | Address the strongest counter-argument | Write both paragraphs in step 5 |

## Review

- Before 1992 the Court protected rights by interpretation (Kol Ha'am) but could not strike down statutes, except for breach of formal entrenchment (Bergman).
- The 1992 and 1994 Basic Laws added rights and limitation clauses; Bank Mizrahi (1995) held them supreme and ordinary laws reviewable.
- Laws in force before March 1992 are preserved under Human Dignity and Liberty s. 10; the Freedom of Occupation override (s. 8) has been used once, for meat imports.
- Review is usually targeted: a single provision, often with a suspended declaration (Tzemach), sometimes based on how the law works in practice (Ressler).
- Supporters and critics of review both have serious arguments; proposals to restrict review have not been enacted as of October 2026.
