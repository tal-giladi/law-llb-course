# 06.6 · Judicial review, its limits and the 2023–2026 dispute

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why Israeli judicial review became unusually broad, what each part of the 2023–2026 dispute over the judiciary changed or tried to change, where each part stands legally as of October 2026, and the strongest legal argument on each side. The lesson takes no position on who is right. It gives you the tools to read the next ruling, or the next Knesset bill, and understand what is at stake.

## Why it matters

Between 2023 and 2026 Israel had its deepest constitutional conflict since independence. The Government announced a reform of the judiciary in January 2023; mass protests and counter-protests followed; one amendment to a Basic Law was passed in July 2023 and struck down in January 2024; another was passed in March 2025 and is being challenged; ministers and the Court clashed over appointments, dismissals and compliance with rulings. Elections are on 27 October 2026, and the incoming Knesset will be the first to which the judicial-selection amendment applies.

Behind the politics are legal questions this module has prepared you for: who has the last word on the constitution (06.1), how far courts may review laws (06.2–06.3) and executive decisions (this lesson), and how judges are chosen. Reasonable lawyers disagree on all of them.

## The law

### The High Court of Justice's jurisdiction

> (ג) בית המשפט העליון ישב גם כבית משפט גבוה לצדק; בשבתו כאמור ידון בענינים אשר הוא רואה צורך לתת בהם סעד למען הצדק ואשר אינם בסמכותו של בית משפט או של בית דין אחר.

*Gist:* The Supreme Court also sits as the High Court of Justice and hears matters in which it sees a need to grant relief for the sake of justice and which are not within another court's jurisdiction. [Basic Law: The Judiciary, s. 15(ג)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_15)

### The reasonableness amendment (2023), struck down

> על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד זה, מי שבידו סמכות שפיטה על פי דין, לרבות בית המשפט העליון בשבתו כבית משפט גבוה לצדק, לא ידון בעניין סבירות ההחלטה של הממשלה, של ראש הממשלה או של שר אחר, ולא ייתן צו בעניין כאמור; בסעיף זה, "החלטה" – כל החלטה, לרבות בענייני מינויים או החלטה להימנע מהפעלת כל סמכות.

*Gist:* No court would hear or rule on the reasonableness of any decision of the Government, the Prime Minister or a minister, including appointments and decisions not to act. Added as s. 15(ד1) by Amendment No. 3 (July 2023); declared void in HCJ 5658/23 on 1 January 2024. [s. 15(ד1), shown struck through](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_15)

### The judicial-selection amendment (2025), not yet in force

Section 4 currently provides a nine-member committee: the President of the Supreme Court and two other justices, the Minister of Justice and another minister, two Knesset members, and two representatives of the Bar Association. Amendment No. 4 (passed 27 March 2025) replaces it from the start of the 26th Knesset:

> (ג) שני חברי הכנסת שייבחרו בכנסת, אחד מסיעות הקואליציה [...] ואחד מסיעות האופוזיציה [...];
> (ד) שני נציגי ציבור שייבחרו בכנסת, באחד יבחרו חברי הכנסת מסיעות הקואליציה ובאחד – חברי הכנסת מסיעות האופוזיציה [...].

*Gist:* The two Bar representatives are replaced by two public representatives chosen by coalition and opposition MKs respectively; the two MKs are also split, one coalition, one opposition. [s. 4(ב)(1), as amended](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_4)

> החלטה של הוועדה תתקבל ברוב של חמישה חברים ובהם אחד מהמנויים בכל אחת מפסקאות (1) עד (3) [...]: (1) שופט; (2) שר, חבר כנסת מקרב סיעות הקואליציה או נציג הציבור שבחרו חברי הכנסת מסיעות הקואליציה; (3) חבר כנסת מקרב סיעות האופוזיציה או נציג הציבור שבחרו חברי הכנסת מסיעות האופוזיציה;

*Gist:* Decisions need five votes including at least one judge, one coalition-side member and one opposition-side member; for the Supreme Court, the five must include one coalition-side and one opposition-side member (s. 4(ה)). [s. 4(ד)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_4)

New s. 4א adds a deadlock mechanism for the Supreme Court: if two seats have been vacant for a year and no one was chosen, the Minister of Justice may, once per Knesset, trigger a procedure in which the coalition side and the opposition side each propose three candidates and the rest of the committee picks one of each. New s. 4ב bars appointments from 90 days before an election until the new members are chosen. [Amendment No. 4, official gazette](https://fs.knesset.gov.il/25/law/25_lsr_6165425.pdf)

### The incapacity amendment (2023), deferred

Amendment No. 12 to Basic Law: The Government (March 2023) provides that the Prime Minister may be declared incapacitated only for physical or mental inability, and only by the Prime Minister's own notice or a three-quarters vote of the Government, each confirmed by a two-thirds vote of the Knesset House Committee. [s. 16(ב1)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הממשלה#סעיף_16)

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 910/86 רסלר נ׳ שר הביטחון (1988)

Reservists challenged the Defence Minister's long-standing deferral of military service for yeshiva students. The Court (Barak) held that a public petitioner may have standing where the issue concerns the rule of law, and that the question was justiciable because it could be decided by legal norms, even though it was politically sensitive. On the merits, the deferrals were then held to be within the Minister's discretion.

Why it matters: it opened the High Court of Justice to public petitioners and narrowed non-justiciability. Supporters say it made the rule of law enforceable against the powerful; critics say it made the Court a forum for every public controversy. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/312)

### בג״ץ 3267/97 רובינשטיין נ׳ שר הביטחון (1998)

Ten years later the number of deferrals had grown. The Court held that a decision of this scale, dividing society, is a "primary arrangement" that only the Knesset may make by statute; the minister's general power was no longer enough ("quantity becomes quality"). It suspended its ruling for twelve months; the Knesset responded with the Tal Law (lesson 06.2).

Why it matters: the primary-arrangements doctrine is a separation-of-powers rule that sends decisions back to the Knesset rather than deciding them for it; both critics and supporters of the Court cite it with approval. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/323)

### בג״ץ 5658/23 התנועה למען איכות השלטון בישראל נ׳ הכנסת (1.1.2024)

All fifteen justices sat. Twelve held the Court may review Basic Laws in extreme cases (lesson 06.1). Eight held that the reasonableness amendment was such a case and declared it void: it barred every court from considering reasonableness for all decisions of the Government and ministers, including extreme unreasonableness, and so caused unprecedented harm to the separation of powers and the rule of law. Seven did not void it: Justices Wilner, Stein and Canfy-Steinitz would have read it narrowly, as removing only the "balancing" reasonableness developed since Dapei Zahav (1980) and leaving review of extreme, arbitrary decisions; Justice Kasher held that it weakened review but did not deny democracy's core; Justices Sohlberg, Mintz and Elron held that the Court had no power to review it and that the challenge was premature.

Why it matters: the first time an Israeli court voided a provision of a Basic Law. Reasonableness review of ministers therefore continues as before. [Official abstract](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/movement-quality-government-v-knesset); [Hayut opinion](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/273)

### בג״ץ 2412/23 התנועה למען איכות השלטון בישראל נ׳ הכנסת (January 2024)

The petitioners argued that the incapacity amendment was personal legislation, passed to protect the sitting Prime Minister from being declared incapacitated because of a conflict of interest arising from his criminal trial. The respondents argued that the amendment filled a gap by defining incapacity and who may declare it, that it binds every future Prime Minister, and that the Court has no power to review a Basic Law. By 6–5, the Court held that the amendment was an abuse of constituent power because of its personal character and immediate application, and that the remedy was to defer its application to the next Knesset rather than void it. The minority would not have intervened. Justices disagreed about whether deferral is a form of invalidation.

Why it matters: it applies the "abuse" doctrine (form, generality) and shows a remedy short of striking down. The amendment will apply from the 26th Knesset. [Basic Law: The Government on Wikisource, which records the ruling against s. 16](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הממשלה#סעיף_16). Fallback: search 2412/23 at https://supremedecisions.court.gov.il.

## How it works in practice

### Status of each element, October 2026

| Element | What it does or did | Legal status |
|---|---|---|
| Reasonableness amendment (Basic Law: The Judiciary, amendment 3, July 2023) | Barred review of ministerial reasonableness | Void (HCJ 5658/23, 1.1.2024, 8–7) |
| Incapacity amendment (Basic Law: The Government, amendment 12, March 2023) | Limits declaring a PM incapacitated | Valid; applies from the 26th Knesset (HCJ 2412/23, January 2024, 6–5) |
| Judicial selection (Basic Law: The Judiciary, amendment 4, March 2025) | New committee composition and voting rules | Takes effect when the 26th Knesset begins; petitions heard by eleven justices on 21 June 2026, supplementary briefs July 2026; no ruling found as of 17 October 2026 |
| Election of the Supreme Court President | Minister of Justice delayed convening the committee | Court ordered a vote; Justice Isaac Amit elected 26 January 2025; the Minister refused to recognise the appointment (as last reported) |
| Dismissal of the Attorney General | Government changed the procedure (June 2025) and dismissed her (August 2025) | Both annulled by a seven-justice panel, 14 December 2025; she remains in office |
| Dismissal of the Shin Bet head | Government dismissed him (March 2025) | Held unlawful (May 2025): no hearing, advisory committee bypassed, conflict of interest; no operative order because he resigned |
| Basic Law: Torah Study (July 2026); arrests law for draft evaders | Value clause; limits on enforcement | Petitions pending; arrests law frozen by interim order |
| Splitting the Attorney General's office | Separate prosecutor and adviser | Passed first reading June 2026; not enacted |

Items not covered by a published judgment that you have read are based on news reports; check the Judiciary's site before relying on them.

### The strongest legal argument on each side

**Reasonableness.** *For limiting it:* "reasonableness balancing" lets judges weigh the same considerations a minister weighed and choose a different balance, without a clear legal standard; elected ministers answer to the Knesset and voters, judges do not; other common-law systems use a narrower test. *For keeping it:* in Israel, with one chamber and a Government that controls it, reasonableness is often the only effective check on appointments, dismissals and failure to act; the amendment removed even review of extreme unreasonableness; the other grounds (authority, extraneous considerations, proportionality) do not cover all cases.

**Judicial selection.** *For the amendment:* judges are appointed by elected representatives in most democracies; the old committee gave judges and the Bar a majority and an effective veto, so the Court largely chose its own members; the new rules require consent from both coalition and opposition and keep three justices on the committee. *Against:* the change removes the professional Bar members and gives politicians or their appointees six of nine seats, with a deadlock route that weakens the judges' say; Israel lacks other checks (a constitution, a second chamber, federalism), so politicised appointments threaten judicial independence; the Attorney General supports the petitioners. *On review itself:* the Knesset argues the petition is premature and that preferring a different system is no reason to void a Basic Law; the petitioners argue this is the kind of extreme case 5658/23 described.

**Gatekeepers.** *For more government control:* the Attorney General's opinions bind the Government in practice, so an official no one elected can block policy; ministers should be able to choose their legal advisers and dismiss senior officials they do not trust. *Against:* independent legal advice and prosecution are what prevent the Government from acting unlawfully, especially where the Prime Minister is on trial; dismissals must follow proper procedure and avoid conflicts of interest.

## Worked example

Michal Peretz, a law student in Haifa, has to predict how the Court might decide the judicial-selection petitions. She lists the doctrines:

1. **Ripeness.** The amendment applies only from the 26th Knesset. The Knesset argues the petition is premature; the Court heard it anyway, which suggests it sees the question as ready, but a ruling could still say "wait".
2. **Abuse of constituent power (form).** Is the amendment stable, general, fitting the constitutional fabric? It is permanent and applies to all future Knessets, unlike the incapacity amendment, so this ground looks weaker than in 2412/23.
3. **Deviation from constituent authority (content).** Does it deny the democratic core (judicial independence)? This is the 5658/23 test; it requires "extreme" harm. The answer depends on how much weight the Court gives to the deadlock mechanism and the loss of the Bar seats.
4. **Remedies.** The Court could void parts (for example s. 4א), read them narrowly, or uphold the amendment entirely. In 2412/23 it deferred application; here application is already deferred.
5. **Her conclusion** is a range, not a prediction: the outcome turns on step 3, where in 5658/23 the Court split 8–7. She writes both the majority and minority opinion she expects, each at its strongest.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The current text of s. 4 of Basic Law: The Judiciary on Wikisource.
- The [official abstract of 5658/23](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/movement-quality-government-v-knesset).
- A news search for the latest on the judicial-selection petitions.
- 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Draw the current committee and the committee from the 26th Knesset as two diagrams: who sits, who chooses them, what majority is needed for lower courts and for the Supreme Court.
2. For one Supreme Court vacancy, simulate a vote under each version: which members could block an appointment alone?
3. Check today's status of the petitions (pending or decided, and the date).
4. Write a one-page "argument map": the petitioners' three strongest arguments, the Knesset's three strongest arguments, and the doctrine each relies on (ripeness, abuse, deviation, remedy).
5. Mark which arguments are legal and which are policy. Remove any sentence that states your political view.
6. Ask someone who disagrees with you to read it and tell you if their side is fairly stated.

### Template

None. Use the argument map in step 4.

### How you know it worked

- Your diagrams show the voting rules correctly (five votes including the required members).
- You can say which members can block a Supreme Court appointment under each version.
- A reader cannot tell your political view.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Constitutional challenges to Basic Laws are brought by organisations and Knesset factions with expert lawyers. What you can do yourself is follow and use the doctrines: reasonableness, proportionality and proper procedure are the grounds you will rely on in any petition about a decision in your own matter (lesson 06.8).

### Where and how

- Follow cases on the Judiciary's decision search by case number (for example 5658/23) and on the Cardozo Israeli Supreme Court Project for English translations.
- Read Knesset bills and committee protocols on the Knesset website (module 7).
- You may submit written comments to Knesset committees on bills in your own name (module 7).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- You are directly affected by an appointment, dismissal or decision covered by these disputes and want to join a petition.
- You are asked to sign an affidavit for a petition; you must be able to stand behind every fact in it.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saying "the Court cancelled reasonableness review" or "the Court cancelled a Basic Law" loosely | Inaccurate: it voided one amendment, so review continues | State exactly what was voided and by what vote | Use the status table |
| Treating the judicial-selection amendment as in force now | Wrong analysis of current appointments | It applies from the 26th Knesset | Check the "החל מיום" note in s. 4 |
| Presenting one side's arguments as law and the other's as politics | Partisan, unreliable analysis | Separate legal from policy arguments for both sides | Do step 5 |
| Relying on news for a ruling's content | Errors in holdings and votes | Read the official abstract or the ruling | Cite the ruling, not the headline |
| Ignoring the minority opinions | You miss what may become the law after new appointments | Note each minority view | Read the abstract's list of opinions |

## Review

- Israeli review is broad because of relaxed standing and justiciability (Ressler, 1988) and reasonableness balancing (Dapei Zahav, 1980); the primary-arrangements doctrine (Rubinstein, 1998) sends basic choices to the Knesset.
- The 2023 reasonableness amendment was voided 8–7 on 1 January 2024; twelve of fifteen justices held Basic Laws are reviewable in extreme cases.
- The 2023 incapacity amendment was held an abuse of constituent power (6–5) and deferred to the next Knesset.
- The 2025 judicial-selection amendment changes the committee from the 26th Knesset; petitions were pending before eleven justices as of October 2026.
- Each element has serious legal arguments on both sides; know the status, the vote and the minority view.
