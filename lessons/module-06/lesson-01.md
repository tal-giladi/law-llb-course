# 06.1 · A constitution built from Basic Laws: the constituent-authority debate

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why Israel has a set of Basic Laws rather than one constitutional document, set out the three competing legal theories about the Knesset's power to make them, and say what the Supreme Court currently holds about the limits of that power. Every later lesson in this module, from judicial review to the 2023–2026 dispute, depends on this debate, so the lesson presents each position at its strongest.

## Why it matters

Most democracies have a constitution adopted in a special procedure (a constituent assembly, a referendum, a supermajority) and changed only by a harder procedure. Israel does not. Its constitutional law is built from Basic Laws (חוקי יסוד), passed by the Knesset one at a time since 1958, mostly by ordinary majorities, with no fixed rule about how a Basic Law is made or changed.

That design leaves two questions open, and Israeli constitutional law has argued about both for decades:

1. **Are Basic Laws legally superior to ordinary laws?** If they are, a court may set aside an ordinary law that contradicts one. Since Bank Mizrahi (1995) the answer has been yes (lesson 06.2), but the reasoning behind it is still disputed.
2. **Is there anything the Knesset cannot do by calling a law a "Basic Law"?** If the Knesset is a constituent authority, are there limits on that authority, and who enforces them? Since 2021 the Supreme Court has said there are limits, and in January 2024 it enforced them for the first time (lesson 06.6). Others argue that no court has the power to review the constitution itself.

For a self-represented person this is not abstract. Whether a law that harms you can be challenged at all, and on what grounds, depends on where it sits in this hierarchy.

## The law

### The starting point: no constitution, but a promise of one

The Declaration of the Establishment of the State (14 May 1948) called for a constitution to be adopted by an elected Constituent Assembly by 1 October 1948. The war delayed elections; the Constituent Assembly elected in January 1949 turned itself into the First Knesset (Transition Law 1949) and did not adopt a constitution. On 13 June 1950 the Knesset adopted the **Harari Decision**: the constitution would be built chapter by chapter, each chapter a Basic Law, to be joined together when complete. The decision did not say what majority a Basic Law needs, whether Basic Laws outrank ordinary laws, or when the work would end. (The date and wording are summarised in the 5658/23 judgment; [official abstract](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/movement-quality-government-v-knesset).)

As of October 2026 there are fourteen Basic Laws: the Knesset (1958), Israel Lands (1960), the President (1964), the Government (1968, re-enacted 1992 and 2001), the State Economy (1975), the Military (1976), Jerusalem (1980), the Judiciary (1984), the State Comptroller (1988), Human Dignity and Liberty (1992), Freedom of Occupation (1994), Referendum (2014), Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People (2018) and Torah Study (2026). There is still no Basic Law: Legislation that would fix how Basic Laws are made and amended.

### Entrenchment: what the Basic Laws say about themselves

> הכנסת תיבחר בבחירות כלליות, ארציות, ישירות, שוות, חשאיות ויחסיות, לפי חוק הבחירות לכנסת; אין לשנות סעיף זה, אלא ברוב של חברי הכנסת.

*Gist:* Knesset elections are general, national, direct, equal, secret and proportional, and this section may be changed only by a majority of all Knesset members (61). [Basic Law: The Knesset, s. 4](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הכנסת#סעיף_4)

> הרוב הדרוש לפי חוק זה לשינוי הסעיפים 4, 9א, 34, 44 או 45 יהא דרוש להחלטות מליאת הכנסת בקריאה הראשונה, בקריאה השניה ובקריאה השלישית. לענין סעיף זה "שינוי" – בין מפורש ובין משתמע.

*Gist:* The special majority is needed in all three readings, and "change" includes an implied change. [s. 46](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הכנסת#סעיף_46)

> אין לשנות חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק־יסוד שנתקבל ברוב של חברי הכנסת.

*Gist:* Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation may be changed only by a Basic Law passed by 61 members. [Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, s. 7](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_חופש_העיסוק#סעיף_7)

Note what is missing. Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty has no entrenchment clause at all; most institutional Basic Laws have only a few entrenched sections; and no Basic Law requires a special majority to *create* a new Basic Law. The most recent one shows how light the procedure is:

> לימוד התורה הוא ערך יסוד במורשת העם היהודי ובמדינת ישראל.

*Gist:* Torah study is a foundational value in the heritage of the Jewish people and the State of Israel. Passed 63–52 on 13 July 2026, a single section. [Basic Law: Torah Study](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_לימוד_תורה)

### Supremacy in substance: the limitation clause

> אין פוגעים בזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק ההולם את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל, שנועד לתכלית ראויה, ובמידה שאינה עולה על הנדרש או לפי חוק כאמור מכוח הסמכה מפורשת בו.

*Gist:* The rights in the Basic Law may be infringed only by a law that befits the values of the State, for a proper purpose, and to an extent no greater than required. [Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, s. 8](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_8)

This clause is the textual basis for review of ordinary laws (lessons 06.2–06.3). It says nothing about who decides whether a law meets it, or about Basic Laws that amend other Basic Laws.

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 98/69 ברגמן נ׳ שר האוצר (1969)

A 1969 law funded election campaigns only for parties already in the outgoing Knesset. It was passed without the 61-member majority that s. 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset requires for changes to the equality of elections. The Court held that the law infringed equality and, since it lacked the required majority, could not stand unless the Knesset re-enacted it by 61 votes or removed the inequality. The Court expressly left open whether the entrenchment itself was valid, because the State did not contest it.

Why it matters: the first time an Israeli court refused to apply a Knesset law because of a Basic Law. It showed that formal entrenchment works, decades before the "constitutional revolution". [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/92)

### ע״א 6821/93 בנק המזרחי המאוחד בע״מ נ׳ מגדל כפר שיתופי, פ״ד מט(4) 221 (1995)

A nine-justice panel held that the Basic Laws on human rights of 1992 and 1994 are supreme over ordinary legislation, and that a court may declare void an ordinary law that infringes them without meeting the limitation clause. The justices gave three different accounts of where the Knesset's power comes from:

- **Constituent authority (President Barak, majority).** The power to make a constitution passed from the Constituent Assembly to every later Knesset. The Knesset wears "two hats": legislative and constituent. When it enacts a Basic Law it acts as a constituent authority, and its Basic Laws bind it when it legislates. The best reading of Israel's legal history (the "rule of recognition") supports this.
- **Unlimited sovereignty (former President Shamgar).** The Knesset is sovereign and may enact any law, including one that limits its own future legislation. It can bind itself because it is all-powerful, not because it inherited a separate constituent power.
- **No constituent power (Justice Cheshin, dissenting on this point).** The First Knesset's constituent power was not transferable and was not transferred. The Knesset is a legislature only. It can entrench specific provisions, but nothing in the legislative history shows the members believed they were adopting a constitution when they passed the Basic Laws.

All three agreed on the result in the case (the challenged law was upheld). They disagreed on the source of the Knesset's power, and that disagreement shapes every later question. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/391)

### בג״ץ 4908/10 בר־און נ׳ הכנסת (2011)

The Knesset twice used a temporary Basic Law to pass a two-year state budget, departing from the one-year rule in Basic Law: The State Economy. The petitioners argued that a temporary Basic Law tailored to one government's needs is not really constitutional. The Court dismissed the petition, but warned that using Basic Laws for ordinary political purposes could amount to an abuse of constituent power and that it might intervene if the practice continued.

Why it matters: the first statement of the "abuse of constituent power" idea. It treats the question "is this really a Basic Law?" as a matter of form and process, not content. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/80)

### בג״ץ 5658/23 התנועה למען איכות השלטון בישראל נ׳ הכנסת (1.1.2024): the constituent-power question

Fifteen justices sat. On the question for this lesson, twelve held that the Knesset as a constituent authority is not all-powerful (relying on Hasson v. Knesset, 2021, on the Nation-State Basic Law): it may not deny the core character of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, and the Court may review a Basic Law in extreme cases. Two of the twelve also pointed to the Declaration of Independence or s. 15(ג) of Basic Law: The Judiciary as sources. The Court summarised the "abuse" tests developed in Shafir v. Knesset (2021): stability, generality, compatibility with the constitutional fabric, and distinctiveness. Justices Sohlberg and Mintz held that the Court has no legal source for reviewing Basic Laws at all; Justice Elron called the doctrine a role reversal and left open at most a narrow exception for extreme harm to individual rights. The outcome for the specific amendment is lesson 06.6.

Why it matters: it is the current law on the limits of constituent power, and the closest vote on the court's own authority in Israeli history. [Official abstract](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/movement-quality-government-v-knesset); [majority opinion, English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/273)

## How it works in practice

### Two kinds of limit, two kinds of review

| Limit | What is checked | Example | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entrenchment (formal) | Did a law that changes an entrenched section get the required majority? | Bergman (1969) | Settled |
| Limitation clause (substantive, ordinary laws) | Does an ordinary law infringe a protected right without meeting s. 8 / s. 4? | Bank Mizrahi (1995) onward | Settled; scope debated |
| Abuse of constituent power (form) | Is this norm really a Basic Law: stable, general, fitting the constitutional fabric? | Bar-On (2011), Shafir (2021) | Accepted by a majority; disputed |
| Deviation from constituent authority (content) | Does a Basic Law deny the core of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state? | Hasson (2021), 5658/23 (2024) | Accepted by 12 of 15 in 2024; strongly disputed |

### The strongest argument on each side

**For limits enforced by the court.** Israel has no special procedure for constitution-making, the coalition controls the Knesset, and the Knesset can amend a Basic Law overnight by a simple majority. Without some limit, a temporary majority could rewrite the rules of the game, including elections or the courts, to entrench itself. Many democracies recognise "unamendable" principles (for example Germany's eternity clause, India's basic-structure doctrine). If there are limits, someone must apply them, and the court is the institution that interprets the law.

**Against.** No text gives the court power to review Basic Laws, and in a system with no written constitution the court would be creating its own authority over the constitution. The limits ("the core of a Jewish and democratic state") are vague and are defined by the same judges who apply them, including in cases about the courts' own powers. The remedy for a bad Basic Law is political: elections, public pressure, or a proper constitution adopted by a broad process. Some who hold this view would accept a narrow exception for extreme violations of individual rights, while others reject any exception.

Both sides agree on one thing: the absence of a Basic Law: Legislation, setting how Basic Laws are adopted and changed, is the root of the problem. Several drafts have been proposed over the decades; none has passed.

## Worked example

Yonatan Amsalem, a civics teacher in Dimona, wants to explain to his students whether a hypothetical "Basic Law: Elections (Temporary Provision)" postponing the next election by two years, passed 62–58, would be valid.

1. **Entrenchment.** Section 8 of Basic Law: The Knesset sets a four-year term. Section 9א(א) allows the Knesset to extend its term only by a law passed by 80 members and only if special circumstances prevent elections on time. Sections 45 and 45א require 80 members to change s. 9א(א), and s. 46 requires that majority in every reading, for express or implied changes. A 62-vote "temporary" Basic Law meets neither requirement.
2. **Abuse of constituent power.** Even apart from entrenchment, the norm is temporary, tailored to the sitting Knesset and takes effect immediately: it would likely fail the stability and generality tests in Shafir.
3. **Deviation from constituent authority.** Postponing elections without special circumstances strikes at the democratic core; Justice Kabub in 5658/23 used exactly this example of something the Knesset cannot do even by 120 votes.
4. **The opposing view.** Justices Sohlberg and Mintz would say the court has no power to review Basic Laws; but even on their view, a law that fails a written entrenchment requirement is defective, as in Bergman.
5. **Conclusion for the class.** On the current case law, very likely invalid on several independent grounds; on the minority view, invalid only if it breaches an express entrenchment.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The theory decides what you can argue in a petition: an ordinary law can be challenged under the limitation clause; a Basic Law can be challenged only on the narrow grounds above, and only in the High Court of Justice.

### Where and how

- Read Basic Laws for free on Wikisource and the Knesset legislation database; check the "תיקון" notes for the latest amendments.
- When a new Basic Law or amendment is passed, the Knesset's explanatory notes (דברי הסבר) in the bill state its purpose; you will use them in any argument about generality or purpose.
- Lesson 06.8 shows how a self-represented person files a petition in the High Court of Justice.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- You want to challenge a Basic Law or an amendment to one. These cases are heard by large panels, take years, and turn on fine doctrinal points; public-interest organisations usually bring them.
- A Basic Law issue is part of a case in which you have personal stakes (your job, your status, your property).

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treating every Basic Law as "the constitution" with full supremacy | You argue that a law is void when the Basic Law has no entrenchment or limitation clause | Check the specific Basic Law's text for entrenchment and limitation clauses | Build the table in this lesson and keep it |
| Citing Bank Mizrahi for one theory as if it were unanimous | Misstating the holding | Say the result was agreed; the theories differed | Count opinions on each issue separately |
| Confusing "abuse" with "deviation" | Weak argument; the court applies different tests | Use Shafir's form tests for abuse; Hasson's core test for deviation | Use the four-row table |
| Presenting one side of the debate as the only legal view | A judge or reader dismisses your analysis as partisan | Add the opposing argument at its strongest | Write both paragraphs before deciding |
| Relying on an old list of Basic Laws | Missing a new Basic Law (for example Torah Study, 2026) | Check Wikisource or the Knesset database for the current list | Note the check date in your notes |

## Review

- Israel's constitution is being built from fourteen Basic Laws under the 1950 Harari Decision; there is still no Basic Law: Legislation fixing how they are made.
- Some sections are formally entrenched (Bergman, 1969); the rights Basic Laws carry limitation clauses, and Bank Mizrahi (1995) held them supreme over ordinary laws.
- Bank Mizrahi offered three theories of the Knesset's power: constituent authority (Barak), unlimited sovereignty (Shamgar), no constituent power (Cheshin).
- Since Bar-On, Shafir and Hasson, the Court recognises limits on constituent power; in 5658/23 (2024) twelve of fifteen justices held it may review Basic Laws in extreme cases. Three justices rejected such review.
- Each side has a serious legal argument; the current law is the majority view.
