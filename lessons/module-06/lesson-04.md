# 06.4 · Rights: dignity, liberty, equality, expression, religion, property, occupation

By the end of this lesson you will be able to say, for any public decision that affects you, which constitutional right it engages, where that right comes from (an express section or a right the courts derived from human dignity), and how the courts have weighed it in leading cases. Israel has no complete bill of rights, so knowing the source of a right is the first step of every constitutional argument.

## Why it matters

The two rights Basic Laws are short. They list life, body and dignity, property, personal liberty, leaving and entering Israel, privacy, and freedom of occupation. They do not mention equality, freedom of expression, freedom of religion, freedom from religion, the right to family life, education or health. Proposals to list these rights expressly failed in 1992, largely because the parties could not agree on equality and religion.

The courts filled part of the gap by reading these rights into "human dignity". This is one of the most debated moves in Israeli law. Supporters say a democracy cannot protect dignity without equality and expression, and that the Knesset chose a broad word. Critics say the Knesset deliberately left these rights out, so reading them in transfers to judges questions that the legislature could not settle. The compromise the Court adopted (the "intermediate model") protects derived rights only where they are closely tied to the core of human dignity, which leaves room for argument in almost every case.

## The law

### The listed rights

> אין פוגעים בחייו, בגופו, או בכבודו של אדם באשר הוא אדם.

*Gist:* No one may harm a person's life, body or dignity as such. [Human Dignity and Liberty, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_2)

> אין פוגעים בקנינו של אדם.

*Gist:* No one may harm a person's property. [s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_3)

> אין נוטלים ואין מגבילים את חירותו של אדם במאסר, במעצר, בהסגרה או בכל דרך אחרת.

*Gist:* Liberty may not be taken or limited by imprisonment, arrest, extradition or otherwise. [s. 5](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_5)

> (א) כל אדם זכאי לפרטיות ולצנעת חייו.

*Gist:* Everyone is entitled to privacy; the section also protects the home, searches and correspondence. [s. 7](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_7)

> כל אזרח או תושב של המדינה זכאי לעסוק בכל עיסוק, מקצוע או משלח יד.

*Gist:* Every citizen or resident may engage in any occupation, profession or trade. [Freedom of Occupation, s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_חופש_העיסוק#סעיף_3)

Section 6 adds the right of every person to leave Israel and of every citizen abroad to enter it. Note the different subjects: most rights belong to "every person" (אדם); freedom of occupation and the right to enter belong to citizens (and, for occupation, residents).

### Who is bound

> כל רשות מרשויות השלטון חייבת לכבד את הזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה.

*Gist:* Every governmental authority must respect these rights. [s. 11](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_11)

The duty falls on the State, ministries, local authorities, the police, the IDF and bodies performing public functions. Private parties are not directly bound, but constitutional values enter private law through open terms such as good faith and public policy (module 3).

### The Nation-State Basic Law (2018)

> (ב) מדינת ישראל היא מדינת הלאום של העם היהודי, שבה הוא מממש את זכותו הטבעית, התרבותית, הדתית וההיסטורית להגדרה עצמית.

*Gist:* Israel is the nation-state of the Jewish people, in which it realises its right to self-determination. [s. 1(ב)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_ישראל_–_מדינת_הלאום_של_העם_היהודי#סעיף_1)

> המדינה רואה בפיתוח התיישבות יהודית ערך לאומי, ותפעל על מנת לעודד ולקדם הקמה וביסוס שלה.

*Gist:* The State sees developing Jewish settlement as a national value and will act to encourage it. [s. 7](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_ישראל_–_מדינת_הלאום_של_העם_היהודי#סעיף_7)

The Basic Law does not mention equality or democracy. How it relates to equality is the subject of Hasson, below.

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 4541/94 מילר נ׳ שר הביטחון (1995)

Alice Miller asked to be tested for the Air Force pilots' course; the army refused all women, citing training costs and planning given women's shorter reserve service and absences for pregnancy. The majority (Mazza, Strasberg-Cohen, Dorner) held that budget and planning considerations could not justify a general exclusion of women; equality required individual consideration. Justices Kedmi and Tal dissented, holding the Court should not interfere with the army's professional considerations.

Why it matters: a foundational equality case, decided partly on administrative grounds, and an early discussion of equality as part of human dignity. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/258)

### בג״ץ 5016/96 חורב נ׳ שר התחבורה (1997)

The Minister of Transportation closed Bar-Ilan Street in Jerusalem to traffic on Sabbaths during prayer hours. Secular residents and drivers argued it infringed freedom of movement; a religious petitioner asked for a full closure. The Court held that protecting the religious lifestyle and feelings of local residents is a legitimate consideration in a democracy, but that the Minister had not properly weighed the interests of secular residents of the street itself, and set the decision aside for reconsideration. Several justices would have held the Minister had no authority to close the road at all.

Why it matters: the model for balancing religion and freedom from religion in public space. It treats both groups' interests as legitimate, and shows the Court sending a decision back rather than deciding the policy itself. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/195)

### בג״ץ 366/03 עמותת מחויבות לשלום וצדק חברתי נ׳ שר האוצר (2005)

Petitioners challenged cuts to income-support benefits as below the minimum needed to live with dignity. The majority (Barak and five others) recognised a constitutional right to minimum dignified existence derived from human dignity, but held the petitioners had not proved that the cuts left recipients below that level; the petitions were dismissed. Justice Levy, dissenting, held the burden had passed to the State, which had not shown it considered the right at all.

Why it matters: it shows how a derived social right works: recognised in principle, hard to prove in practice, with the challenger carrying the first burden. Hassan (lesson 06.3) later applied it to strike down a specific rule. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/113)

### בג״ץ 5555/18 חסון נ׳ הכנסת (8.7.2021)

Fifteen petitions, led by former Druze MK Akram Hasson, argued that the Nation-State Basic Law denied the democratic character of the State and equality. An eleven-justice panel dismissed them 10–1. President Hayut held that the Knesset's constituent power is limited (lesson 06.1), but that this Basic Law did not deny Israel's democratic core: it must be read together with the other Basic Laws, its omission of equality does not cancel equality protected by Human Dignity and Liberty, and s. 7 on Jewish settlement does not permit allocating state land on the basis of religion or nationality. Justice Karra dissented, holding that the law harmed the core of equality and should be voided in part.

Why it matters: it sets the current relationship between the Nation-State Basic Law and equality, and it is where the Court first stated limits on constituent power. Readings of the law remain sharply contested: supporters see a statement of Jewish self-determination that does not reduce individual rights; critics see a hierarchy between citizens. [Library of Congress summary](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2021-07-27/israel-supreme-court-affirms-constitutionality-of-basic-law-israel-nation-state-of-the-jewish-people/). Fallback: search 5555/18 at https://supremedecisions.court.gov.il.

## How it works in practice

### Rights map

| Right | Source | Leading case(s) | Typical public decisions it engages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life, body, dignity | HDL s. 2, 4 | Commitment to Peace; Hassan | Welfare benefits, prison conditions, medical decisions by the State |
| Property | HDL s. 3 | Bank Mizrahi | Expropriation, taxes, debt write-offs, building restrictions |
| Personal liberty | HDL s. 5 | Tzemach; Desta | Arrest, detention, house arrest, travel bans |
| Leaving and entering Israel | HDL s. 6 | — | Exit bans for debtors, passport refusals |
| Privacy | HDL s. 7 | — | Searches, databases, surveillance, data sharing |
| Occupation | FO s. 3 | Bank Mizrahi line; Gavish | Licensing, professional exams, opening hours |
| Equality | Derived from dignity | Miller; Ressler; Hasson | Allocations, benefits, service duties, land |
| Expression | Derived (and pre-1992 case law) | Kol Ha'am | Protest permits, media closures, advertising bans |
| Religion / freedom from religion | Derived (and Declaration) | Horev | Road closures, marriage and burial, Sabbath rules |
| Family life | Derived | Adalah | Family unification, residence status |

### Current disputes (October 2026)

- **Equality in military service.** After the Tal Law expired in 2012, the Court held in June 2024 (בג״ץ 6198/23) that without a statutory exemption the State must enforce the Defence Service Law on yeshiva students and may not fund institutions for students who do not report ([Library of Congress summary](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2024-07-02/israel-high-court-rules-that-state-must-draft-yeshiva-students-to-the-military/)). In July 2026 the Knesset passed Basic Law: Torah Study and a law limiting arrests of draft evaders; the High Court froze the arrests law by interim order and petitions against both were pending as of October 2026. Supporters of the new laws argue that Torah study is a core Jewish value the State may protect and that service arrangements belong to the Knesset; opponents argue that they entrench unequal burden-sharing and are designed to bypass court rulings.
- **Expression and media.** Petitions against several 2024–2026 media measures (a law allowing foreign broadcasters to be closed, and government decisions on Army Radio and advertising) were pending as of October 2026.

## Worked example

Noa Ben-David, a secular resident of a mostly Haredi neighbourhood in Beit Shemesh, finds that the municipality has approved permanent barriers closing her street to cars from Friday afternoon to Saturday night, with no exception for residents. Her neighbour, Rabbi Moshe Weiss, supports the closure and wants it extended to the next street.

1. **Rights.** Noa: freedom of movement and freedom from religion (derived), possibly property (access to her home and parking). Rabbi Weiss: freedom of religion and the residents' religious lifestyle, a legitimate interest under Horev.
2. **Who is bound.** The municipality is a governmental authority (s. 11).
3. **Legality.** Does the traffic power used allow closures for religious reasons? Horev says the consideration is legitimate, but the decision must weigh all residents.
4. **Proportionality.** A closure with access for residents and emergency vehicles, or limited to prayer hours, would likely achieve most of the purpose with less harm.
5. **Route.** Noa writes to the municipality asking for a residents' exemption (lesson 06.8); if refused, a petition to the Administrative Affairs Court. Rabbi Weiss may ask the municipality to extend the closure, and the same balancing applies to his request.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One public decision that affects you or your community (a municipal decision, a benefit rule, a permit refusal, a ministry policy).
- The rights map above and the Basic Laws texts.
- 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Describe the decision in two sentences: who decided, what, when, under what power.
2. List every right it engages, for you and for others affected, including those who benefit from the decision.
3. For each right, write the source: section, or "derived from dignity" plus a case.
4. Mark whether the right is close to the core of dignity (for derived rights, this decides whether it is constitutionally protected).
5. Check s. 11: is the decision-maker a governmental authority or a body with public functions?
6. Write the strongest argument of the other side, in their terms.
7. Save the result; it is step 1 of the proportionality lab (06.7) and the project.

### Template

None. Use the rights map as a table.

### How you know it worked

- Each right on your list has a source you can cite.
- You included the rights and interests of people who support the decision.
- You can say whether the body is bound by the Basic Laws.

### Self-check

- [ ] I can list the rights the Basic Laws state expressly.
- [ ] I can name four derived rights and a case for each.
- [ ] I can explain the intermediate model in one sentence.
- [ ] I can state what Hasson held about the Nation-State Basic Law and equality, and Justice Karra's dissent.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes, as an argument in your own matter: a rights analysis belongs in any letter, objection or petition you write about a public decision that affects you. Writing it for others as an occupation or for pay is reserved to lawyers (Bar Association Law s. 20).

### Where and how

- First, a written request to the decision-maker ([letter to a public authority](../../templates/letter-to-authority.md)), which must answer within the period in [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).
- Complaints about how a public body treated you go to the Ombudsman (lesson 06.5), which is free.
- Discrimination by businesses (not the State) is covered by statutes such as the Prohibition of Discrimination in Products, Services and Entry to Places of Entertainment Law 2000, which you can enforce by a civil claim; the Basic Laws do not bind businesses directly.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- The decision affects your liberty (arrest, detention, travel ban) or your status (residence, citizenship).
- You want to argue that a derived right exists where no case has yet recognised it.
- There is a short statutory deadline for an appeal you might miss.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing "the right to equality in Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty" as an express section | The State points out it is not listed | Cite it as derived from dignity, with Miller or Hasson | Use the rights map |
| Claiming freedom of occupation as a non-resident | Not a holder of the right | Rely on dignity or equality instead | Check who holds the right |
| Suing a private business under the Basic Laws | Wrong legal basis | Use the specific anti-discrimination statute or private-law terms | Check s. 11: who is bound |
| Ignoring the other side's rights | Weak and partisan argument | Address their interest and propose a balance | Do step 6 every time |
| Relying on a news report of a pending case as law | The case may be decided otherwise | Cite only decided rulings as law; mark pending ones | Check the status date |

## Review

- The Basic Laws list life, body, dignity, property, liberty, leaving and entering, privacy and occupation; equality, expression, religion and family life are derived from dignity.
- The intermediate model protects derived rights where they are closely tied to the core of dignity.
- The Basic Laws bind governmental authorities (s. 11); private parties are reached through statutes and private-law terms.
- Leading cases: Miller (equality), Horev (religion and movement), Commitment to Peace (minimum dignified existence), Hasson (Nation-State Basic Law read with equality, 10–1).
- Equality in military service and several media measures are the subject of pending petitions as of October 2026.
