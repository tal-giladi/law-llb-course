# 11.3 · Law and society: how law works in practice

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why a right written in a statute so often never reaches the person it was written for, describe the state of access to justice in Israel with current official figures, and map the path between a problem in your own life and a remedy, including where most people drop out and how you can avoid doing so.

## Why it matters

Legal doctrine describes what the law says. Law and society (sociology of law) studies what the law does: who uses it, who does not, what happens in courts and offices, and how informal norms, money, knowledge and power shape outcomes. The American jurist Roscoe Pound named the difference in 1910 as "law in books" and "law in action"; the socio-legal tradition since then has measured it.

Three ideas organise the field:

- **The dispute pyramid.** Of all the grievances people experience, only some are recognised as legal problems (naming), fewer are blamed on someone (blaming), fewer still become demands (claiming), and only a small fraction reach a court. Every step loses people, and the losses are not random.
- **Repeat players and one-shotters.** Marc Galanter's 1974 article "Why the 'Haves' Come Out Ahead" argued that organisations that litigate often (insurers, banks, the state) have structural advantages over individuals who litigate once: expertise, lower costs per case, the ability to play for rules rather than for a single result, and the ability to settle cases they might lose.
- **Living law.** Much social order rests on norms that are not law at all: business customs, community rules, family expectations. Formal law is one resource among several, and people often prefer the others.

For a self-represented person this is practical knowledge. It tells you where people like you usually fail (before ever filing), why the other side may be more comfortable in court than you are, and which informal or administrative routes often work better than litigation.

## The law

### Access to courts as a value in the Regulations

> החלטת בית המשפט בעניין פסיקת ההוצאות ושיעורן תבטא את האיזון הראוי שבין הבטחת זכות הגישה לערכאות, הגנה על זכות הקניין של הפרט ושמירה על שוויון בין בעלי הדין.

*Gist:* A court's decision on costs must balance the right of access to the courts, the individual's property right and equality between the parties. ([Civil Procedure Regulations 2018, reg. 151(ב)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/תקנות_סדר_הדין_האזרחי)) The sentence admits that costs rules can deter people from suing; lesson 11.5 shows how.

### Legal Aid: means, merit and subject

> לשכת סיוע משפטי תתן שירות משפטי, לרבות ההוצאות הכרוכות בו, בענינים ובהיקף שנקבעו בתקנות, למי שידו אינה משגת לשאת בהם, או למי שנקבע בתוספת בענינים ובהיקף שנקבעו בה;

*Gist:* Legal Aid gives legal services, including their expenses, in the matters and scope set by regulations, to people who cannot afford them, or to groups listed in the Schedule regardless of means. ([Legal Aid Law s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הסיוע_המשפטי)) The head of the office may refuse a case that is vexatious or has no reasonable basis in law, facts or evidence (s. 4); a refusal can be appealed to the district court (s. 5). Costs awarded to an aided person go to the State Treasury (s. 6). Lesson 01.6 explains how to apply.

### A court designed for the unrepresented

> ייצוג בעל דין בבית משפט לתביעות קטנות על ידי עורך דין יהיה רק ברשות בית המשפט ומטעמים מיוחדים שיירשמו;

*Gist:* In the small-claims court a lawyer may represent a party only with the court's permission and for special recorded reasons. ([Courts Law s. 63(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בתי_המשפט)) This is the clearest legislative attempt to level the field between repeat players and one-shotters: the company may not bring its law firm (lesson 08.3).

## Leading cases

Law-and-society work rests on data rather than rulings. The key texts below are the current official reports; all are free.

### Key text: Ministry of Justice, "Access to Justice in Israel 2023" (published December 2024)

The report is based on Israel's first large legal-needs survey, carried out in August 2022 among 2,032 adults, using the questionnaire the OECD recommends, translated in full into Arabic. Its main findings:

- About 70% of Israelis met, within two years, at least one legal need they could not solve without outside help; the average was 3.5 such needs.
- About 50% of legal needs were resolved within a year, about 30% carried on into the next year, and about 20% ended with the person giving up.
- About 80% tried to get help, most often from family and friends (48%); among professional sources, Kol-Zchut (31%) and lawyers (30%) led. Only about half of those who sought help said they got the help they needed. Getting help raised the chance of solving the problem.
- In only about two thirds of cases did people take any active step; consumer, employment and benefits problems had the highest rates of inaction.
- Only about 10% of attempts to resolve a problem went to court. Arab respondents went to court relatively often (18%), Haredi respondents somewhat less (9%) than non-Haredi Jewish respondents (12%).
- Mediation and informal processes were rated clearer, fairer and quicker than average but were used in only about 6% of cases. Courts were seen as fair and respectful but slow, complex and unpredictable.
- About 45% were not sure they could get a fair result in a dispute with an employer or a public authority.

Why it matters: the bottom of the dispute pyramid is where most rights are lost in Israel, not in court. [Report (PDF)](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/reports/access-to-justice-report/he/Access%20to%20Justice%20Report%202023.pdf)

### Key text: Legal Aid in 2025 (Ministry of Justice annual report)

The Ministry's 2025 report under the Freedom of Information Law says that Legal Aid made 108,812 appointments for representation in new proceedings in 2025, across civil law, debts, personal status, National Insurance, public housing and specialised areas such as legal capacity and old age, involuntary hospitalisation, crime victims and minors. It ran first-line advice points in Arab and Bedouin localities (about 4,000 clients in 2025), and from 30 April 2025, when an amendment widening aid to victims of sexual offences took effect, opened about 1,100 such files. It also notes that a new operating system introduced in 2025 changed how requests and appointments are counted, so year-on-year comparisons need care (lesson 11.4). [Report (PDF)](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/dynamiccollectorresultitem/2025-annual-report/he/2025-annual-report.pdf)

### Key text: the Judiciary's annual report for 2023

In 2023, 897,342 cases were opened and 838,259 closed in Israel's courts and labour tribunals, a clearance rate of 93.4%, with 497,799 cases pending at year end. The report attributes the drop partly to the emergency regime after 7 October 2023 (only urgent hearings until 30 November 2023), judicial vacancies, and an increase of about 35,900 new cases over 2022. At the end of 2023 there were 765 judges. Lesson 11.4 reads these figures in detail. [Report (PDF)](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/news/spokesmenmessage090724/he/%D7%93%D7%95%D7%97%20%D7%A9%D7%A0%D7%AA%D7%99%202023%20%D7%94%D7%A0%D7%94%D7%9C%D7%AA%20%D7%91%D7%AA%D7%99%20%D7%94%D7%9E%D7%A9%D7%A4%D7%98.pdf)

### Key text: State Comptroller, "Ensuring the Rights of People with Disabilities in Legal Proceedings" (Annual Report 69B)

The Comptroller found that accessibility duties under regulations had been implemented years late: the police began their initial review of required adjustments only in June 2018, about four years after the deadline; only about 28% of the Ministry of Justice lawyers who needed accessibility training had received it; judicial training had reached about 5% of judges and registrars; and in 95% or more of cases in which a guardian was appointed, the person concerned was not heard at all. [Report page](https://library.mevaker.gov.il/sites/DigitalLibrary/Pages/Reports/1427-3.aspx) The publication year shown in the file name is 2019; ⚠️ confirm on the Comptroller's site before citing the date.

Why it matters: it is a textbook gap between law in books (detailed accessibility regulations) and law in action.

## How it works in practice

### The dispute pyramid in Israeli figures

| Stage | What happens | Israeli evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Grievance | Something goes wrong | About 70% have an unsolved legal need in two years |
| Naming | You see it as a legal problem | Many do not; the survey distinguishes those who saw the problem as legal |
| Blaming and seeking help | You look for help | About 80% try, mostly family and friends; half do not get what they need |
| Claiming | You act: letter, complaint, negotiation | Only about two thirds take an active step |
| Court | You file | About 10% of resolution attempts |

### Why the haves come out ahead, in an Israeli courtroom

| Advantage of a repeat player | Example | What a one-shotter can do |
|---|---|---|
| Knows the procedure and the judges | Insurer's claims department | Read lessons 08.2–08.3; use the court information centre |
| Spreads costs over many cases | Bank or phone company | Use small claims, where lawyers need leave (s. 63) |
| Plays for the rule, settles risky cases | A company settles the strong claims quietly | Ask for a reasoned judgment if the issue matters to others; consider a class action (lesson 11.1) |
| Has documents and records | The company's call logs and contracts | Ask for your file and records in writing early; keep your own evidence |
| Can wait | Delay costs the company little | Ask for fast track or small claims; claim interest |

### Informal and administrative routes

The survey shows that most problems are handled, if at all, outside court. For many everyday disputes the cheapest effective routes are: a written demand to the other side; a complaint to the regulator (consumer, banking, insurance, communications); the Ombudsman for public bodies (lesson 06.5); a request under the Freedom of Information Law (lesson 02.7); and mediation. Courts remain the backstop, and the threat of a well-prepared claim is often what makes the informal routes work.

### Critical voices

- **Data limits.** A survey measures what people report, not what happened; legal needs are counted differently across countries.
- **Who sets the agenda.** Critical scholars (lesson 09.4) ask whether "access to justice" programmes help people use the law as it is, or whether they hide the need to change the law itself.
- **Delegalisation.** Some argue that pushing disputes to mediation and self-help lowers the visibility of injustice; others answer that most people simply want their problem solved quickly.

## Worked example

Amal Abu Kaf, 34, from Rahat, worked for eight months as a cleaner through a manpower contractor at a public office in Be'er Sheva. She was paid less than the hours on her time sheets show, received no payslips for three months, and was let go without notice. She has not done anything for five months.

1. **Naming.** Amal thought of it as "bad luck with an employer", not a legal problem. Her cousin, who read a Kol-Zchut page in Arabic, told her that unpaid wages and missing payslips are rights violations. This is the step the survey shows many people never take.
2. **Barriers.** She lists them: she does not know the contractor's legal name; she fears the cost of a lawyer; she works days; the labour court is in Be'er Sheva; she has only photos of time sheets.
3. **Help.** She calls Legal Aid and checks the means test; she also contacts a workers' rights organisation and the Legal Aid "access to justice" point at the court. She learns that a worker may file a labour-court claim herself, and asks Legal Aid whether her matter is one it covers and whether she passes its tests.
4. **Cheapest effective route.** First, a written demand to the contractor and to the public body that used its services, asking for payslips and the unpaid sums, with a 14-day deadline. Second, a complaint to the Ministry of Labour's enforcement unit about missing payslips. Third, if there is no answer, a claim in the regional labour court, by herself or with Legal Aid.
5. **Outcome.** The contractor sends the missing payslips and pays part of the wages after the public body asks it to respond. Amal keeps the remainder of the claim open and diarises the limitation period.

What changed was not the law but Amal's position in the pyramid: from not naming the problem to claiming, with help.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your own list of problems over the last two years: work, housing, consumer, benefits, debts, family, dealings with authorities.
- The Ministry of Justice report (link above), or its summary in this lesson.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Inventory (10 min).** List every problem you had in the last two years that involved a right or an obligation, even if you never thought of it as legal. Aim for at least five.
2. **Pyramid (10 min).** For each, mark how far it went: grievance, named as legal, sought help, made a claim, reached a body that decides (court, tribunal, regulator, committee). Note the outcome.
3. **Barriers (10 min).** For the two problems that stopped earliest, write the barriers: knowledge, cost, time, fear, language, distance, documents, the other side's power.
4. **Repeat-player check (5 min).** For each, was the other side a repeat player? Which of its advantages applied?
5. **Route (10 min).** For one unresolved problem, choose the cheapest route that could still solve it (demand letter, regulator, Ombudsman, FOI request, mediation, small claims, labour court). Find the free help that applies (see [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md)).
6. **One action (5 min).** Write the first concrete step and a date. If a deadline may apply, find it now.

### Template

None. Use a table with the columns: problem, stage reached, barriers, other side, route, first step.

### How you know it worked

- You found at least one problem you had not recognised as legal.
- For one problem you identified a route and a free source of help, and set a date for the first step.
- You can say which stage of the pyramid you personally tend to drop out at.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. It helps you decide your own case by showing where you are likely to lose your right before any court is involved, and which routes are cheaper than litigation. You may always act for yourself; in the small-claims court the other side normally may not bring a lawyer either (s. 63).

### Where and how

- Information: Kol-Zchut (Hebrew and Arabic), the court information centres and the Judiciary's call centre for procedure.
- Free representation: Legal Aid, if you pass the means and merit tests or belong to a group listed in the Schedule (lesson 01.6); law-school clinics; workers', tenants' and consumers' organisations.
- Public bodies: the Ombudsman (lesson 06.5) and the regulator responsible for the sector.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- A deadline is close and you do not know which forum is right.
- The problem touches your liberty, your home, custody of children or legal capacity (guardianship): ask Legal Aid or the Public Defender at once.
- You have a disability or language barrier and the forum is not accommodating it: ask for an accommodation in writing and get help to enforce it.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treating a rights problem as bad luck | The right expires unused | Check Kol-Zchut or ask for advice now | Ask "is there a rule about this?" for every problem |
| Relying only on family advice | Wrong information, missed deadlines | Confirm with an official or professional source | Use family for support, not law |
| Going straight to court when a regulator or the other side's complaints unit would solve it | Months of delay, fees | Send a demand or complaint while the case is pending | Climb the pyramid one step at a time |
| Giving up after one refusal | You join the 20% who abandon | Escalate: written demand, regulator, Ombudsman, court | Plan two further steps before you start |
| Assuming Legal Aid is only for criminal cases | You never apply | Apply for civil aid (Legal Aid is civil; the Public Defender is criminal) | Read lesson 01.6 |

## Review

- Law in books and law in action differ because rights must be named, blamed and claimed before any court can enforce them, and each step loses people.
- Israel's 2022 legal-needs survey (report of December 2024) found that about 70% have an unsolved legal need within two years, about 20% of needs end with giving up, and only about 10% of resolution attempts reach a court.
- Legal Aid made 108,812 appointments in new proceedings in 2025; the courts closed 838,259 cases against 897,342 opened in 2023 (clearance rate 93.4%).
- Repeat players have structural advantages; small claims (s. 63), Legal Aid and class actions are partial counterweights.
- In your own affairs, use the cheapest route that can work, and do not drop out at the first step.
