# 11.2 · Behavioural law and economics

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise the predictable mistakes that real people, including judges and lawyers, make with numbers, risks and losses, and use that knowledge in two places where it matters most to a self-represented person: negotiating a settlement and reading a consumer contract. You will see which Israeli consumer rules were written with these mistakes in mind, and you will rewrite a settlement offer so that it anchors the discussion where you want it.

## Why it matters

Classical law and economics (lesson 11.1) assumes that people are rational: they know what they want, use the information they have, and choose what serves them best. Behavioural economics, built on the work of the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, showed in decades of experiments that people depart from this model in systematic, predictable ways. Behavioural law and economics asks what follows for legal rules.

Two consequences matter in this course:

- **Bargaining fails more often than the Coase model predicts.** People overvalue what they already have, see fairness through their own interest, and react to the first number put on the table. Disputes that "should" settle go to trial, and settlements are reached at figures shaped by anchors rather than by the merits.
- **Contracts are not read, and defaults stick.** Most consumers accept standard terms without reading them and stay with whatever the default is. Lawmakers respond with mandatory rules, defaults that favour the consumer, and disclosure duties. Israeli consumer law is full of such rules.

The academic debate is real. Supporters say behavioural findings justify "nudges" and protective rules. Critics reply that many experiments use students and small stakes, that real markets and experienced professionals correct some biases, and that regulators are human and biased too, so protective rules can reflect the lawmaker's mistakes rather than correct the consumer's.

## The law

Israel has no "behavioural statute". The rules below are ordinary consumer and procedural rules whose design answers specific biases.

### Inertia and automatic renewal: Consumer Protection Law s. 13א

> לתנאי בחוזה הקובע כי ההתקשרות בין הצדדים תימשך לאחר מועד סיום העסקה או ההתחייבות, לא יהיה תוקף, ויראו כאילו נקבע בחוזה שהוא בטל באותו מועד.

*Gist:* In a fixed-term consumer transaction (including a period at a discounted price), a term that the contract continues after the end date is void, and the contract is treated as ending on that date. ([s. 13א(ג)(1)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הגנת_הצרכן)) The dealer must notify the consumer of the end date within a set notice window before it ([current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md)); an extension needs the consumer's consent, and the dealer bears the burden of proving it (s. 13א(ה)). Some listed services may continue if the dealer gave written notice in the window (s. 13א(ד)).

Behavioural reading: without the rule, consumer inertia (status-quo bias, forgetting) would turn every introductory discount into an open-ended, more expensive contract. The law flips the default: silence ends the contract instead of renewing it.

### Easy exit from continuing transactions: s. 13ד

A continuing transaction ends within a few business days of the consumer's cancellation notice given in one of the ways s. 14ט requires the dealer to offer (for example by phone, email or on the website), with longer periods for registered mail ([current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md)). ([s. 13ד(ג)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הגנת_הצרכן)) Behavioural reading: the law attacks "friction", the deliberate hassle of cancelling that relies on people giving up.

### Unread standard terms: Standard Contracts Law s. 3

> בית משפט ובית הדין יבטלו או ישנו, בהתאם להוראות חוק זה, תנאי בחוזה אחיד שיש בו – בשים לב למכלול תנאי החוזה ולנסיבות אחרות – משום קיפוח לקוחות או משום יתרון בלתי הוגן של הספק העלול להביא לידי קיפוח לקוחות

*Gist:* A court will cancel or change a term in a standard contract that deprives customers or gives the supplier an unfair advantage, considering the contract as a whole. ([s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_האחידים)) Section 4 lists terms presumed unfair. Lesson 03.6 covers the doctrine. Behavioural reading: since rational customers do not read terms they cannot negotiate, and optimistic customers assume nothing will go wrong, competition does not discipline the fine print; the court does.

### Settlement proposals by the court

> בית משפט הדן בענין אזרחי רשאי, בהסכמת בעלי הדין, לפסוק בענין שלפניו, כולו או מקצתו, בדרך של פשרה.

*Gist:* With the parties' consent the court may decide a civil case by way of compromise. ([Courts Law s. 79א(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בתי_המשפט)) At the pre-trial stage the court may also, on its own initiative, propose a settlement to the parties (reg. 63(ב)(17) of the [Civil Procedure Regulations 2018](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/תקנות_סדר_הדין_האזרחי)). Lesson 08.6 covers the procedure. Behavioural reading: a number proposed by the judge is a powerful anchor for both sides, because it carries the court's authority.

## Leading cases

There is no Israeli ruling that applies behavioural economics as a doctrine. The key texts are research works, all free.

### Key text: Kahneman, "Maps of Bounded Rationality" (Nobel lecture, 2002)

Kahneman summarises the joint work with Tversky in three areas: judgment heuristics (representativeness, availability, anchoring), risky choice (prospect theory) and framing. The central claim of prospect theory is that people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses from a reference point, not as final states of wealth; that they are risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses; and that the value function is loss-averse, steeper for losses than for gains "by a factor of about 2–2.5". [Lecture (PDF)](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/kahnemann-lecture.pdf)

Why it matters: in a lawsuit the plaintiff usually sees a settlement as a sure gain and the defendant sees it as a sure loss. Prospect theory predicts that, with the same odds, the plaintiff will tend to accept a sure sum and the defendant will tend to gamble on trial. Knowing which frame you are in, and which frame the other side is in, helps you shape an offer.

### Key text: Jolls, Sunstein and Thaler, "A Behavioral Approach to Law and Economics" (1998)

The article that launched the field in legal scholarship. Two of its points are directly useful. First, self-serving bias: parties see fairness in the light most favourable to themselves, which helps explain why cases that should settle are litigated. Second, citing Ward Farnsworth's study of about twenty nuisance cases litigated to an injunction or its refusal, it reports that in none did the parties even try to bargain around the court order after judgment, although economic theory predicts they would. [Open-access working paper](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-3e88-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)

Why it matters: it explains why the Coase deals of lesson 11.1 are rarer than the model predicts, and why the time to settle is before positions harden, not after judgment.

### Key text: Guthrie, Rachlinski and Wistrich, "Inside the Judicial Mind" (2001)

The authors tested 167 American federal magistrate judges on five cognitive illusions: anchoring, framing, hindsight bias, the representativeness heuristic and egocentric bias. Each had a significant effect on the judges' decisions, although judges were somewhat less susceptible to framing and representativeness than lay people. In the anchoring test an irrelevant figure presented to judges shifted the damages they awarded. [Cornell repository, free PDF](https://scholarship.law.cornell.edu/facpub/814/)

Why it matters: judges are expert but human. The figure you put in your statement of claim, and the order in which you present facts, can influence the result. That cuts both ways: an inflated claim may anchor, but it also raises the court fee and the risk of costs (lesson 11.5), and it can cost you credibility.

## How it works in practice

### The biases a self-represented litigant meets

| Bias | What it does | Where you meet it | Counter-move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | The first number pulls later estimates towards it | The other side's opening offer; the sum in a claim; a judge's proposal | Make the first reasoned offer yourself; tie every number to a document |
| Loss aversion and framing | Losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please | Defendant refusing to "lose" money; plaintiff afraid of losing at trial | Frame your offer as avoiding a loss for them (costs, interest, time) |
| Endowment effect | People value what they have more than what they could get | A tenant refusing to leave; a seller holding out for an unrealistic price | Separate the object from the money: what would you pay to buy it today? |
| Self-serving bias | Each side thinks the fair result is close to its own position | Both parties confident of winning | Ask a neutral person to read both sides' documents; write the other side's best argument |
| Over-optimism | People overestimate their chance of success | "I will surely win" | Use base rates (lesson 11.4) and the worksheet in lesson 11.5 |
| Status-quo bias | Defaults stick | Auto-renewing subscriptions; signing the standard form as it is | Diary the end date; use s. 13א; ask for the term to be changed before signing |
| Sunk-cost fallacy | Past costs drive future decisions | "I have already spent so much on this case" | Decide only on future costs and benefits |

### Settlement offers: a short method

1. **Find your reference point and theirs.** For you, it may be what you paid; for them, what they think they owe (often zero). Each will frame the same figure differently.
2. **Anchor with reasons.** Open with a figure you can justify line by line: invoice, repair quote, receipts, interest. A reasoned anchor is harder to dismiss than a round number.
3. **Frame as a loss avoided.** "If we go to court you will also pay the court fee, costs and interest" speaks to the other side's loss aversion. Link to concrete rules: costs under reg. 151 and interest under the Adjudication of Interest and Linkage Law (lesson 11.5).
4. **Expect the judge's number.** If a judge proposes a compromise, do not accept or reject it on the spot because it is "the court's view". Ask for time, compare it with your expected value (lesson 11.5), and answer in writing if the court allows.
5. **Watch your own biases.** Before the meeting, write down your walk-away figure, based on expected value, and why. Bring it with you.

### Consumer contracts: what to check

- **End date and renewal.** Does the contract renew automatically at a higher price? If it is a fixed-term transaction, s. 13א voids automatic continuation; note the end date in your calendar anyway.
- **Exit route.** How can you cancel? The dealer must offer the ways listed in s. 14ט. Keep proof of the notice.
- **Unfair terms.** Exclusion of liability, unilateral changes, forum and arbitration clauses: compare with the presumptions in Standard Contracts Law s. 4.
- **Framing in the price.** "Only ₪9.90 a month" for 36 months is ₪356.40; "first month free" anchors you at zero. Convert every offer into a total cost.

## Worked example

Esther Gutman, a teacher from Bnei Brak, had her car repaired at a garage after a minor accident. The garage charged ₪7,800 and the car broke down again a week later with the same fault; a second garage fixed it for ₪3,400, with a written report that the first repair was faulty. Esther wants the ₪3,400 back, plus ₪400 for towing.

1. **The other side's anchor.** The first garage's manager calls and offers "a goodwill gesture" of ₪500, and says he "never pays for other people's work". His reference point is zero: any payment feels like a loss to him.
2. **Esther's anchor.** She writes a pre-action letter (template in lesson 08.8) claiming ₪3,800, itemised: second repair ₪3,400 (invoice and report attached) and towing ₪400 (receipt), with a 14-day deadline and a statement that she will sue in the small-claims court for the sum with interest and costs.
3. **Framing.** Her letter adds one paragraph: a claim would cost him a day in court, the court fee he may be ordered to repay, costs and interest, and a judgment on record. Paying now avoids those losses.
4. **His counter-offer.** He offers ₪2,000. Esther's walk-away figure, worked out with the method in lesson 11.5, is ₪2,900: her chance of winning is high, but the hearing would cost her a working day and several months.
5. **The deal.** She proposes ₪3,200 paid within 7 days, citing the report again. He agrees to ₪3,000. She signs a short settlement that includes a default clause (lesson 08.6).
6. **Consumer side.** The garage's work order she had signed included a printed line that "the garage is not liable for any later fault". Esther notes, for her file, that such a term is a candidate for cancellation under Standard Contracts Law s. 3–4, which strengthened her position.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Either a dispute in which you have received, or plan to make, a money offer, or a consumer contract you have signed (phone, gym, insurance, streaming, kindergarten). Ideally both.
- The contract and any letters or offers.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Bias audit (10 min).** For your dispute, write which biases from the table are likely to affect you and which the other side. Give one sentence of evidence for each.
2. **Reference points (5 min).** Write your reference point and your best guess of theirs. How will each of you frame a settlement at the midpoint?
3. **Rewrite the offer (15 min).** Draft a short Hebrew settlement offer that (a) anchors with an itemised figure tied to documents, (b) frames the offer as avoiding losses for the other side, (c) states a deadline. Do not send it yet.
4. **Contract check (15 min).** For your consumer contract, find: the end date, whether it renews, the price after any discount period, how to cancel, and any term that looks like one of the presumptions in Standard Contracts Law s. 4. Convert the price into a total cost.
5. **Action (5 min).** Put the end date and the notice window in your calendar. Decide whether to cancel, renegotiate or stay.

### Template

None. Lesson 08.8 has a pre-action letter template; lesson 03.10 has a consumer withdrawal notice.

### How you know it worked

- Every number in your offer is tied to a document.
- Your offer states what the other side avoids by accepting, not only what you want.
- You know your contract's end date and how to leave it, and have noted it.

- [ ] I can define anchoring, loss aversion, the endowment effect and self-serving bias with an example from litigation.
- [ ] I can explain why a defendant may gamble on trial when a plaintiff with the same odds would settle.
- [ ] I can explain how s. 13א of the Consumer Protection Law responds to inertia.
- [ ] I wrote down my walk-away figure before negotiating.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Behavioural insight helps you decide your own case: how to open a negotiation, how to answer a judge's proposal, and when your own optimism is misleading you. Anyone may negotiate and settle their own dispute; a settlement can be given the force of a judgment (lesson 08.6).

### Where and how

- Consumer disputes: the Consumer Protection and Fair Trade Authority handles complaints about dealers; small claims is the usual court (lesson 08.3).
- Cancelling a continuing transaction: use one of the ways the dealer must offer under s. 14ט and keep the confirmation.
- Free information: Kol-Zchut explains consumer rights in plain Hebrew; the court information centres explain procedure (see [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md)).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- The other side's offer comes with a general waiver of all claims ("ויתור על כל טענה") and you have other possible claims, such as personal injury.
- A judge's proposal is for a settlement in a matter that affects your status or home (eviction, family, land).
- The contract involves a large sum or long commitment (a mortgage, a purchase of a flat, a franchise) and the terms are complex.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Letting the other side make the first offer in a dispute about a sum you can document | Talks start around their low anchor | Re-anchor with an itemised figure and documents | Send your reasoned figure first |
| Accepting a judge's proposal on the spot because it "must be right" | You may settle below your expected value | Ask for time to consider and answer in writing | Prepare your walk-away figure before the hearing |
| Inflating the claim to anchor high | Higher court fee, risk of costs, loss of credibility | Amend to a supported figure | Claim what you can prove |
| Relying on a gym or phone contract's renewal notice | You pay months you did not want | Cancel by a s. 14ט method and ask for a refund if s. 13א was breached | Diary the end date yourself |
| Believing you are "sure to win" | You reject a fair offer and lose time and money | Re-run the numbers with a neutral reader | Use base rates and lesson 11.5's worksheet |

## Review

- Behavioural economics shows systematic departures from rationality: anchoring, loss aversion and framing, the endowment effect, status-quo bias, over-optimism and self-serving bias.
- In litigation these biases explain failed settlements, the force of the first number, and the weight of a judge's proposal; the counter-moves are reasoned anchors, loss framing and a walk-away figure fixed in advance.
- Israeli consumer law answers inertia and unread terms with void automatic renewal (s. 13א), easy cancellation (ss. 13ד, 14ט) and judicial control of unfair standard terms (Standard Contracts Law ss. 3–4).
- Judges are expert but human; research on judges finds the same illusions, somewhat weaker for some.
- Critics warn that experiments may not transfer to real markets and that regulators are biased too.
