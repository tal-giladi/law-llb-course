# 11.1 · Law and economics: efficiency, incentives, Coase

By the end of this lesson you will be able to look at a legal rule the way an economist does: ask what behaviour it rewards and punishes, who bears the cost, and whether the parties could have reached a better result by bargaining. You will work through the Coase theorem with numbers, learn the difference between a property rule and a liability rule, and see how the Israeli Supreme Court dealt with both ideas in Ata v. Schwartz, the 1976 factory-noise case that brought economic analysis into Israeli law.

## Why it matters

Every legal rule is also a price. A rule that makes a factory pay for the noise it causes raises the cost of making noise; a rule that lets a court order the factory to stop gives the neighbour a right the factory must buy if it wants to keep running. Law and economics studies these prices: how rules change incentives, which rules produce the most value for society, and what happens when bargaining between the parties is cheap or expensive.

The approach matters at university level for three reasons:

- **It is a theory of legal doctrine.** Much of tort, contract and property law can be read as an attempt to put costs on the person best able to avoid them. Lessons 04.2 (negligence) and 03.8 (remedies) contain rules that make most sense in those terms.
- **It is a contested theory of justice.** Critics answer that efficiency counts dollars, not people; that a rich party's willingness to pay is not a better claim than a poor party's need; and that some rights should not be for sale. The Israeli Supreme Court voiced exactly these objections in Ata v. Schwartz, while using the economists' vocabulary.
- **It is a practical tool.** For a person handling their own dispute, the economic questions (What is this worth to each side? What does fighting cost? What deal would leave both better off?) are often more useful than the doctrinal ones. Lesson 11.5 turns them into a worksheet for deciding whether to sue.

## The law

The statutes below are not "economic" laws. They are ordinary private-law rules whose structure the economic analysis explains. Each is quoted from the consolidated text on Wikisource.

### Private nuisance: the entitlement

> מיטרד ליחיד הוא כשאדם מתנהג בעצמו או מנהל את עסקו או משתמש במקרקעין התפושים בידו באופן שיש בו הפרעה של ממש לשימוש סביר במקרקעין של אדם אחר או להנאה סבירה מהם בהתחשב עם מקומם וטיבם;

*Gist:* Private nuisance is conduct, a business or a use of land that substantially interferes with the reasonable use or enjoyment of another's land, judged by the place and its character; damages require damage. ([Civil Wrongs Ordinance s. 44(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הנזיקין)) Lesson 04.4 covers the elements; here the point is that s. 44 *allocates an entitlement*: the neighbour, not the business, has the right to reasonable quiet.

### The remedy: injunction first, damages as the exception

> בית המשפט לא יתן ציווי, אם הוא סבור שהפגיעה או הנזק שנגרמו לתובע הם קטנים וניתנים להערכה בכסף ולפיצוי מספיק בתשלום כסף, ומתן ציווי יהיה בו משום התעמרות בנתבע, אך רשאי הוא לפסוק פיצויים.

*Gist:* The court will not grant an injunction where the harm is small, measurable in money and adequately compensated by payment, and an injunction would be oppressive to the defendant; it may award damages instead. ([s. 74](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הנזיקין)) In economic terms, the default protection is a **property rule** (the defendant must stop, or buy the right from the plaintiff); s. 74 lets the court switch to a **liability rule** (the defendant may continue and pay a court-set price) only when all its conditions are met.

### Contract: enforcement first, damages, and mitigation

> הנפגע זכאי לאכיפת החוזה, זולת אם נתקיימה אחת מאלה:

*Gist:* The injured party is entitled to specific performance unless the contract is impossible to perform, concerns personal work or service, needs unreasonable court supervision, or enforcement would be unjust. ([Remedies Law s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_(תרופות_בשל_הפרת_חוזה))) Israeli contract law, unlike English common law, makes enforcement the primary remedy: again a property rule by default.

> הנפגע זכאי לפיצויים בעד הנזק שנגרם לו עקב ההפרה ותוצאותיה ושהמפר ראה אותו או שהיה עליו לראותו מראש...

*Gist:* Damages cover the foreseeable loss caused by the breach. ([s. 10](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_(תרופות_בשל_הפרת_חוזה)))

> אין המפר חייב בפיצויים ... בעד נזק שהנפגע יכול היה, באמצעים סבירים, למנוע או להקטין.

*Gist:* The breaching party does not pay for loss the injured party could reasonably have avoided or reduced, but does reimburse reasonable mitigation costs. ([s. 14](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_(תרופות_בשל_הפרת_חוזה))) This is the cleanest efficiency rule in Israeli private law: it puts the cost of avoidable loss on the person who could avoid it most cheaply.

## Leading cases

### ע״א 44/76 אתא חברה לטקסטיל בע״מ נ׳ שוורץ, פ״ד ל(3) 785 (1976)

Ata, then one of Israel's largest industrial companies, ran a textile plant in Kiryat Ata separated from a residential area by a narrow buffer strip. From the 1950s its cooling and ventilation systems produced constant noise. Ze'ev Schwartz, who had lived on his plot since the 1930s, when the plant was still small, sued in nuisance. The magistrates' court ordered the factory to stop the nuisance; the order was upheld on two appeals, up to the Supreme Court, despite the company's argument that it would cause serious harm to employment and the economy.

The Supreme Court discussed the then-new property-rule/liability-rule analysis of Calabresi and Melamed and declined to turn the case into one for damages only: treating payment as a substitute for stopping a nuisance would, in its view, frustrate the legislature's choice to make nuisance a wrong to be prevented, and would remove the defendant's incentive to look for a technical solution. Section 74 was read as a narrow exception.

Why it matters: it is the Israeli leading case on the choice between injunction and damages, and the first serious engagement of an Israeli court with economic analysis. David Schorr's historical study argues that the judgment, while praising individual rights and criticising economic reasoning, in fact imported that reasoning into Israeli law. The judgment itself is not on a free host the course could open; read Schorr's free article (Sources) for the facts and the reasoning, and cite the case by its פ״ד reference.

### ע״א 10085/08 תנובה מרכז שיתופי נ׳ עיזבון ראבי (2011)

In 1995 Tnuva added silicone to about 13 million litres of long-life milk without listing it on the label. A consumer class action succeeded in the district court; the Supreme Court reduced the award to ₪38.5 million, recognised compensation for infringement of autonomy, noted that the accumulation of small harms lies at the heart of the class action, and, because individual members could not be identified, ordered the money to be used for the public benefit under s. 20(ג) of the Class Actions Law.

Why it matters: each consumer's loss was far too small to sue for. Without aggregation, the expected cost of breaking the labelling rules would have been close to zero, and so would the incentive to obey them. The class action is the legal system's answer to a collective-action problem that economics predicts. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/386)

### Key text: Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960)

Coase argued that harm is reciprocal (the factory harms the neighbour, but stopping the factory harms the factory) and that, if bargaining were costless, the parties would reach the efficient use of resources whatever the law said, because whoever values the right more would buy it. The law's allocation would decide only who pays whom. Coase's real point, which he stressed later, was the opposite of a slogan that "law does not matter": because transaction costs are never zero, the legal rule often decides the outcome, so lawmakers must compare the real costs of alternative rules. [Chicago Unbound record](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jle/vol3/iss1/2); background on the [University of Chicago's page](https://www.law.uchicago.edu/lawecon/coaseinmemoriam/problemofsocialcost).

### Key text: Calabresi and Melamed, "One View of the Cathedral" (1972)

The article distinguished three ways of protecting an entitlement: a **property rule** (it can be taken only with the holder's consent, at a price the holder accepts), a **liability rule** (it can be taken if the taker pays a price set by a court) and **inalienability** (it cannot be sold at all, such as a vote or a kidney). Combined with the question of who gets the entitlement, this yields four possible nuisance rules, including the "fourth rule" in which the polluter may be stopped but only if the neighbours compensate it. [Free text](https://cyber.harvard.edu/openlaw/DVD/articles/calabresi.html)

## How it works in practice

### Efficiency in two senses

- **Pareto efficiency:** a change is an improvement if it makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off. Voluntary contracts are the standard example. Few legal rules meet this test, because almost every rule has losers.
- **Kaldor-Hicks efficiency:** a change is an improvement if the winners gain more than the losers lose, so that the winners *could* compensate the losers, even if they do not. Most law-and-economics arguments use this test. Its critics point out that the losers are not in fact paid.

### Incentives: the expected cost of breaking a rule

An economist asks what a rule costs the person deciding whether to obey it. If a breach costs a gain $G$, and the probability of being sued and losing is $p$ with damages $D$, a purely self-interested actor breaks the rule when

$$G > p \times D$$

This simple inequality explains a great deal: why rules that nobody enforces are ignored, why small harms spread over many people (Tnuva) go unremedied without class actions, and why courts sometimes add costs or statutory compensation without proof of damage. It also shows the limit of the model: many people obey rules because they believe in them, not because of $p$ and $D$ (lesson 11.3).

The same logic underlies the "Hand formula" from American negligence law: a precaution costing $B$ is required if $B < P \times L$, where $P$ is the probability of the accident and $L$ the loss. It is a way of reading the reasonable-person test of lesson 04.2, not a rule an Israeli court applies mechanically.

### Coase with numbers

A carpentry workshop's machines disturb the family next door. Silencing the machines costs ₪30,000. The family values quiet at ₪20,000 (the drop in the value of their home and their own discomfort).

| Legal rule | No transaction costs | High transaction costs |
|---|---|---|
| Family has the right (injunction) | Workshop offers between ₪20,000 and ₪30,000 to keep working; family accepts. Machines stay loud. | No deal; workshop spends ₪30,000 on silencing. ₪10,000 of value lost. |
| Workshop has the right (no nuisance) | Family would pay at most ₪20,000, less than ₪30,000; no deal. Machines stay loud. | Same: machines stay loud. |

With costless bargaining the result is the same (loud machines, the efficient outcome) under both rules; only the payment differs. With high transaction costs (many neighbours, distrust, lawyers' fees, the family's refusal to "sell" its quiet), the legal rule decides the outcome. The theorem is a tool for seeing *why* the law matters, not a proof that it does not.

### Property or liability rule: an Israeli map

| Entitlement | Usual protection | Economic reading |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom from nuisance (s. 44) | Injunction; damages only under s. 74 | Property rule: the defendant must bargain |
| Contractual performance (Remedies Law s. 3) | Specific performance, with exceptions | Property rule; limits "efficient breach" |
| Physical harm in negligence | Damages | Liability rule: courts set the price |
| Land taken for public purposes | Compensation set by law | Liability rule imposed by the state |
| Body, vote, liberty | Cannot be sold | Inalienability |

### Where the analysis meets its critics

- **Distribution.** Willingness to pay depends on wealth. A rule chosen because the factory "values" its noise more may simply favour the richer party. The Ata court made this kind of point when it refused to let the company buy its way out.
- **Endowment and refusal.** People demand more to give up a right than they would pay to acquire it (lesson 11.2). That makes bargaining harder than the Coase model assumes.
- **Rights.** Some entitlements (dignity, liberty, a home) are seen as beyond the market. Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty and the inalienability category of Calabresi and Melamed are two ways of expressing this.

## Worked example

Rabia Halabi runs a small furniture workshop on the ground floor of a mixed building in Daliyat al-Karmel. Yusra and Kamal Kheir, who live on the floor above, work night shifts and sleep in the mornings; the planer and the compressor wake them every day from 8:00. They have complained twice. Rabia says the workshop was there first and that his business supports his family and two workers.

1. **Entitlement.** Under s. 44, the question is substantial interference with reasonable use, judged by the place and its character. Coming to the nuisance is no defence (lesson 04.4). In a mixed building, ordinary daytime workshop noise may be reasonable; very loud machinery directly below a flat may not. Assume a court would probably find a nuisance at the noise levels the Kheirs have recorded.
2. **Remedy.** The Kheirs' harm is not small or easily measured in money (it is sleep and health), so s. 74 probably does not apply: the likely remedy is an injunction limiting hours or requiring insulation. The Kheirs hold a property right.
3. **Values.** Moving the planer and compressor to the back room and insulating the ceiling costs about ₪12,000. Starting work at 11:00 instead of 8:00 would cost Rabia about ₪1,500 a month in lost output. The Kheirs value quiet mornings highly; selling or moving would cost them far more than either figure.
4. **Bargaining range.** Litigation would cost both sides time, fees and the relationship. A deal in which Rabia insulates and moves the two loudest machines within 60 days, and the Kheirs accept normal hand-tool work from 8:00, gives both a better result than a court order setting hours. The Kheirs could even contribute a small sum to the insulation (the "fourth rule" idea), which speeds the work.
5. **Transaction costs.** The obstacle is not money but mistrust. A written agreement with a deadline, a noise measurement before and after, and a clause that the Kheirs may sue if it is not done (lesson 08.6) lowers it.

Outcome: a signed agreement, insulation done in seven weeks, and no claim. The economic analysis did not decide who was right; it found the deal both could live with.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One dispute or friction in your own life where someone's activity imposes a cost on someone else: a neighbour, a landlord, a supplier, a shared driveway. A supplied option: the worked example with different figures.
- A note of rough money values: what each side gains from the activity, what it costs the other, what each solution costs.
- 45 minutes.

### Steps

1. Describe the activity and the harm in two sentences, and name the legal rule that allocates the entitlement (for example s. 44 for nuisance, a contract clause, a by-law).
2. Decide who holds the entitlement now, and whether it is protected by a property rule (injunction, enforcement) or a liability rule (damages). Cite the section.
3. Estimate three numbers: the value of the activity to its owner, the harm to the other side, and the cost of the cheapest fix. Write the Coase table for your case.
4. List the transaction costs that block a deal: number of parties, information, mistrust, emotions, the cost of lawyers or court.
5. Propose one settlement that leaves both sides better off than litigation. State what each side gives and gets, and how you would reduce the transaction costs (deadline, measurement, written terms).
6. Write one paragraph on the strongest non-economic objection to your proposal (fairness, rights, the relationship) and how you would answer it.

### Template

None. Use the steps as headings.

### How you know it worked

- Your analysis names the legal rule and the remedy, not only the money.
- Your proposed settlement is better for each side than its realistic result in court, in its own terms.
- You identified at least one transaction cost and a concrete way to reduce it.

### Self-check

- [ ] I can state the Coase theorem and why transaction costs make the legal rule decisive.
- [ ] I can explain the difference between a property rule and a liability rule with an Israeli example of each.
- [ ] I can summarise the result and the reasoning of Ata v. Schwartz.
- [ ] I can explain why class actions matter for incentives when each harm is small.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The economic questions help you decide your own case before any filing: whether a demand letter, a negotiated deal or a claim is worth it, and what to ask for. Anyone may sue for themselves in the magistrates' and small-claims courts (lesson 08.3); an injunction to stop a nuisance needs the magistrates' court, because the small-claims court cannot order a neighbour to stop (lesson 04.4).

### Where and how

- Use the Coase table in your demand letter: a concrete offer that saves both sides the cost of a trial is more persuasive than an accusation.
- Before suing over noise or smells, ask the municipality's environmental or licensing unit to measure; its report is evidence and sometimes ends the matter.
- Mediation (lesson 08.6) is the institutional way of lowering transaction costs. In the magistrates' court above the threshold in [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md), the mediation-intake meeting is free.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- You need an urgent temporary injunction (a ציווי לשעה under s. 73): the evidence and drafting must be right the first time.
- The other side is a large business or a public body and argues public interest, planning permission or statutory authority.
- The dispute concerns land rights, a building permit or a condominium's common property, where other laws and forums apply.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading Coase as "the law does not matter" | You ignore the rule that will decide your case | Ask what the rule decides when bargaining is costly | Always add transaction costs to the analysis |
| Asking a court for damages when you want the activity stopped | You get money and the noise continues | Amend the claim to seek an injunction in the right court | Decide which remedy you need before choosing the court |
| Assuming a business may "pay to continue" a nuisance | You overestimate the defendant's position | Cite s. 74's narrow conditions and Ata | Treat injunction as the default for nuisance |
| Valuing only money | A deal fails because the other side's real interest was sleep, pride or a relationship | Reopen with an offer that addresses that interest | List non-money interests in step 3 |
| Ignoring mitigation | Damages reduced under Remedies Law s. 14 | Show the steps you took and their cost | Take reasonable steps early and keep receipts |

## Review

- Law and economics reads legal rules as prices that change behaviour; efficiency (Pareto, Kaldor-Hicks) is its main normative standard, and distribution and rights are the main objections.
- The Coase theorem says that with costless bargaining the outcome does not depend on the legal rule; because bargaining is never costless, the rule and the remedy often decide the outcome.
- Israeli law protects nuisance and contract rights mainly by property rules (injunction under the Civil Wrongs Ordinance, enforcement under Remedies Law s. 3), with damages as the exception (s. 74) and mitigation (s. 14) as a clear efficiency rule.
- Ata v. Schwartz (1976) refused to let a large employer buy its way out of a nuisance injunction and brought the Calabresi-Melamed framework into Israeli law; Tnuva v. Raabi (2011) shows how class actions restore incentives when individual harms are tiny.
- In your own dispute, use the economic questions to find a settlement both sides prefer to court.
