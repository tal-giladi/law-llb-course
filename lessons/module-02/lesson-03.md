# 02.3 · Briefing a ruling

By the end of this lesson you will be able to reduce a forty-page Israeli judgment to a one-page brief that tells you, and anyone you show it to, exactly what the court decided, why, and how far the decision reaches.

## Why it matters

Israeli Supreme Court judgments are long. Each justice may write separately; the reasoning wanders through comparative law and academic writing; the binding rule may be a single sentence on page 31. Law students learn to brief (לתמצת) rulings in their first semester because everything else depends on it: you cannot argue from a precedent you have not pinned down, and you cannot distinguish a ruling the other side cites unless you know its facts and its holding precisely.

For a self-represented person the brief is also a working document. When you write to an authority, file a claim or answer a defence, you will quote two or three paragraphs of a ruling. The brief tells you which paragraphs, whose opinion they come from, and whether they bind.

## The law

### What binds

> (ב) הלכה שנפסקה בבית המשפט העליון מחייבת כל בית משפט, זולת בית המשפט העליון.

*Gist:* What binds lower courts is the **halacha**, the rule the Supreme Court laid down, not everything it wrote. ([Basic Law: The Judiciary, s. 20(ב)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה))

The Basic Law does not define halacha. In practice (lesson 01.5), the holding is the legal rule necessary to decide the case on its facts, as stated or adopted by the majority. Remarks about questions the court did not need to decide are obiter: they can be highly persuasive, especially from a large bench, but they do not bind.

### Who wrote what

A Supreme Court bench usually has three justices; important cases are heard by five, seven, nine or more. Each justice may write, or simply write "אני מסכים" / "אני מסכימה" (I agree). The judgment ends with a result line, for example "הוחלט כאמור בפסק דינו של השופט ע' פוגלמן" (decided as stated in Justice Fogelman's opinion). When the opinions differ, the result line says which view prevailed ("הוחלט ברוב דעות…").

## Leading cases

### עע"ם 1758/10 לשכת עורכי הדין בישראל נ' שגיא (15.8.2011)

A three-justice bench, one written opinion and two concurrences: the simplest structure, briefed in full in the worked example below. Free text: [Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פסק_דין_עע%22ם_1758/10_לשכת_עורכי_הדין_בישראל_נ'_שלום_שגיא).

### בג"ץ 9232/01 "נח" – ההתאחדות הישראלית של הארגונים להגנת בעלי חיים נ' היועץ המשפטי לממשלה (11.8.2003)

The Court held that force-feeding geese for foie gras is "abuse" (התעללות) under s. 2(א) of the Cruelty to Animals (Protection of Animals) Law and annulled the regulations that permitted it. Justice Strasberg-Cohen wrote the lead opinion; Justice Rivlin concurred in the result with his own reasoning; Justice Grunis dissented, reading the statute in light of "agricultural needs".

Why it matters for briefing: two justices agreed on the result by partly different routes, and the third disagreed. Your brief must record the holding as the common ground of the majority, list the concurrence's separate reasoning, and keep the dissent in its own line, because the dissent's arguments return when the issue comes up again. English translation: [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/%E2%80%9Cnoah%E2%80%9D-israeli-federation-animal-protection-organizations-v-attorney-general).

### ע"א 165/82 קיבוץ חצור נ' פקיד שומה רחובות, פ"ד לט(2) 70 (1985)

Open the [free copy](https://www.efraty.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/khtsvr82.doc) and look at the first page. Before the words "פסק-דין, השופט א' ברק" come a list of the statutes cited, subject headings ("מיני-רציו"), a numbered summary beginning "בית המשפט העליון פסק", and lists of rulings cited. Those parts were written by the report's editors, not by the Court. They are a useful map, but a brief, a memo or a pleading quotes the judgment itself, by page or paragraph. Here item (5) of the summary, "לעולם יש לפתוח בלשון החוק, אך לעולם אין לסיים בה", happens to repeat a sentence from Barak's opinion word for word; that is the sentence to quote, from the opinion. Lesson 02.4 reads the opinion.

## How it works in practice

### The anatomy of an Israeli judgment

1. **Header (כותרת):** court and capacity ("בבית המשפט העליון בשבתו כבית משפט לערעורים בעניינים מנהליים"), case number, bench, parties and their roles (מערער/משיב, עותר/משיב), the decision appealed from with its date, hearing date, counsel.
2. **Opening:** one paragraph stating what the proceeding is about.
3. **Background (רקע עובדתי ודיוני):** facts and procedural history, usually numbered paragraphs.
4. **The parties' arguments (טענות הצדדים).**
5. **Discussion (דיון):** often split into the normative framework (המסגרת הנורמטיבית) and its application (מן הכלל אל הפרט).
6. **Conclusion and result**, including costs (הוצאות).
7. **Other justices' opinions.**
8. **Result line and date ("ניתן היום…").**

### The brief, section by section

| Heading | What to write | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Citation | Full citation (lesson 02.5) and link | 1 line |
| Bench | Justices; who wrote; who concurred; who dissented | 1 line |
| Procedural history | Court below, its result, who appealed | 2 lines |
| Facts | Only facts the decision turns on | 3 to 6 lines |
| Issue(s) | Each legal question as a yes/no question | 1 line each |
| Holding | The rule, in one or two sentences, phrased generally enough to apply to the next case | 2 lines |
| Reasoning | The steps that lead to the holding, with paragraph numbers | 5 to 10 lines |
| Concurrence / dissent | What differs and why | 2 lines each |
| Result | Allowed/dismissed, remedy, costs | 1 line |
| Obiter worth noting | Remarks beyond what was needed | optional |
| Why it matters for me | How it helps or hurts your matter; how it could be distinguished | 2 to 4 lines |

### Reading order that saves time

Read the header, the opening paragraph and the result line first. Then read the discussion of the main opinion. Read the facts only as far as you need them to understand the discussion. Read other opinions last, starting with any dissent. Mark the paragraph numbers you will cite.

### Phrasing the holding

A holding is too narrow if it only restates the outcome ("the Bar may refuse Sagi"); too wide if it claims more than the court decided ("the Bar may refuse anyone under investigation"). Aim for the rule as the court framed it, with the conditions that mattered: "Findings of fact in a final civil judgment are admissible administrative evidence under s. 44(א)(2) of the Bar Association Law, and may, if weighty, justify deferring a candidate's admission until a related criminal investigation ends."

## Worked example

Brief of the Wikisource text of עע"ם 1758/10, prepared by Yehuda Klein, a bookkeeper in Bnei Brak who is preparing for a dispute with a professional licensing body about his own licence.

**Citation:** עע"ם 1758/10 לשכת עורכי הדין בישראל נ' שגיא (אר"ש 15.8.2011). [Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פסק_דין_עע%22ם_1758/10_לשכת_עורכי_הדין_בישראל_נ'_שלום_שגיא).

**Bench:** Justices Hayut, Melcer, Fogelman. Fogelman wrote; Hayut and Melcer agreed. Unanimous.

**Procedural history:** The Tel Aviv Administrative Affairs Court (עת"מ 2622-07, 3.2.2010) allowed the candidate's petition and ordered the Bar to admit him. The Bar appealed. During the appeal the Supreme Court sent the matter back to the Bar's Central Committee, which made a third decision (2.1.2011); the parties agreed the appeal would review that decision.

**Facts:** The candidate passed the Bar exams in 2007. A district court, in civil receivership proceedings, found that he had taken company money and falsified records, and ordered him to repay over ₪4 million. A police investigation into the same acts was open, with no indictment yet. The Bar deferred his admission until the criminal process ends.

**Issues:** (1) May the Bar rely on findings in a civil judgment as "other facts" under s. 44(א)(2) of the Bar Association Law? (2) Was deferring admission until the investigation ends reasonable and proportionate?

**Holding:** (1) Yes: civil findings by a competent court are admissible administrative evidence and may carry high weight; the standard of proof of criminal law does not apply to an administrative decision. (2) Yes: in these circumstances the deferral was proportionate, given that it was temporary and concerned initial admission rather than revocation.

**Reasoning** (paragraph numbers counted continuously through the judgment; the Wikisource page restarts its numbering under each heading): Evidence: the test for administrative evidence is what a reasonable authority would rely on, and the weight required grows with the harm to rights (para. 11). Language: s. 44(א)(2) speaks of "עובדות אחרות", in contrast to (א)(1), which requires a criminal conviction (para. 14). Purpose: protecting the integrity of the profession (paras. 12, 14). Weight: findings of serious, prolonged dishonesty by a court that heard the candidate are weighty evidence (paras. 15–16). Proportionality: rational connection, least harmful means, proportionality stricto sensu; the harm is reduced because the deferral is temporary and concerns a first licence rather than revocation (paras. 18–21).

**Result:** Appeal allowed; the Administrative Court's judgment set aside; the Bar's decision of 2.1.2011 stands. No costs.

**Obiter worth noting:** The quotation from בג"ץ 4495/99 on the values of the profession ("עורך-דין אינו אך טכנאי-משפט", para. 21) describes the values of the profession; it supports the purpose but is not the holding.

**Why it matters for me:** A licensing body may rely on findings from civil proceedings, but must give a hearing and must weigh the harm. Distinguishing points for Yehuda: his case concerns revocation of an existing licence (more severe), and there are no judicial findings against him.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One Supreme Court judgment from your lesson 02.2 list, or, if you have none, עע"ם 9135/03 on [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/council-higher-education-v-haaretz) (English) together with the Hebrew text from the Supreme Court decisions site.
- [The case brief template](../../templates/case-brief.md).
- 90 minutes.

### Steps

1. Read the header, the opening and the result line. Fill in citation, bench and result.
2. Write the procedural history in two lines.
3. Read the main opinion's discussion. Mark the paragraph where the rule is stated.
4. Write each issue as a yes/no question.
5. Write the holding in no more than two sentences. Test it: would it decide this case? Would it decide a slightly different one?
6. List the reasoning steps with paragraph numbers.
7. Read any other opinions. Record concurrences and dissents in one or two lines each.
8. Write the facts last, keeping only what the reasoning uses.
9. Write "Why it matters for me": how the ruling helps, how the other side could distinguish it.
10. Cut the brief to one page.

### How you know it worked

Someone who has not read the ruling can tell from your brief what the court decided, why, and whether it applies to a new set of facts you describe to them. Every reasoning point has a paragraph number. The brief fits on one page.

### Self-check

- [ ] My holding is a general rule, not just the outcome.
- [ ] I distinguished majority, concurrence and dissent.
- [ ] I did not quote the editors' headnote as the court's words.
- [ ] Every point in the reasoning has a paragraph or page reference.
- [ ] I wrote how the ruling could be distinguished.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Briefing rulings is study and preparation, open to everyone. It shows up in practice every time you cite a ruling in a letter, a statement of claim, a petition or an appeal: a self-represented party who quotes the right paragraph of the right opinion is taken seriously.

### Where and how

Use the sources from lesson 02.2. Keep your briefs in one folder per matter; you will reuse them.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your case turns on a ruling with conflicting opinions and you cannot tell which view prevailed.
- The other side argues that the ruling you rely on was obiter or has been narrowed, and the point is decisive.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quoting a dissent as if it were the court's ruling | The other side or the judge points it out; your argument collapses | Re-check the result line; quote the majority, mention the dissent separately | Record who wrote what before you read the reasoning |
| Stating the holding too broadly | You claim the ruling decides your case when it does not | Narrow the holding to the facts the court relied on | Test the holding against slightly different facts |
| Quoting the editor's headnote | The words are not the court's; a judge notices | Find the passage in the opinion and quote it with the page or paragraph | Quote only text that appears after "פסק-דין" |
| Briefing from a summary | Errors in the summary become your errors | Read the judgment itself | Use summaries only to find rulings |
| Omitting the facts the court relied on | You cannot distinguish or apply the ruling | Add the decisive facts | Ask: which facts does the reasoning mention? |

## Review

- A brief records citation, bench, procedural history, facts, issues, holding, reasoning, other opinions, result and why it matters to you, on one page.
- Only the holding binds (Basic Law: The Judiciary s. 20); obiter persuades; dissents do not bind but often return.
- Read the header, the opening and the result line first; then the main discussion; facts and other opinions last.
- Headnotes and summaries in printed reports are editors' work; quote the judgment itself.
- Phrase the holding as a rule that would decide the next case, not as the outcome.
