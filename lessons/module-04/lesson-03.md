# 04.3 · Causation, remoteness and damage

By the end of this lesson you will be able to test whether a careless act actually and legally caused a loss, list the heads of damage a court awards, and build a damages table in which every shekel has a document behind it.

## Why it matters

Proving that someone was careless is half a case. The other half is proving that their carelessness caused *your* loss, and how much that loss is. Many self-represented claims fail here: the plaintiff shows a hazard and an injury but not the link between them, or asks for a round number without receipts. Defendants and insurers fight hardest on causation and quantum, because that is where the money is.

Causation is also where tort law meets its deepest questions: what to do when science cannot say whether the doctor's delay caused the harm, how far liability should reach down a chain of events, and whether relatives who suffer because a loved one was injured can sue. The Supreme Court has given different answers over time, and you need to know where it stands now.

## The law

### Fault must be a cause

> ”אָשָם“ הוא מעשהו או מחדלו של אדם, שהם עוולה לפי פקודה זו, או שהם עוולה כשיש בצדם נזק, או שהם התרשלות שהזיקה לעצמו, ורואים אדם כמי שגרם לנזק בַּאֲשָמוֹ, אם היה האשם הסיבה או אחת הסיבות לנזק; אולם לא יראוהו כך אם נתקיימה אחת מאלה:
> (1) הנזק נגרם על ידי מקרה טבעי בלתי רגיל, שאדם סביר לא יכול היה לראותו מראש ואי אפשר היה למנוע תוצאותיו אף בזהירות סבירה;
> (2) אֲשָמוֹ של אדם אחר הוא שהיה הסיבה המכרעת לנזק;

*Gist:* A person caused damage by their fault if the fault was the cause or one of the causes, unless the damage was caused by an unusual natural event that a reasonable person could not foresee or prevent, or another person's fault was the decisive cause. ([Civil Wrongs Ordinance, s. 64](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הנזיקין#סעיף_64))

"One of the causes" is enough: you do not need to show the defendant was the only cause. Section 64(2) is the statutory home of the intervening-cause doctrine.

### Which consequences are compensated

> (1) סבל התובע נזק, יינתנו פיצויים רק בשל אותו נזק שעלול לבוא באורח טבעי במהלכם הרגיל של הדברים ושבא במישרין מעוולת הנתבע;
> (2) סבל התובע נזק ממון, לא יינתנו לו פיצויים בשל הנזק אלא אם מסר פרטים עליו בכתב התביעה או בצירוף לו.

*Gist:* Damages are given only for damage that may naturally arise in the ordinary course of things and came directly from the tort, and pecuniary damage is compensated only if its particulars are given in or with the statement of claim. ([s. 76](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הנזיקין#סעיף_76))

### Death, insurance and benefits

- The victim's claims survive for the estate (s. 19); a death caused by a tort gives the spouse, parents and children a claim for their actual financial loss of support, including funeral costs (ss. 78–80).
- Money you receive from **your own insurance** is not deducted from tort damages (s. 86): you paid for that cover.
- National Insurance work-injury benefits are deducted from damages owed **by the employer** (s. 82); in other cases National Insurance may itself claim from the wrongdoer (National Insurance Law, beyond this module).

## Leading cases

### דנ"א 4693/05 בית חולים כרמל חיפה נ' מלול (29.8.2010)

A baby was born with severe brain damage after a delay in delivery; experts could not say whether the delay caused the damage, and the chance that it did was well below 50%. In 2005 a panel had awarded damages in proportion to that chance. A further hearing of nine justices, by five to four, held that the general rule remains the balance of probabilities: if causation is more likely than not, the plaintiff recovers in full; if not, nothing. Proportional liability is allowed only in a narrow class of "recurring distortion" (הטיה חוזרת) cases, where the same defendant repeatedly creates a risk to an actual group of plaintiffs and the all-or-nothing rule would systematically let it escape. The minority (Justice Naor and others) would have allowed probability-based compensation for a single plaintiff with bodily injury where causation is inherently uncertain. Why it matters: for an individual plaintiff, you must show it is more likely than not that the fault caused your damage. English summary: [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/carmel-haifa-hospital-v-malul-summary).

### ע"א 140/00 עיזבון המנוח מיכאל אטינגר ז"ל נ' החברה לשיקום ופיתוח הרובע היהודי (15.3.2004)

A twelve-year-old boy died after falling into an unfenced pit at an archaeological site next to a playground in Jerusalem's Old City. Five justices held, departing from an earlier precedent, that a person whose life expectancy is shortened by a tort is entitled to compensation for the earnings of the "lost years", and that this claim passes to the estate when the victim dies, with a deduction for what dependants receive for the same years. Why it matters: it shows how courts translate a life lost into heads of damage, and that tort damages aim at full restoration of the loss, not punishment (the Court left open whether punitive damages are available). English text: [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ettinger-estate-v-jewish-quarter-company).

### ע"א 754/05 לוי נ' המרכז הרפואי שערי צדק (5.6.2007)

A foetus died in the womb after the hospital sent the mother away during labour. The Court applied the rule from רע"א 444/87 אלסוחה נ' עיזבון דהאן (1990): relatives in the first degree who suffer mental harm because a loved one was killed or badly injured may recover, but only for a severe and substantial mental reaction, not ordinary grief and distress. The majority treated the father as a secondary victim and still compensated him, using the flexibility Alsoucha allows in "clear and difficult" cases; Justice Hayut would have treated both parents as direct victims. Why it matters: emotional harm is compensable, but the plaintiff's relationship and the severity of the harm decide whether and how much. English text: [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/levy-v-shaare-zedek-medical-center).

## How it works in practice

### Testing causation in three steps

1. **But-for.** Without the defendant's fault, would the damage have happened anyway? If yes, no causation (a patient who would have died even with perfect treatment).
2. **Legal causation.** Courts use a combination of tests: was this kind of damage foreseeable (the foreseeability test); is it within the risk that made the conduct careless (the risk test); and does common sense see the fault as a cause? The extent of the damage need not be foreseeable once its kind was: a defendant takes the victim as he finds him ("the thin skull rule").
3. **Intervening causes.** A third party's later act, or the victim's own act, breaks the chain only if it is the decisive cause (s. 64(2)) and, in practice, was not itself foreseeable. A foreseeable intervention (a thief taking advantage of an unlocked door the defendant left open) usually does not break it.

### Heads of damage

| Head | What you prove | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Property damage | Cost of repair, or market value if repair is uneconomic | Two quotes or an assessor's report (שמאי), receipts, photos |
| Loss of use | Reasonable cost of a substitute (rental car, alternative lodging) | Receipts, rental agreement |
| Medical and travel expenses (past) | Each expense | Receipts, HMO statements |
| Lost earnings (past) | Income before and after, days missed | Payslips, employer letter, tax returns for the self-employed |
| Future loss of earning capacity | Disability and its effect on work | Medical expert opinion (essential), earnings history |
| Future expenses and care | Expected needs | Medical and care opinions |
| Pain and suffering | Nature and duration of injury, hospital days | Medical records; awarded at the court's discretion |
| Interest and linkage | From the date of loss | Calculated by the court |

Small property claims rarely need more than the first three rows. Personal-injury claims usually need medical evidence and are where you should think about a lawyer.

### Mitigation

A plaintiff who unreasonably lets damage grow (leaves a leak running for weeks, refuses a simple treatment) will not recover the avoidable part. Keep a short log of what you did to limit the damage.

## Worked example

Avi and Svetlana Kogan, retirees in Ashdod, return from a week away to find water dripping from their ceiling: the upstairs neighbour's washing-machine hose burst. The neighbour's plumber confirms the hose was old and cracked. Their damage:

| Item | Amount claimed | Evidence | Causation note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling and wall repainting | ₪4,200 | Two painter quotes (₪4,200 and ₪4,600) | Direct result of the leak |
| Damaged laminate floor in hallway | ₪6,800 | Flooring quote, photos | Direct; the neighbour argues the floor was old; the Kogans have the 2021 invoice |
| Mould treatment | ₪900 | Receipt | Natural consequence of water left for a week |
| Ruined rug | ₪1,500 | Purchase receipt from 2019 | Value reduced for age: they claim ₪900 |
| Hotel nights while floor was replaced | ₪1,200 | Hotel invoice | Reasonable loss of use |
| "Stress" | — | — | Not claimed: ordinary distress of this kind is not separately compensated |
| **Total** | **₪14,000** | | |

Causation: but for the cracked hose, no leak. Legal causation: water damage to the flat below is exactly the risk of an unmaintained hose. The neighbour's argument that the Kogans should have noticed earlier fails on the facts: they were away. Within days they shut the water and called the building committee, so they mitigated. The amount is below the small-claims ceiling; lesson 04.9 shows how to file it. They first check whether their own home-contents insurance pays (s. 86: insurance received is not deducted from damages, but their insurer may then claim from the neighbour).

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your own incident from lesson 04.2, or the supplied case there, or the Kogans' case above with your own numbers.
- A spreadsheet or a table on paper.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. List every loss, one per line, with the date it arose.
2. For each loss, write the but-for test in one sentence: "Without X, Y would not have happened because…".
3. For each loss, check legal causation: is it the kind of harm the defendant's carelessness risked? Mark anything remote.
4. Note any intervening act (your own, a third party's, nature) and whether it is decisive under s. 64.
5. Put an amount next to each remaining item, and next to it the document that proves it. Items without a document go in a separate "to obtain" list.
6. Note what you did to limit the damage.
7. Total the provable items. Compare with the small-claims ceiling in [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).

### Template

None yet; the table in the worked example is the format. Lesson 04.8 turns it into a demand letter.

### How you know it worked

- Every line has a causation sentence and a document, or is on the "to obtain" list.
- Remote or doubtful items are marked, not silently included.
- The total is a sum of items, not a round number.

### Self-check

- [ ] I applied but-for to each item separately.
- [ ] I removed ordinary distress that the law does not compensate.
- [ ] I reduced old items for age where honest.
- [ ] I recorded my mitigation steps.
- [ ] I know whether my own insurance covers part of the loss.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes, for property damage and expenses you can document. Personal-injury damages for permanent disability or future loss need medical expert opinions; you may file them yourself, but the risk of under-claiming is high.

### Where and how

- Property claims within the ceiling: small claims (lesson 04.9). Above it: magistrates' court.
- Bodily injury in a road accident: the special law (lesson 04.6), against the insurer.
- An assessor's report (שמאי) costs money; for small claims, two independent quotes are usually enough.
- Free help: [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Causation depends on medical science (delayed diagnosis, birth injury, a disease after exposure).
- You claim future loss of earnings or care.
- A relative died or was badly injured and you are considering a claim for your own mental harm.
- The defendant blames an intervening cause and has its own expert.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claiming a round figure with no breakdown | Pecuniary items rejected for lack of particulars (s. 76(2)) | Amend the claim with an itemised table and documents | Build the table before writing the claim |
| Including new-for-old replacement | The court reduces the award and doubts the rest | Concede a reduction for age | Claim fair value, not the cost of upgrading |
| Ignoring a gap in the causal chain | The defendant wins on "you would have been hurt anyway" | Get evidence on the but-for question (expert, records) | Write the but-for sentence for each item |
| Letting damage grow | The avoidable part is not compensated | Act now and document it | Keep a mitigation log from day one |
| Claiming distress as a separate head in a property case | Rejected, and it weakens credibility | Drop it | Claim only heads the law recognises |

## Review

- Fault must be "the cause or one of the causes" (s. 64); an unusual natural event or another's decisive fault breaks the link.
- Factual causation is the but-for test; legal causation asks whether the harm is of a kind within the risk and foreseeable.
- For a single plaintiff, causation is all-or-nothing on the balance of probabilities; proportional liability is an exception for recurring distortion (Malul).
- Damages restore the loss: itemise pecuniary damage with documents (s. 76(2)); your own insurance is not deducted (s. 86).
- First-degree relatives may recover for severe mental harm under Alsoucha, not for ordinary grief (Levy v. Shaare Zedek).
