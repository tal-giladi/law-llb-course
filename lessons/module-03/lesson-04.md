# 03.4 · Good faith in negotiation and performance (ss. 12 and 39)

By the end of this lesson you will be able to recognise the conduct Israeli courts treat as bad faith before and after a contract is signed, state what the injured party can recover, and use good faith in your own letters without overusing it. Good faith is the most powerful and most quoted principle in Israeli private law; it is also the argument judges hear most often from people who have nothing better.

## Why it matters

Israeli law imposes a duty of good faith at two moments: while negotiating (s. 12) and while performing a contract or using a right under it (s. 39). Section 61(ב) spreads both across all of private law. In practice this means that a landlord who refuses consent to a reasonable sublet only to extract a payment, a seller who hides a known defect during negotiations, or a contractor who uses a technical clause to escape a deal that turned out less profitable can all be held to account even if the contract's words are on their side.

Many common-law systems have no general duty of good faith in negotiation. Israel does, and the Supreme Court has given it real teeth, including, in some cases, damages for the profit lost because the contract was never made.

## The law

### Good faith in negotiation

> (א) במשא ומתן לקראת כריתתו של חוזה חייב אדם לנהוג בדרך מקובלת ובתום לב.
> (ב) צד שלא נהג בדרך מקובלת ולא בתום־לב חייב לצד השני פיצויים בעד הנזק שנגרם לו עקב המשא ומתן או עקב כריתת החוזה, והוראות סעיפים 10, 13 ו־14 לחוק החוזים (תרופות בשל הפרת חוזה), תשל״א–1970, יחולו בשינויים המחוייבים.

*Gist:* In negotiations a person must act in a customary manner and in good faith; one who does not must compensate the other side for damage caused by the negotiation or by making the contract, applying the Remedies Law's rules on foreseeable damage, non-pecuniary damage and mitigation. [s. 12](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_%28חלק_כללי%29#סעיף_12)

### Good faith in performance and in using rights

> בקיום של חיוב הנובע מחוזה יש לנהוג בדרך מקובלת ובתום לב; והוא הדין לגבי השימוש בזכות הנובעת מחוזה.

*Gist:* A contractual obligation must be performed, and a contractual right exercised, in a customary manner and in good faith. [s. 39](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_%28חלק_כללי%29#סעיף_39)

Breach of s. 39 is a breach of contract, so the Remedies Law applies in full (enforcement, cancellation, damages). Breach of s. 12 gives damages only, because there may be no contract to enforce.

### Two connected sections

- **Deceit includes non-disclosure** of facts that law, custom or the circumstances required you to disclose (s. 15, lesson 03.5). The duty to disclose during negotiation is the meeting point of ss. 12 and 15.
- **A good-faith warning is not duress:** "אזהרה בתום לב על הפעלתה של זכות אינה בגדר איום" (s. 17(ב)). Threatening to sue in good faith is legitimate pressure.

## Leading cases

### ע״א 6370/00 קל בנין בע״מ נ׳ ע.ר.מ. רעננה לבניה והשכרה בע״מ (2002)

A developer invited ten contractors to a private tender, negotiated with the appellant to agreement on all terms subject to board approval, and then signed with a contractor who had never bid. In the first round the Supreme Court found bad faith in negotiations (s. 12) though no contract had been formed. In this second round the question was the measure of damages. The court held that a party who negotiates in bad faith may, where the contract would otherwise have been concluded, be liable for expectation damages (the lost profit), not only reliance damages (wasted costs). President Barak called ss. 12 and 39 and their extension in s. 61(ב) fundamental provisions of the legal system; Justice Englard examined the question also through Jewish law.

Why it matters: it sets the ceiling for s. 12 damages. In most private disputes reliance damages are the realistic claim; expectation damages require showing that, but for the bad faith, you would have had the contract. [English translation (portions)](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/219)

### רע״א 6339/97 רוקר נ׳ סלומון (1999)

Neighbours in a small condominium built into the common property without consent. The question before a seven-justice panel was whether a court may refuse to order demolition because insisting on it would be an abuse of the other owners' right. The majority upheld the order to restore the common property. Justice Englard, dissenting, would have refused demolition as an abuse of right (Land Law s. 14), drawing on the Jewish-law rule "כופין על מידת סדום" (one may be compelled not to act meanly).

Why it matters: the abuse-of-right idea that s. 39 expresses for contracts runs through all of private law, but the majority shows its limits: a person who insists on a clear right against someone who acted unlawfully is not acting in bad faith merely because the result is harsh. Courts intervene in the extreme case, not whenever a right is used firmly. [English translation (Englard's dissent and part of Turkel's reply)](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/316)

## How it works in practice

### What courts have treated as bad faith in negotiation

- Negotiating with no real intention of contracting (for example to learn the other side's prices).
- Breaking off at the last minute for an improper reason after leading the other side to rely on the deal.
- Hiding or misrepresenting material facts that the other side could not easily discover.
- Running a tender and then ignoring its own rules.

Breaking off negotiations is not bad faith by itself. People may walk away; the question is how and why.

### What courts have treated as bad faith in performance

- Using a technical clause to escape a deal for a reason unrelated to the clause's purpose.
- Refusing consent where the contract requires consent "not to be unreasonably withheld", or using a veto only to extract a payment.
- Insisting on literal wording in a way that defeats the evident purpose of the contract.
- Cancelling for a trivial breach without giving the chance to cure, where the circumstances call for it.

### The debate

Supporters of a strong good-faith principle argue it lets contract law reflect the values of fairness and cooperation and protects weaker parties. Critics, including many business lawyers, argue it creates uncertainty: parties cannot know in advance which clause a court will soften. The 2026 amendment to s. 25 (lesson 03.7) reflects part of that critique for interpretation of business contracts; it did not change ss. 12 or 39.

### Using it in your own letters

Good faith is strongest as a second argument. Lead with the specific right (the lease clause, the statute), then add: "Moreover, your conduct is contrary to the duty to act in a customary manner and in good faith (s. 39 of the Contracts (General Part) Law)", with two or three facts that show why.

## Worked example

Amir Saleh, a software developer from Nazareth, rents a flat in Haifa. His lease allows subletting "with the landlord's consent, which shall not be withheld unreasonably". Amir gets a six-month job abroad and proposes a subtenant: a doctor at Rambam hospital with payslips and references. The landlord, Ora, replies that she will consent only if Amir pays her ₪4,000 "for the trouble".

Step by step:

1. **The right.** Ora has a contractual right to consent. Its use is governed by the clause itself ("not unreasonably") and by s. 39.
2. **The facts.** The subtenant is fully suitable; Ora's only stated reason is a payment the contract does not provide for.
3. **Analysis.** Using a consent right to extract a payment unrelated to its purpose (protecting the property and rent) is a classic bad-faith use of a right. It also breaches the express "not unreasonably" term.
4. **Remedy.** Amir may treat the refusal as a breach: ask for consent within a set time, and if she still refuses, sublet and defend his position, or claim damages for the rent he lost. He should weigh the risk: if a court disagrees, he will have breached the lease.
5. **Letter.** Amir writes: the subtenant's details, the clause, s. 39, a request for written consent within 7 days, and a statement that he reserves all rights.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One situation from your life where someone used a contractual right or negotiated in a way you felt was unfair. Or use Amir's case.
- 55 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write the facts in date order, separating what was said in writing from what was said orally.
2. Decide which stage you are at: negotiation (s. 12) or performance and use of a right (s. 39).
3. Name the right or obligation involved and its purpose.
4. List the facts that show bad faith (purpose unrelated to the right, hidden facts, last-minute reversal after reliance).
5. List the facts that cut the other way (a legitimate interest, your own conduct).
6. Decide the remedy: for s. 12, reliance damages (list your costs) or, rarely, expectation damages; for s. 39, the Remedies Law options.
7. Draft a short paragraph for a letter that states the right, the facts and the section, and asks for a specific act by a specific date.

### Template

None. Lesson 03.10 provides the notice-of-breach template; this paragraph will go into it.

### How you know it worked

- You chose s. 12 or s. 39 correctly for the stage.
- You can say what legitimate purpose the right serves and why its use here departs from it.
- Your paragraph asks for something concrete and does not rely on "good faith" alone.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes, in your own disputes: letters, small claims and magistrates' court claims that rely on ss. 12 and 39.

### Where and how

- Most good-faith disputes between individuals or with small businesses fit the small claims court (see [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md) for the ceiling); the claim must state the facts and the damage.
- Keep the evidence that shows purpose: the other side's messages giving reasons, the timeline of reliance (expenses, other offers you turned down).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- You are claiming expectation damages for a contract that was never made: proof of lost profit is technical.
- The dispute concerns a tender, a business acquisition or land.
- The other side relies on a long negotiated contract with "entire agreement" and limitation clauses.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Building the whole claim on "bad faith" | The judge sees no concrete right or damage | Add the specific clause or statute and quantify the loss | Lead with the specific right; use good faith second |
| Claiming lost profit for every broken-off negotiation | Claim rejected or reduced to reliance costs | Claim documented wasted costs | Ask: would the contract certainly have been made? |
| Treating any firm use of a right as abuse | Weak claim (Roker) | Show an improper purpose or disproportion | Identify the purpose of the right first |
| Hiding a known defect when selling, thinking "buyer beware" | Liability under s. 12 and cancellation for deceit (s. 15) | Disclose and renegotiate | Disclose material facts the buyer cannot easily find |
| Not keeping proof of reliance | Damages cannot be proved | Reconstruct from bank records and messages | Keep receipts and dates from the start |

## Review

- Section 12 requires good faith in negotiations; breach gives damages, usually reliance damages.
- Kal Binyan: where the contract would otherwise have been made, expectation damages may be awarded.
- Section 39 requires good faith in performing obligations and using contractual rights; breach is a breach of contract.
- Section 61(ב) extends both beyond contracts; Roker shows the limits: insisting on a clear right is not abuse by itself.
- Good faith is a powerful supporting argument, not a substitute for a concrete right and proven damage.
