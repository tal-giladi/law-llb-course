# 21.2 · Finding and reading the literature with free Israeli journals

By the end of this lesson you will know where Israeli legal scholarship is published free online, how to search it, how to grow ten good sources into a complete picture of the debate by following footnotes, and how to record what you read so that you can cite it accurately three weeks later.

## Why it matters

Lesson [02.2](../module-02/lesson-02.md) taught you to find rulings and lesson [02.1](../module-02/lesson-01.md) statutes. A paper also needs the literature: what scholars have already said about your question. Without it you cannot show what your paper adds, you will miss the best counter-arguments, and you risk "discovering" a point someone published ten years ago. Supervisors read the footnotes first; a paper whose footnotes cite only rulings and Wikipedia signals that the literature was never read.

The good news is that most Israeli law journals now publish their articles free online. A student with no library card can build a serious bibliography. Access was checked for each source below on 2026-10-17.

## The law

### Copying articles for your own research is a fair-use purpose (Copyright Law s. 19(a))

> שימוש הוגן ביצירה מותר למטרות כגון אלה: לימוד עצמי, מחקר, ביקורת, סקירה, דיווח עיתונאי, הבאת מובאות, או הוראה ובחינה על ידי מוסד חינוך.

*Gist:* Fair use of a work is permitted for purposes such as self-study, research, criticism, review, journalistic reporting, quotation, or teaching and examination by an educational institution. ([Copyright Law 2007 s. 19(a)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_זכות_יוצרים))

Downloading an article and keeping a copy for your own research falls within the listed purposes. Whether a particular use is fair still depends on the four factors in s. 19(b) (lesson [21.5](lesson-05.md)). Posting a full journal PDF to a class group or a public site is a different use and is not covered by this lesson.

### Journals' own terms

The journals add their own conditions. Tel Aviv University's עיוני משפט states on its volumes page that access to articles and use of the content are permitted for teaching, academic and research purposes only, and that commercial use is prohibited ([TAU Law Review, all volumes](https://www.taulawreview.sites.tau.ac.il/issues)). The Israel Law Review's recent articles carry Creative Commons licences, which tell you exactly what you may do with them.

## Leading cases

No ruling governs literature searches, so this section uses a model paper.

### Niva Elkin-Koren & Neil Weinstock Netanel, "Transplanting Fair Use Across the Globe: A Case Study Testing the Credibility of U.S. Opposition", 72 Hastings L.J. 1121 (2021)

The two authors, from Tel Aviv University and UCLA, reviewed all reported Israeli fair-use rulings from the first decade of the 2007 law (May 2008 to May 2018) and compared the outcomes with American practice. The paper is a model of using the literature: it starts by stating the opposing view (that fair use cannot be exported outside the United States), shows who holds it, and then answers it with data. Its footnotes are also a map of the Israeli field: they lead you to the Supreme Court's fair-use rulings (Premier League 2012, Safecom 2013, Nestlé 2019) and to the Hebrew literature. Why it matters: when your question has an English-language literature, an article like this is the fastest entry point, and its footnotes save you days. [Free PDF](https://www.uclawjournal.org/wp-content/uploads/72.4-NetanelElkin-Koren.pdf)

### A search routine that works

1. **Start with the leading ruling.** Read its paragraphs on your issue; judges often cite the main articles ("ראו: …"). Write them down.
2. **Search the journal sites** in Hebrew, with two or three key terms ("שימוש הוגן" ציטוט). Site search is often weak, so also use Google with the site restricted: `site:law.haifa.ac.il "שימוש הוגן"`.
3. **Google Scholar.** Search Hebrew and English terms separately. Use "Cited by" to find newer work that cites a key article (forward search), and "All versions" for a free copy.
4. **Snowball backwards.** The footnotes of the newest good article lead to the older ones. Stop when new sources keep citing ones you already have: you have reached saturation.
5. **Look for debates.** Response articles (מאמרי תגובה) show the disagreement in its sharpest form. For example, משפטים volume נז lists a response by Noam Sohlberg and Omri Goldwin to an article by Harel, Enoch and Medina on constitutional rights and certainty: reading both sides of an exchange like that gives you the strongest version of each position, whichever you end up holding.
6. **Record as you go** (next section). Never rely on memory for a pinpoint.

```text
site:lawjournal.huji.ac.il "תום לב" משא ומתן
site:law.haifa.ac.il "מאסר על תנאי"
"fair use" Israel "section 19"
```

### Reading an article efficiently

- **First pass (10 minutes):** abstract, introduction (the roadmap tells you the claim), section headings, conclusion. Decide: core, background or irrelevant.
- **Second pass (core sources only):** read the part relevant to your question, mark the author's thesis, the method and the best supporting argument, and the counter-arguments the author addresses.
- **Footnote pass:** scan for sources you do not have.

### The literature map

For each source, one row:

| Source (full citation) | Type | Claim | Method | Pinpoint for my paper | Agrees or disagrees with my thesis | Accessed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Record the page or paragraph of every passage you might quote, and the date you opened any web source. Published articles do the same: the two Haifa articles in lesson 21.1 both open with a note that all websites cited were last viewed on a stated date.

### How authoritative is a source?

- **Strongest:** statutes and Supreme Court rulings (the law itself); articles in the faculty journals above; books by recognised scholars.
- **Useful, with care:** online forums and short notes (often the newest thinking, less reviewed); SSRN drafts (cite the final version if it exists); Knesset Research and Information Center papers (good facts, no legal authority).
- **Background only:** newspaper reports, blogs, law-firm updates, Wikipedia, AI tool answers. Use them to find primary sources; never cite them for a legal proposition.

## Worked example

Dmitri Volkov, a student from Ashdod, has the question: "Is the purpose test in Copyright Law s. 19(a) an independent precondition for fair use?"

1. **Leading ruling first.** He searches for Israeli Supreme Court fair-use rulings and finds an English translation of ע"א 7996/11 Safecom on the Cardozo Versa site, which says s. 19(a) is an open list (lesson [21.5](lesson-05.md)).
2. **Google Scholar.** "fair use" Israel "section 19" brings up Elkin-Koren and Netanel (2021). Its footnotes list the Nestlé ruling (2019), which, the authors report, held that the purpose test remains an independent preliminary test, and the 2012 Premier League ruling.
3. **Hebrew journals.** `site:lawjournal.huji.ac.il "שימוש הוגן"` and the same on the Haifa and TAU sites give him three Hebrew articles.
4. **Gap.** Elkin-Koren and Netanel found that only a minority of rulings in their study acknowledged that the list is open. That tension between the statute's open wording and lower-court practice is Dmitri's opening.
5. **Map.** Nine sources: the statute, three Supreme Court rulings, one English article, three Hebrew articles, one Knesset committee protocol cited by the English article. Each row records the pinpoint and access date.
6. **What he did not use.** A law-firm blog post summarising Nestlé: he used it only to find the case number, then searched for the ruling itself.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your research question from lesson 21.1.
- A spreadsheet or the literature-map table in the [proseminar paper outline](../../templates/proseminar-paper-outline.md), part B.
- 75 minutes.

### Steps

1. Find the leading ruling on your question and list every academic source it cites (15 minutes).
2. Search two journal sites from the table and Google Scholar, in Hebrew and English (20 minutes).
3. Pick the newest relevant article and snowball its footnotes backwards (10 minutes).
4. Use "Cited by" on the most cited source to find newer work (5 minutes).
5. Fill in at least eight rows of the literature map, with pinpoints and access dates (20 minutes).
6. Mark each source core, background or irrelevant, and write one sentence on where the literature disagrees (5 minutes).

### Template

[Proseminar paper outline](../../templates/proseminar-paper-outline.md), part B (literature map).

### How you know it worked

- You have at least two Israeli journal articles, one primary source of each kind you need (statute, ruling) and at least one source that disagrees with your provisional thesis.
- New sources you find mostly cite sources already on your map.
- Every row has a pinpoint and, for web sources, an access date.

### Self-check

- [ ] I searched in both Hebrew and English.
- [ ] I followed footnotes backwards and "cited by" forwards.
- [ ] I have the strongest source against my thesis.
- [ ] No legal proposition in my map rests only on a blog, news report or AI answer.
- [ ] I saved a copy of every PDF I rely on, for my own research only.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Finding the literature is a research skill. In your own court case it matters less than finding the law, but it helps in two situations: when the case law is unsettled, an article often collects all the rulings on both sides in one place; and Israeli courts do cite scholarship, so a well-chosen article can support an argument on how a statute should be read.

### Where and how

Use the same journal sites. In a pleading, cite the article in full (lesson [21.4](lesson-04.md)) and give the page. Because a judge may not have the article to hand, it is good practice to quote the short passage you rely on rather than only refer to it.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

Your argument depends on an academic view that the courts have not yet accepted. Persuading a court to adopt a new reading is possible, but it is a high-risk strategy for a self-represented party when much is at stake.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Searching only in English, or only in Hebrew | Half the literature missed | Repeat the search in the other language | Make two search lists from the start |
| Citing a blog or news report for a legal proposition | Weak authority; supervisor marks it down | Find and cite the primary source the blog relied on | Use secondary web sources only as leads |
| No pinpoints recorded | Hours lost re-finding quotes; risk of misquoting | Re-open the PDFs and add pages | Fill the map as you read |
| Missing the leading opposing article | Paper looks one-sided | Search "cited by" and response articles | Ask: who would disagree with me, and where did they publish? |
| Sharing journal PDFs publicly | Possible infringement; breach of journal terms | Remove; share links instead | Keep copies for your own research |

## Review

- Most Israeli law journals publish free online: משפטים, עיוני משפט, משפט וממשל, דין ודברים; the Israel Law Review has been open access since 2025.
- Start from the leading ruling, then journal sites, Google Scholar, footnotes backwards and "cited by" forwards.
- Read in passes: abstract, introduction and conclusion first; detail only for core sources.
- Record every source's claim, method, pinpoint and access date in a literature map.
- Primary sources and journal articles carry an argument; blogs, news and AI answers are leads only.
