# 36.5 · Presenting and defending your paper

By the end of this lesson you will be able to present your seminar paper to a group in under fifteen minutes, take hard questions without either collapsing or digging in, turn every comment into a decision in a response memo, and give a useful critique of a classmate's paper. These are the same skills a party uses at an oral hearing.

## Why it matters

Most seminars include a presentation of work in progress, and the grade may reflect it. More importantly, the presentation is the cheapest peer review you will ever get: twenty informed readers hear your argument before it is final. Students waste it in two ways. Some read twenty minutes of text and leave no time for questions; others treat every question as an attack and defend positions they should have dropped. A presentation is not a defence of the finished paper; it is a test of the argument while you can still change it.

## The law

### The presentation can count (TAU faculty regulations s. 84(א))

> ציון הסמינר ייקבע בהתאם לעבודה הסמינריונית בכתב, והוא יכול לשקף גם את רמת הצגתה בעל פה של העבודה ותרומתו הפעילה של התלמיד לדיונים במהלך הסמינר.

*Gist:* The seminar grade is set by the written paper but may also reflect the oral presentation and the student's active contribution to the discussions. ([TAU Faculty of Law study regulations 2016–17, s. 84(א)](https://yedion-law.tau.ac.il/sites/law.tau.ac.il/files/media_server/law_heb/yedion2016-17/takanon2016-7.pdf)) Check your syllabus for the actual weight.

### Deadlines after the presentation (s. 83(ז))

> המרצה רשאי לאשר הגשת עבודה סמינריונית באיחור של עד שבועיים מהמועד הנקוב בס"ק ד'. הגשה באיחור כאמור תלווה באישור בכתב מהמרצה.

*Gist:* The lecturer may approve submission up to two weeks after the set date, with written approval; the same section lets the faculty officer add two more weeks for a student who shows sufficient cause, and says the faculty has no power to extend further. (Same source, s. 83(ז).) Comments at the presentation often mean more work. Plan the revision inside the deadline, and if you need an extension, ask in writing and early. These are the 2016–17 rules; your faculty's current rules prevail.

## Leading cases

No ruling governs seminar presentations. The models are published exchanges, where scholars respond to each other in writing.

### Response articles in משפטים

The Hebrew University law journal publishes response articles (מאמרי תגובה) next to the article they answer; lesson [21.2](../module-21/lesson-02.md) points to the exchange in volume נז between Noam Sohlberg and Omri Goldwin and the authors Harel, Enoch and Medina on constitutional rights and certainty. Why it matters: a good response restates the other side's argument fairly, identifies the precise step it disputes, and offers a reason or a source; a good reply concedes what must be conceded and defends the core. That is the shape of every answer you give in a seminar. [Volumes of משפטים](https://lawjournal.huji.ac.il/page/203)

### אסתר חיות "התובענה הייצוגית ככלי לאכיפה אזרחית-ציבורית" משפט ועסקים יט 935 (2016)

Written up from a lecture given at a 2014 conference on an empirical study of class actions, in response to that study's findings. Why it matters: it shows how an informed respondent engages with someone else's research: accept the findings, then ask what follows from them for doctrine and legislation. A discussant who only says "very interesting" has not done the job. [Free PDF](https://law.tau.ac.il/sites/law.tau.ac.il/files/media_server/law_heb/Tovanot/Articles/1479313050s50Us.pdf)

## How it works in practice

### A 12–15 minute presentation

| Minutes | Content | Slides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The hook: a case, a number or a puzzle | 1 |
| 1 | The question and the thesis, in one sentence each | 1 |
| 1–2 | The contribution: the gap sentence (lesson [36.1](lesson-01.md)) | 1 |
| 2 | Method and sources, with one honest limit | 1 |
| 5–6 | Two or three findings or steps of the argument, each with one piece of evidence | 3 |
| 1–2 | The strongest objection and your current answer | 1 |
| 1 | What you want help with: one or two specific open questions | 1 |

Rules: speak from notes, not a script; show quotations and data, not paragraphs of your own text; name the source on the slide; end on time. Ending with an open question invites the comments you actually need.

### Taking questions

Use a three-part answer: **restate** the question in one sentence (it shows you understood it and gives you time), **answer** directly, and **close** with what you will do. Four kinds of answer are available, and the best presenters use all of them:

- **Concede:** "That's right; the data cannot show settlements outside court. I'll say so in the limits section."
- **Cite:** "The protocol of the subcommittee's session in November 2005 addresses exactly that; I'll quote it."
- **Distinguish:** "That ruling was about a different item of the Second Schedule, so it doesn't decide my point."
- **Plan:** "I hadn't considered that. I'll check whether the amendment changed it and come back to you."

Write down every comment as it is made, with the name of the person who made it. Do not decide anything in the room.

### Being the discussant

If you are assigned to respond to a classmate's paper: read it twice; use the ten questions of the peer-review checklist, part 3 ([paper review checklist](../../templates/paper-review-checklist.md)); speak for five minutes at most; start with what works; then raise the one or two points that would most improve the paper, each with a reason or source; end with a question the author can answer. Do not rewrite their paper aloud.

### The response memo

Within two days, list every comment and decide:

| # | Comment (who) | Decision | What I will change, or why not | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ____ | Accept / Accept in part / Reject | ____ | ____ |

Rejecting a comment is fine if you can give a reason in one sentence. Comments that several people made, or that go to the thesis, come first in the revision plan. Send the memo to the supervisor if the seminar expects it.

## Worked example

Yair Ben-David, a kibbutz member from the Galilee, presents his paper on the access-to-court purpose of the Class Actions Law.

1. **Presentation.** 13 minutes, 9 slides; the open question at the end: "Can a settlement that pays a fund instead of class members serve access to court?"
2. **Hard question 1 (from the supervisor):** "Your coded applications are all against two operators. Isn't that a biased sample?" He restates it, concedes ("yes, the population is two companies, by design"), and plans: "I'll retitle the chapter and say the findings are about mobile operators only."
3. **Hard question 2 (from a classmate):** "Tnuva treats awards to the public as legitimate. Doesn't that undercut your thesis?" He distinguishes: Tnuva concerns relief where members cannot be identified (lesson [33.1](../module-33/lesson-01.md)), while his question is whether members get access at all; he promises to address it in the counter-arguments chapter.
4. **Discussant's point:** the method section does not explain how he searched the protocols. He accepts and adds his search log as an appendix.
5. **Response memo.** Nine comments: five accepted, two accepted in part, two rejected with reasons (one asked him to add American law, outside his question). He asks the lecturer in writing for a one-week extension, which the rules allow with written approval, and gets it.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your chapter plan and argument map from lesson 36.4, or a draft.
- Slide software or paper.
- A listener: a study partner, family member or a recording of yourself.
- The [paper review checklist](../../templates/paper-review-checklist.md), part 3.
- 75 minutes.

### Steps

1. Build the presentation from the table above: at most 9 slides (25 minutes).
2. Write the five hardest questions you expect, and for each choose concede, cite, distinguish or plan, with one sentence (15 minutes).
3. Give the talk to your listener or record it; time it (15 minutes).
4. Ask your listener for two comments using questions 4, 5 and 6 of the peer-review checklist, or answer them yourself after watching the recording (10 minutes).
5. Write the response memo for those comments plus your own five questions (10 minutes).

### Template

[Paper review checklist](../../templates/paper-review-checklist.md), part 3 (for the discussant); the response-memo table above.

### How you know it worked

- You finished within 15 minutes with time left for questions.
- A listener can state your question, thesis and contribution after hearing the talk once.
- Each of your five hard questions has an answer of one of the four kinds.
- Every comment in your memo has a decision and, if rejected, a reason.

### Self-check

- [ ] My last slide asks for help on a specific open question.
- [ ] I conceded at least one point in practice or in my prepared answers.
- [ ] I wrote down comments instead of arguing with them in the room.
- [ ] My revision plan fits inside the submission deadline.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes, in the sense that matters: any party may present their own case orally in an Israeli court (lesson [01.1](../module-01/lesson-01.md) and [can I act without a lawyer](../../references/can-i-act-without-a-lawyer.md)). Not a filing lesson, but the presentation skills are the same ones a self-represented party needs at a hearing.

### Where and how

At a hearing in your own case, the judge's questions play the role of seminar comments. Prepare a short opening (the question in dispute, your answer, your two best points), list the questions the judge is likely to ask, and choose your answer type in advance: concede what is clearly against you, cite the document or exhibit, distinguish the other side's ruling, or ask for time to respond in writing if the point is new. Bring the [hearing-day kit](../../templates/hearing-day-kit.md), and see lesson [15.7](../module-15/lesson-07.md) on the trial and summations.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

The hearing involves cross-examining expert witnesses, the other side is represented and raises procedural objections you cannot answer, or the judge suggests you get legal advice.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading a script for 20 minutes | No time for comments; the audience stops listening | Cut to the table's structure; speak from notes | Rehearse with a timer |
| Defending every word | The useful comments are lost; you look unsure of the core | Concede the small points, defend the thesis | Prepare concede-cite-distinguish-plan answers |
| Deciding in the room | Promises you cannot keep, or rash rejections | Write everything down; decide in the memo | Bring a notebook; say "I'll consider that" |
| Discussant who summarises the paper | No value to the author | Give one or two points with reasons | Use the peer-review questions |
| Revision that misses the deadline | Late submission needing approval, or a penalty | Ask for an extension in writing, early | Plan revision time after the presentation |

## Review

- A seminar presentation tests the argument while it can still change; the grade may reflect it (TAU s. 84(א)).
- Structure the talk around the question, the contribution, two or three findings, the strongest objection and an open question.
- Answer by restating, then conceding, citing, distinguishing or planning.
- Turn comments into a response memo with a decision and reason for each.
- As a discussant, start with what works and raise one or two points with reasons; the same skills serve you at a hearing in your own case.
