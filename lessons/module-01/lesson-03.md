# 01.3 · Sources of law and the hierarchy of norms

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take any rule that affects you, from a municipal parking bylaw to a Basic Law, place it in the Israeli hierarchy of norms, find the higher norm it depends on, and say what happens when two rules collide. That is the first step in every legal argument against an authority: a lower rule that exceeds the higher rule authorising it can be challenged.

## Why it matters

When a municipality fines you, a ministry refuses a permit or a regulator imposes a condition, it acts under a rule. That rule is valid only if a higher rule authorises it. A bylaw needs a statute that empowers the municipality; a regulation needs a statute that empowers the minister; a statute that limits a protected right must meet the conditions of the Basic Law that protects it. Each link in the chain is a place where the authority can be wrong.

Self-represented people often argue "this is unfair". Courts rarely accept that alone. They do accept "this regulation goes beyond the section that authorises it" or "this directive cannot override the regulation". Knowing the hierarchy turns a complaint into a legal argument.

## The law

### What counts as "law": Interpretation Law 1981, s. 3

> "חוק" – חוק של הכנסת או פקודה;
> "חיקוק" – חוק או תקנה;
> "תקנה" – הוראה שניתנה מכוח חוק והיא בת־פעל תחיקתי.

*Gist:* A "law" is a Knesset statute or a Mandate ordinance; an "enactment" is a law or a regulation; a "regulation" is a provision of legislative effect issued under the authority of a law. [Full text, s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הפרשנות#סעיף_3)

> "דין" – כל אחד מאלה: (1) חיקוק; (2) דינים דתיים [...] כפי תקפם במדינה; (3)(א) אקט של הפרלמנט הבריטי או דבר המלך במועצתו [...] ודיני המשפט המקובל ועקרוני היושר של אנגליה, כפי תקפם במדינה; (ב) דינים עותומניים כפי תקפם במדינה;

*Gist:* "Law" in the wide sense (דין) includes enactments, religious law as in force in Israel, and the British and Ottoman law still in force. [s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הפרשנות#סעיף_3)

The same section also defines a "הוראת מינהל" as an instruction given under a law that is **not** of legislative effect: a licence, a permit, a notice. The difference matters. A regulation binds everyone; an administrative instruction or internal procedure binds the authority's staff and guides its discretion but does not bind the public in the same way.

### The limitation clause: Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, s. 8

> אין פוגעים בזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק ההולם את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל, שנועד לתכלית ראויה, ובמידה שאינה עולה על הנדרש או לפי חוק כאמור מכוח הסמכה מפורשת בו.

*Gist:* Rights under this Basic Law may be limited only by a statute (or under express authority in a statute) that fits the state's values, serves a proper purpose and goes no further than necessary. [Full text, s. 8](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_8)

> אין בחוק־יסוד זה כדי לפגוע בתקפו של דין שהיה קיים ערב תחילתו של חוק־היסוד.

*Gist:* Laws that existed before the Basic Law came into force (1992) remain valid. [s. 10](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_10)

Section 8 is the engine of constitutional review: it sets the test a later statute must pass. Section 10 protects older law from that test, although older law is still interpreted in the Basic Law's spirit.

### The hierarchy in one table

| Level | Examples | Valid if |
|---|---|---|
| Basic Laws | Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty; Basic Law: The Judiciary | Enacted by the Knesset as a Basic Law (and, per HCJ 5658/23, not an extreme abuse of constituent power) |
| Statutes (laws, ordinances) | Contracts (General Part) Law; Civil Wrongs Ordinance | Consistent with the Basic Laws, or protected by a "preservation of laws" clause |
| Regulations, orders, bylaws | Civil Procedure Regulations 2018; a city's parking bylaw | Authorised by a statute, within its scope, made by the right body by the right procedure |
| Administrative directives and procedures | AG guidelines; a ministry's internal procedure | Consistent with all of the above; bind the authority more than the public |
| Case law | A Supreme Court holding | Binds lower courts (lesson 01.5); interprets every level above |

Two further rules settle conflicts at the **same** level: the later norm prevails over the earlier one, and the specific norm prevails over the general one. Where the two point different ways, the court decides by interpretation, looking at purpose.

## Leading cases

### ע״א 6821/93 בנק המזרחי המאוחד בע״מ נ׳ מגדל כפר שיתופי, פ״ד מט(4) 221 (1995)

A nine-justice panel held that the Basic Laws enacted by the Knesset have constitutional status, superior to ordinary statutes, and that courts may declare void a statute that infringes a protected right without meeting the limitation clause. The statute challenged in the case (an amendment affecting debts of agricultural settlements) was in fact upheld as meeting the limitation clause.

Why it matters: this is the foundation of the modern hierarchy of norms. Before it, the Knesset's ordinary statutes were effectively supreme; after it, Basic Laws sit above them. The ruling was and remains contested: its critics argue that the Knesset never enacted the Basic Laws as a constitution and that this step should have been taken by the people's representatives in an explicit constitutional process. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/391)

### בג״ץ 3267/97 רובינשטיין נ׳ שר הביטחון, פ״ד נב(5) 481 (1998)

For decades the Minister of Defence deferred military service for yeshiva students under a general power in the Defence Service Law. The court held that a matter of such principle, a "primary arrangement", must be decided by the Knesset in a statute, not by a minister using a general administrative power. It gave the Knesset time to legislate.

Why it matters: it explains the line between statute and secondary legislation. A regulation or administrative decision may implement policy; it may not create the basic policy itself on a fundamental issue. When you challenge a regulation, ask whether it carries a decision only the Knesset may make. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/323)

### בג״ץ 5658/23 התנועה למען איכות השלטון בישראל נ׳ הכנסת (1.1.2024)

A fifteen-justice panel considered Amendment No. 3 to Basic Law: The Judiciary, which barred courts from reviewing the reasonableness of decisions of the Government, the Prime Minister and ministers. Twelve justices held that the court has authority, in exceptional and extreme cases, to review Basic Laws for abuse of the Knesset's constituent power. Eight held that this amendment was such a case and declared it void; seven would have upheld it or interpreted it narrowly.

Why it matters: it adds a level at the very top of the hierarchy, a limit on Basic Laws themselves. The strongest argument for the ruling is that a parliament with a bare majority could otherwise remove core democratic protections by calling an ordinary law a Basic Law. The strongest argument against is that no text authorises the court to review Basic Laws, so the court, whose own powers are set by a Basic Law, should not sit above the constitution-maker. The consolidated text of the Basic Law now shows the struck amendment with a note citing the ruling. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/273)

> [!NOTE]
> The relationship between the Knesset and the courts was the subject of intense political and legal dispute in 2023–2026. Module 6 presents the arguments in depth. This course states the law as it stands and each side's legal arguments, not a political verdict.

## How it works in practice

Use the chain test on any rule that is applied against you.

1. **Find the rule.** The decision letter, the fine or the form should name it. If not, ask the authority in writing which provision it relied on.
2. **Find its authority.** A regulation's opening words say under which section it was made ("בתוקף סמכותי לפי סעיף ... לחוק ..."). A municipal bylaw names the municipal law that empowers it. Open that section.
3. **Check scope.** Does the authorising section cover this subject, this person, this kind of sanction? A section allowing the minister to set "procedures" does not obviously allow a new fee or a new prohibition.
4. **Check procedure.** Some regulations need a Knesset committee's approval or publication in Reshumot to take effect. Wikisource and the Knesset database show the publication reference.
5. **Check the top.** If a statute itself limits a right protected by a Basic Law, the limitation clause applies. That argument belongs in the High Court of Justice or an administrative court, usually with a lawyer.
6. **Check directives.** An internal procedure may not add conditions the law does not set. Ask for the procedure in writing (Freedom of Information Law, module 7 and year 2) and compare it to the regulation.

## Worked example

Daniel Avraham, an Ultra-Orthodox father of five from Bnei Brak, receives a municipal parking fine for stopping in a loading zone outside his home at 23:00. The sign shows loading hours of 07:00–19:00. The fine cites a section of the city's parking bylaw.

1. **The rule.** The bylaw section on loading zones. Daniel reads it on the municipality's site and finds it prohibits parking in a loading zone "during the hours shown on the sign".
2. **Its authority.** The bylaw states it was made under the Municipalities Ordinance. That ordinance allows municipalities to regulate parking by bylaw, so the bylaw is valid in principle.
3. **Scope.** The bylaw makes the sign's hours part of the rule. At 23:00 the sign's hours do not apply.
4. **Conclusion.** Daniel's argument is not "this is unfair" but "under the bylaw's own section the prohibition applies only during the hours on the sign; at 23:00 there was no offence". He attaches a photo of the sign and requests cancellation (the request process for parking fines is covered in a later practice module).
5. **Hierarchy point.** If the municipality had instead relied on an internal enforcement procedure saying loading zones are "always" restricted, that procedure could not override the bylaw.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One rule that affected you in the last year: a fine, a fee, a refusal, a condition on a permit or a benefit.
- Wikisource or the Knesset database, and the authority's own website.
- 45 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write down the exact rule the authority relied on (section number and name).
2. Classify it: Basic Law, statute, regulation, bylaw, order, administrative directive.
3. If it is below statute level, find the statutory section that authorises it. Copy the authorising words.
4. Compare: does the authorising section cover this subject and this sanction? Write two sentences.
5. Check whether a Basic Law right is touched (property, dignity, liberty, occupation). Note it, but do not build your argument on it yet.
6. Write a one-paragraph "chain" summary: rule, authority, scope, conclusion.

### Template

None. A four-line chain (rule, authority, scope, conclusion) is enough.

### How you know it worked

- You found the actual text of the rule, not a summary from a news site.
- You can point to the section in the higher norm that authorises the lower one.
- Your conclusion is a legal sentence ("exceeds s. X", "within s. X"), not an opinion about fairness.

### Self-check

- [ ] I can define חיקוק, תקנה and הוראת מינהל from the Interpretation Law.
- [ ] I can state the four elements of the limitation clause.
- [ ] I can explain the "primary arrangements" rule from Rubinstein.
- [ ] I can give the strongest argument for and against reviewing Basic Laws.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Partly. You may raise a "beyond authority" argument yourself in any proceeding you are a party to: a request to cancel a fine, an objection to an administrative decision, a defence in a local affairs court, or an administrative petition. Arguments that a statute is unconstitutional are usually made in the High Court of Justice and are technically demanding.

### Where and how

- First step is always a written request to the authority asking it to reconsider and naming the provision you say it exceeded.
- Fines: the request and then the local affairs court (lesson 01.4).
- Decisions of ministries and listed authorities: administrative courts or the High Court of Justice (lesson 01.4; modules 6, 8 and year 2).
- Fees for petitions are in [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md) as they are added.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- You want to argue that a statute (not a regulation) is void under a Basic Law.
- The decision affects your liberty, residence status, livelihood or home and a deadline is short.
- The authority answers with a detailed legal opinion and your next step is a petition.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arguing fairness instead of authority | The court defers to the authority | Rewrite the argument as "exceeds s. X" or "contradicts reg. Y" | Do the chain test before writing |
| Treating an internal procedure as law | You accept a condition the law does not impose | Ask for the authorising provision in writing | Check whether the rule is a regulation or just a directive |
| Quoting a repealed or amended regulation | Your argument fails on its premise | Use the current consolidated text and check the date | Look at the amendment list in the header |
| Jumping to "unconstitutional" | Weak argument, wrong forum, possible costs | Start with interpretation and authority arguments | Use constitutional arguments only with legal help |
| Ignoring Basic Law s. 10 | You attack a pre-1992 law under the limitation clause | Argue interpretation in the Basic Law's spirit instead | Check the law's date before invoking s. 8 |

## Review

- The hierarchy runs Basic Laws, statutes, secondary legislation, administrative directives; case law interprets all of them and binds lower courts.
- The Interpretation Law defines "חיקוק", "תקנה" and "הוראת מינהל"; only enactments bind the public as legislation.
- Bank Mizrahi (1995) gave Basic Laws constitutional status and enabled review of statutes under the limitation clause.
- Rubinstein (1998) requires primary arrangements to be set by the Knesset, not by secondary legislation or ministerial discretion.
- HCJ 5658/23 (2024) held, by majority, that Basic Laws themselves may be reviewed in extreme cases; the issue remains contested.
