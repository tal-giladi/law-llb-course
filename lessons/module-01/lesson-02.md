# 01.2 · Israel's mixed legal system

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take a rule you meet in daily life, such as a tort claim, a land registration or a marriage, and say which historical layer it comes from, why it still applies, and what an Israeli judge does when no statute or precedent answers the question at all.

## Why it matters

Israeli law is not one code written at one time. It is a stack of layers: Ottoman law that applied until 1917, the British Mandate's ordinances and its import of English common law, the laws of the Knesset since 1948, and seventy-plus years of case law that has knitted the layers together. Many statutes you will meet in this course still carry their Mandate names (the Civil Wrongs Ordinance, the Evidence Ordinance, the Income Tax Ordinance). Personal status still runs largely through religious law, a system the Ottomans and the British left in place.

Knowing the layer tells you how to read a rule. A Mandate ordinance was drafted in English and its Hebrew is a translation, so older rulings sometimes read it with English case law in mind. A modern Israeli code, such as the Contracts (General Part) Law 1973, was written in Hebrew as part of a deliberate codification and is read on its own terms. And when there is truly no answer, the Foundations of Law Act 1980 tells the judge where to look: not to England any more, but to the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage.

## The law

### Continuity at independence: Law and Administration Ordinance 1948, s. 11

> המשפט שהיה קיים בארץ־ישראל ביום ה׳ באייר תש״ח (14 במאי 1948) יעמוד בתקפו, עד כמה שאין בו משום סתירה לפקודה זו או לחוקים האחרים שיינתנו על ידי מועצת המדינה הזמנית או על פיה, ובשינויים הנובעים מתוך הקמת המדינה ורשויותיה.

*Gist:* All the law in force on 14 May 1948 stayed in force, except where it contradicted the new state's laws or had to change because the state was established. [Full text, s. 11](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_סדרי_השלטון_והמשפט#סעיף_11)

This one sentence is why Ottoman and Mandate law are still part of Israeli law. Nothing was wiped clean in 1948; the state inherited the whole stack and then began replacing it.

### The bridge to England: Order in Council 1922, art. 46 (before its repeal)

> [...] ובמקרים שאלה לא יהיו נוהגים לגבם, ישפטו בתי המשפט האזרחיים בהתאם ליסודות החוק המקובל ולעיקרי הצדק הנוהגים באנגליה [...]

*Gist:* Where Ottoman law, the Orders in Council and the ordinances gave no answer, the civil courts were to decide by the substance of English common law and the doctrines of equity, as far as local conditions allowed. [Text preserved in the Foundations of Law Act page](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט)

Article 46 was the channel through which English law flowed into the country's gaps for almost sixty years. It came with a proviso: English law applied only so far as the "conditions of Palestine and its inhabitants" permitted.

### The break: Foundations of Law Act 1980

> ראה בית המשפט שאלה משפטית הטעונה הכרעה, ולא מצא לה תשובה בדבר חקיקה, בהלכה פסוקה או בדרך של היקש, יכריע בה לאור עקרונות החירות, הצדק, היושר והשלום של המשפט העברי ומורשת ישראל.

*Gist:* When a court faces a legal question it cannot answer from legislation, case law or analogy, it decides in light of the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage. [Full text, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט#סעיף_1)

> (א) סימן 46 לדבר המלך במועצתו לארץ־ישראל, 1922–1947 – בטל.
> (ב) אין בהוראות סעיף קטן (א) כדי לפגוע במשפט שנקלט בארץ לפני תחילת חוק זה.

*Gist:* Article 46 is repealed, but English law already absorbed into Israeli law before 1980 stays. [Full text, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט#סעיף_2)

Three points matter for exams and for practice:

1. **The order of sources.** Statute first, then case law, then analogy. Only if all three fail is there a lacuna that s. 1 fills. Because statute, case law and analogy answer almost every question, s. 1 has been used rarely.
2. **The 2018 amendment.** Until 2018 the section referred only to "מורשת ישראל". A 2018 amendment added the words "המשפט העברי ו" (Jewish law). Whether that changes the section's practical weight is debated: some see a stronger instruction to consult Jewish legal sources, others note that the lacuna threshold is unchanged.
3. **What was not touched.** English law absorbed before 1980 remains (s. 2(ב)). Israeli courts still read English rulings for persuasion, as they read American, Canadian or German rulings, but they are no longer a binding gap-filler.

### Ending the Ottoman civil code: Mejelle Repeal Law 1984

> המג׳לה – בטלה.

*Gist:* The Mejelle, the Ottoman civil code, is repealed. [Full text](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_לביטול_המג'לה)

The repeal came after the Knesset had replaced most of its subject matter with Israeli codes (contracts, property, agency, guarantee, sale, gifts and more). Section 2 kept specific provisions that other laws had preserved and the law that the Sharia courts apply.

### Personal status: Order in Council, art. 51

> [...] יהא השיפוט בעניני המצב האישי, בהתאם להוראות חלק זה, מסור לבתי הדין של העדות הדתיות. [...]

*Gist:* Jurisdiction in matters of personal status (marriage and divorce, maintenance, guardianship and more) belongs to the courts of the religious communities, as set out in that part of the Order in Council. [Full text, art. 51](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/דברי_המלך_במועצה_על_ארץ-ישראל#סעיף_51)

This Ottoman-and-Mandate "millet" arrangement is still the frame for religious courts in Israel, now overlaid by Israeli laws such as the Rabbinical Courts Jurisdiction (Marriage and Divorce) Law 1953 and the Family Court Law 1995 (lesson 01.4).

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 73/53 חברת "קול העם" בע״מ נ׳ שר הפנים, פ״ד ז 871 (1953)

The Minister of the Interior suspended two newspapers under a Mandate ordinance, the Press Ordinance 1933, which allowed suspension of a publication "likely to endanger the public peace". Justice Agranat held that the ordinance had to be read in light of the new state's democratic character as expressed in the Declaration of Independence: the minister could act only where there was a "near certainty" (ודאות קרובה) of real danger, not a remote possibility. The suspension orders were set aside.

Why it matters: it is the classic example of an inherited Mandate law surviving under s. 11 of the 1948 Ordinance, but being read through Israeli values. The Declaration of Independence did not become a constitution, yet it shaped how every older law was interpreted. [English translation, Cardozo Israeli Supreme Court Project](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/228)

### Where the text is the source: the Foundations of Law Act itself

No single ruling settles how s. 1 works; the debate lives in the academic literature and in scattered judgments. The key texts are the Act itself and its 2018 amendment, both in the [consolidated text](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט) with the official Reshumot links in its header. Module 10 (Jewish law) returns to how judges have used, or declined to use, s. 1.

## How it works in practice

When you research any question, find the layer first.

1. **Is there an Israeli statute?** Search the Knesset database or Wikisource for the subject. Most areas of private law (contracts, property, inheritance, family property, consumer protection) are now Israeli codes. A rule in an Israeli code is read by Israeli methods (module 2, lesson on interpretation).
2. **Is it a Mandate ordinance?** Tort law (the Civil Wrongs Ordinance [New Version]), evidence (the Evidence Ordinance [New Version]) and income tax (the Income Tax Ordinance [New Version]) are ordinances in origin, re-enacted in Hebrew "new versions". The text binds; older English cases may explain its wording but do not bind.
3. **Is it personal status?** Marriage and divorce run through the religious courts and religious law; many surrounding issues (property, maintenance in some cases, custody) may be heard either in the religious court or the Family Court (lesson 01.4).
4. **Is there case law?** Israeli rulings fill and interpret statutes (lesson 01.5). A Supreme Court ruling binds lower courts.
5. **Still nothing?** Then, and only then, s. 1 of the Foundations of Law Act points to the principles of Jewish law and heritage. In practice you will almost never reach this step; if you think you have, check again for an analogy.

For a self-represented person the practical lesson is: do not cite English or American cases as if they bind an Israeli court, and do not skip the Israeli statute because a foreign rule seems fairer.

## Worked example

Hila Mizrahi from Be'er Sheva and her neighbour argue about a fig tree whose roots crack her patio. She finds an old English case on encroaching roots and wants to base her letter on it. Walk through the layers.

1. **Statute?** Israeli property law is codified. The Land Law 1969 governs ownership and neighbour relations in land, and the Civil Wrongs Ordinance provides the tort of nuisance (מטרד). Both apply.
2. **Layer of each.** The Land Law is an Israeli code; the Civil Wrongs Ordinance is Mandate-origin law in an Israeli "new version". Both are binding Israeli law today under s. 11 of the 1948 Ordinance and later re-enactment.
3. **Case law?** Israeli rulings on nuisance between neighbours will guide the court. A Supreme Court ruling on the point would bind a magistrates' court.
4. **The English case?** After 1980 it is not a binding gap-filler. It can be cited as persuasive comparative law at most, and only if Israeli law leaves room.
5. **Result.** Hila's letter should cite the Israeli statute sections and, if she finds one, an Israeli ruling. The English case may stay in her notes.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your inventory from lesson 01.1.
- Access to [Wikisource's Open Law Book](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/ויקיטקסט:ספר_החוקים_הפתוח) or the Knesset database.
- 40 minutes.

### Steps

1. Pick five matters from your inventory (for example: rent, a car accident, a payslip, a marriage, a parking fine).
2. For each, find the main statute that governs it. Write its full Hebrew name and year.
3. Classify the statute's layer: Ottoman, Mandate ordinance (often in a "[נוסח חדש]" re-enactment), or Israeli law of the Knesset. The header of each Wikisource page shows the official publication (for example "ס״ח" for an Israeli law, "חא״י" or "ע״ר" for Mandate texts).
4. Mark any matter that touches personal status (marriage, divorce, custody, inheritance between religious communities). Note which religious court might be involved.
5. For one matter, write three sentences explaining why the rule still applies today, naming s. 11 of the Law and Administration Ordinance or the re-enactment.

### Template

None. Use a five-row table: matter, statute, year, layer, why it applies.

### How you know it worked

- Each matter has a statute with its exact Hebrew name.
- The layer matches the publication marker in the statute's header.
- Your three sentences mention the continuity rule or the re-enactment, not "because it is the law".

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The skill here, identifying which layer of law governs, is exactly what you do before you write any letter or claim in your own name.

### Where and how

- In a demand letter or a small claim, cite the Israeli statute and section, and an Israeli ruling if you have one. Judges in the small claims court are not bound by the regular rules of procedure (Courts Law s. 62), but they still decide by Israeli law.
- Free full texts: Wikisource for consolidated statutes, the Knesset database for the official history, and the Judiciary's decision search for rulings (module 2).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your question depends on a rule's historical origin (for example an old Ottoman land classification or a pre-1948 registration) and real property is at stake.
- You find yourself arguing that there is a lacuna in Israeli law: that is a rare, sophisticated argument and a sign the case is beyond self-help.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing an English or American case as binding | The court ignores it; your argument looks unprepared | Replace it with the Israeli statute and an Israeli ruling | Search Israeli sources first; use foreign law only as comparison |
| Relying on a Mandate text's old wording | You quote a version that was replaced by a "new version" | Use the current consolidated text | Always check the header for "[נוסח חדש]" and later amendments |
| Assuming the Mejelle or Ottoman rules still govern a contract | Wrong legal basis | Use the Israeli contract laws | Remember the Mejelle was repealed in 1984 |
| Arguing "there is no law on this" | The court finds an analogy you missed | Look for analogy within Israeli law | Claim a lacuna only after statute, case law and analogy fail |

## Review

- Israeli law is layered: Ottoman, Mandate (with English common law and equity imported through art. 46), Israeli statutes, and Israeli case law.
- Section 11 of the Law and Administration Ordinance 1948 kept all existing law in force; the Knesset has been replacing it since.
- The Foundations of Law Act 1980 repealed art. 46 and points to the principles of Jewish law and the Jewish heritage, but only for a true lacuna after statute, case law and analogy.
- Mandate ordinances still in force are read by Israeli courts through Israeli values, as Kol Ha'am showed in 1953.
- Personal status still runs through religious courts, a structure inherited from the Ottoman and Mandate periods.
