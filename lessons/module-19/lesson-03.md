# 19.3 · Customary international law

By the end of this lesson you will be able to test whether a claimed rule is really customary international law: find the State practice, find the evidence that States follow it because they think they must, and decide whether the rule binds Israel. In Israel this matters more than in many countries, because custom enters Israeli law automatically, while treaties do not (lesson 19.7).

## Why it matters

Custom is unwritten law made by the conduct of States. It covers much of the ground no treaty covers for everyone: the immunity of foreign States (lesson 19.4), the rules on the use of force alongside the UN Charter, the core of the law of treaties itself (lesson 19.2), State responsibility, diplomatic protection, the law of the sea for non-parties to UNCLOS. Custom binds every State, including States that never signed anything, unless a State objected persistently while the rule was forming.

For an Israeli litigant the stakes are practical. The Supreme Court has held for decades that customary international law is part of the law of the land unless a statute says otherwise, so a rule proved to be custom can be applied by an Israeli judge directly. A rule that exists only in a treaty Israel has not incorporated cannot. Much litigation, from foreign-State immunity to the law applied in the West Bank, has turned on exactly this line.

## The law

### The definition

> b. international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law;

*Gist:* Custom has two elements: a general practice, and acceptance of that practice as law. ([ICJ Statute art. 38(1)(b)](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute))

### The ILC's 2018 conclusions

The UN International Law Commission's conclusions are not a treaty, but courts and foreign ministries, including Israel's, use them as the working method.

> To determine the existence and content of a rule of customary international law, it is necessary to ascertain whether there is a general practice that is accepted as law (opinio juris).

> Each of the two constituent elements is to be separately ascertained. This requires an assessment of evidence for each element.

*Gist:* Practice and opinio juris are separate tests, each with its own evidence; you cannot infer one from the other. ([ILC conclusions 2, 3(2)](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf))

> 1. The relevant practice must be general, meaning that it must be sufficiently widespread and representative, as well as consistent.
> 2. Provided that the practice is general, no particular duration is required.

*Gist:* Practice must be widespread, representative and consistent; there is no minimum number of years. ([ILC conclusion 8](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf))

Forms of practice (conclusion 6) include diplomatic acts, conduct around UN resolutions and treaties, operational conduct "on the ground", legislation, administrative acts and **decisions of national courts**, so an Israeli Supreme Court judgment is itself State practice. Evidence of opinio juris (conclusion 10) includes public statements on behalf of States, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence, national court decisions and treaty provisions; silence can count where States were in a position to react and the circumstances called for a reaction.

### Treaties and custom

> 1. A rule set forth in a treaty may reflect a rule of customary international law if it is established that the treaty rule: (a) codified a rule of customary international law existing at the time when the treaty was concluded; (b) has led to the crystallization of a rule of customary international law that had started to emerge prior to the conclusion of the treaty; or (c) has given rise to a general practice that is accepted as law (opinio juris), thus generating a new rule of customary international law.

*Gist:* A treaty article can bind non-parties as custom in three ways: it codified existing custom, it crystallised an emerging one, or it later generated one. ([ILC conclusion 11(1)](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf)) The same conclusion warns that a rule appearing in many treaties is not necessarily custom.

### The persistent objector

> 1. Where a State has objected to a rule of customary international law while that rule was in the process of formation, the rule is not opposable to the State concerned for so long as it maintains its objection.
> 2. The objection must be clearly expressed, made known to other States, and maintained persistently.

*Gist:* A State that objected clearly and consistently while a rule was forming is not bound by it, but this never applies to peremptory norms. ([ILC conclusion 15](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf))

## Leading cases

### North Sea Continental Shelf (Germany/Denmark; Germany/Netherlands), ICJ (20.2.1969)

Denmark and the Netherlands argued that the "equidistance" method for dividing the continental shelf in the 1958 Geneva Convention bound Germany, which had signed but not ratified it, as custom. The Court held that it did not. For a treaty rule to become custom quickly, State practice, "including that of States whose interests are specially affected", had to be extensive and virtually uniform, and the conduct had to show a belief that it was legally required, not just convenient. The practice relied on did not show that belief. Why it matters: it is the classic statement of the two elements, of the role of "specially affected" States, and of why a treaty does not bind non-parties merely because many States joined it. [Case page, judgment and summary](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/51)

### בג״ץ 606/78 איוב נ׳ שר הביטחון; בג״ץ 610/78 מטאוע נ׳ שר הביטחון (פ״ד לג(2) 113) — "Beit El"

Landowners from Al-Bireh and Tubas petitioned against military requisition orders for land later used for civilian settlements, relying on the 1907 Hague Regulations and the Fourth Geneva Convention 1949. Witkon J., for a unanimous Court, held that individuals may rely in an Israeli court on international law only if it has become part of Israeli law, and that a treaty provision is part of Israeli law if it reflects customary international law. Adopting Professor Dinstein's analysis, the Court held that the Hague Regulations are customary and therefore enforceable by the Court, while the Fourth Geneva Convention is conventional and, without Knesset legislation, not enforceable in an Israeli court; under the Hague Regulations it found the temporary requisition lawful and dismissed the petitions. Why it matters here: it is the leading Israeli statement that custom is directly applicable and unincorporated treaties are not. [ICRC casebook summary and translation extracts](https://casebook.icrc.org/case-study/israel-ayub-v-minister-defence)

The substantive questions in Beit El (the status of the West Bank, the application of the Fourth Geneva Convention and the legality of settlements) are among the most contested in international law. In outline, and only as a pointer: Israel's official position is that the Fourth Geneva Convention does not apply de jure to the territories because they were not the sovereign territory of another High Contracting Party in 1967, while Israel has said it applies the Convention's humanitarian provisions in practice; the opposing position, taken by the ICJ (2004 and 2024), the ICRC, the UN Security Council (resolution 2334 of 2016) and most States, is that the Convention applies and that settlements breach art. 49(6). The Israeli Supreme Court in later cases has applied the law of belligerent occupation and the humanitarian provisions of the Convention on the basis of the State's undertaking, without ruling on de jure applicability. International humanitarian law is a year-4 elective; lesson 19.5 gives the current procedural status of the international proceedings.

### רע״א 7092/94 מלכות קנדה נ׳ אדלסון (3.6.1997)

Recapped fully in lesson 19.4. President Barak stated that "Customary international law is part and parcel of the law of the State of Israel", citing a line of cases from Shimshon and Eichmann to Beit El and Afu, and identified the shift from absolute to restrictive State immunity by examining State practice and foreign judgments. Why it matters here: a model of how an Israeli court actually proves custom: statutes and judgments of other States, treaties as evidence, and scholarly writing. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/her-majesty-queen-right-canada-v-edelson)

## How it works in practice

### Proving custom: the evidence file

1. **State the rule precisely.** "States may not be sued for commercial acts" is testable; "States must respect human dignity" is not.
2. **Practice (10–20 States, from different regions and legal systems).** Statutes (the US Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, the UK State Immunity Act), judgments, official manuals, diplomatic notes. Include the specially affected States.
3. **Opinio juris.** Statements that the conduct is legally required: government legal opinions, positions in UN debates, the preamble or travaux of a codification treaty.
4. **Contrary practice.** Note States acting differently and how they justify it. Following *Nicaragua v. United States* (ICJ 1986), conduct inconsistent with a rule, when the State justifies it as an exception, tends to confirm the rule.
5. **Israel's own position.** Has Israel objected? (Search Israel's statements in the UN Sixth Committee and its submissions to the ICJ.)
6. **Subsidiary means.** ICJ judgments, ILC work, leading textbooks (art. 38(1)(d)), as support, not as a substitute for practice.

### In an Israeli court

The party relying on a customary rule must prove it, and Edelson shows the expected evidence: foreign statutes and judgments, treaties as evidence and scholarship. Custom yields to a contrary Knesset statute (Edelson para. 12, quoting Shamgar P.: custom is part of the law of the land "subject to Israeli legislation providing otherwise"). In practice, Israeli courts accept custom most readily when it is codified in a widely ratified treaty and confirmed by ICJ or ILC work.

## Worked example

Ilan Shapiro and his partner Mariam Haddad run a small travel agency in Jaffa. A foreign State's national airline (wholly owned by that State) cancels a group booking and refuses refunds. Before suing, they ask: does custom give the State immunity?

1. **The rule claimed by the airline:** "a foreign State and its agencies are immune from foreign courts".
2. **Practice:** Ilan finds national statutes (UK 1978, US 1976, Canada, Australia) and the UN Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities 2004 (not yet in force) all denying immunity for commercial transactions; national judgments in many States say the same.
3. **Opinio juris:** these laws and the UN Convention present the commercial exception as the law, not a favour.
4. **Israeli position:** the Supreme Court adopted the restrictive rule as custom in Edelson (1997), and the Knesset codified it in the Foreign States Immunity Law 2008 (lesson 19.4).
5. **Conclusion:** they may sue; the airline, as a "separate entity" of the State, is in any case treated under the 2008 Law. Custom explains why the statute takes the form it does.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One rule you suspect is customary (suggestions: the duty not to cause significant transboundary environmental harm; the commercial exception to immunity; diplomatic inviolability of embassies).
- The ILC conclusions (link above) and 35 minutes.

### Steps

1. **State the rule (5 min)** in one testable sentence.
2. **Collect practice (15 min).** At least five States from different regions: a statute, a judgment, an official statement, or conduct. Note sources.
3. **Collect opinio juris (10 min).** At least two statements that the conduct is legally required.
4. **Check Israel (5 min).** Any Israeli statute, judgment or statement accepting or rejecting the rule.

None. Add the evidence table to your module 19 file.

### How you know it worked

- Your table has separate columns for practice and opinio juris (ILC conclusion 3(2)).
- You included at least one contrary example and explained it.
- You can say whether an Israeli court could apply the rule without legislation.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Custom shows up in practice when a foreign State or international body claims immunity (lesson 19.4), and in arguments that an Israeli statute should be read consistently with international law (lesson 19.7). A self-represented party can cite a customary rule, but must bring the evidence.

### Where and how

- ICJ judgments (icj-cij.org), ILC reports (legal.un.org/ilc), and the ICRC customary IHL database for the law of armed conflict.
- University clinics and the [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md) list.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your case depends on proving a customary rule that the State contests.
- The other party is a foreign State, an international organisation or a diplomat (immunity questions arise at the outset).

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proving practice and assuming opinio juris | The court finds mere usage or courtesy | Add statements of legal obligation | Keep two evidence columns |
| Citing only Western States | The practice is not "representative" | Add States from other regions | Plan the sample before searching |
| Treating every widely ratified treaty as custom | Non-parties are not bound | Show codification, crystallisation or later practice | Apply ILC conclusion 11 |
| Ignoring contrary practice | The other side exposes it | Explain it as breach or exception | Search for it yourself |
| Relying on an unincorporated treaty instead of custom | The Israeli court will not enforce it | Recast the argument as custom if you can prove it | Remember Beit El |

## Review

- Custom = general practice + acceptance as law; each element needs its own evidence (ILC 2018).
- Practice must be widespread, representative and consistent, including specially affected States (North Sea); no minimum duration.
- Treaty rules bind non-parties only if they codify, crystallise or generate custom.
- Persistent objectors are not bound, except by jus cogens.
- In Israel custom is part of the law unless a statute provides otherwise (Edelson para. 12); unincorporated treaties are not (Beit El).
