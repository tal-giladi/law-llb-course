# 19.1 · What international law is and its sources

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take any sentence that begins "under international law…", find which source it rests on, and judge how much weight it carries. That is the first skill of the whole module: in an Israeli court, a ministry letter or a UN report, the strength of an international-law argument depends almost entirely on its source.

## Why it matters

Public international law (משפט בינלאומי פומבי) is the law between States, and increasingly between States, international organisations and individuals. It is a full core course in every Israeli faculty (TAU 1411-9223, HUJI 62602, Bar-Ilan 99107), and it reaches daily life more than most people think: the Hague Convention that returns abducted children, the Convention on the Rights of the Child that Israeli courts cite in custody and education cases, the immunity of a foreign embassy that you cannot easily sue, the extradition treaty, the double-tax treaty, the aviation agreement that sets your compensation for a cancelled flight.

It is also the field where political argument most often dresses itself as law. A newspaper writes that something "violates international law"; a minister answers that it does not. Both may be sincere, and the only way to assess them is to ask: which rule, from which source, binding on whom? This lesson gives you that question. Lesson [01.3](../module-01/lesson-03.md) taught the hierarchy of Israeli norms; international law has no such neat pyramid, and that difference is itself the first thing to learn.

The theoretical question "is international law really law?" belongs to legal theory (lesson [09.2](../module-09/lesson-02.md)): Austin said no, because there is no sovereign commanding it; Hart called it a set of primary rules lacking fully developed secondary rules of change and adjudication. In practice States treat it as binding, argue about it in legal terms, and comply most of the time. This course takes the practitioner's view: international law is law, with weak central enforcement and strong decentralised enforcement through reciprocity, domestic courts and reputation.

## The law

International-law sources are quoted in English, the language of the authentic texts used by Israeli courts and the Foreign Ministry.

### The list of sources: ICJ Statute art. 38(1)

> The Court, whose function is to decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it, shall apply:
> a. international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the contesting states;
> b. international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law;
> c. the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations;
> d. subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.

*Gist:* The ICJ applies treaties, custom and general principles as sources of law, and uses judgments and scholarly writing only as evidence of what the law is. ([ICJ Statute](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute))

Article 38 was written for the ICJ, but every international lawyer, including Israel's Supreme Court, treats it as the standard list. Three points follow from its wording:

- **No formal hierarchy among (a), (b) and (c).** A later or more specific treaty usually prevails over custom between its parties, but custom can also change a treaty. Lawyers resolve conflicts with the maxims *lex specialis* and *lex posterior*, not with a pyramid.
- **(c) "civilized nations"** is 1920 language; today it is read as principles common to the main legal systems (good faith, *res judicata*, the right to be heard, estoppel).
- **(d) is not a source but evidence.** There is no binding precedent:

> The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case.

*Gist:* ICJ judgments bind only the parties to the case, although in practice the Court follows its own earlier reasoning closely. ([ICJ Statute art. 59](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute))

### Rules from which no derogation is permitted

> A treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law. For the purposes of the present Convention, a peremptory norm of general international law is a norm accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character.

*Gist:* A small set of rules (generally accepted examples: the prohibitions of genocide, slavery, torture, aggression) override any treaty that conflicts with them. ([VCLT art. 53](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/1_1_1969.pdf)) Israel is not a party to the VCLT (lesson 19.2), but the concept of peremptory norms is generally treated as customary.

### The Charter's priority rule

> In the event of a conflict between the obligations of the Members of the United Nations under the present Charter and their obligations under any other international agreement, their obligations under the present Charter shall prevail.

*Gist:* Obligations under the UN Charter, including binding Security Council decisions, prevail over other treaty obligations of UN members. ([UN Charter art. 103](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text))

### Soft law

General Assembly resolutions, declarations (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights 1948), concluding observations of treaty bodies and guidelines are not in art. 38. They are not binding as such, but they can be evidence of custom (lesson 19.3) or an agreed interpretation of a treaty. The ILC's 2018 conclusions say a resolution "cannot, of itself, create a rule of customary international law" but may provide evidence of one. Treat soft law as an argument, not a rule.

## Leading cases

### The Case of the S.S. Lotus (France v. Turkey), PCIJ Series A No. 10 (7.9.1927)

A French steamer collided on the high seas with a Turkish ship, killing Turkish sailors; Turkey prosecuted the French officer of the watch. France argued that Turkey needed a permissive rule of international law to try him. The Permanent Court (by the President's casting vote) held that restrictions on State independence cannot be presumed: unless a rule of international law prohibits it, a State may extend its laws and courts to acts outside its territory. Why it matters: the "Lotus principle" is the purest statement of the consent-based view, that States are bound only by what they accepted. Modern law has narrowed it (the collision rule itself was reversed by later treaties), but it still frames every jurisdiction argument in lesson 19.4. [Judgment (PCIJ archive)](https://www.icj-cij.org/sites/default/files/permanent-court-of-international-justice/serie_A/A_10/30_Lotus_Arret.pdf)

### ע״פ 336/61 אייכמן נ׳ היועץ המשפטי לממשלה (29.5.1962)

Adolf Eichmann was brought from Argentina and tried in Jerusalem under the Nazis and Nazi Collaborators (Punishment) Law 1950 for crimes committed in Europe before Israel existed. He argued that the Law was retroactive and extraterritorial, contrary to international law. The Supreme Court held that no rule of customary international law forbids a State to legislate retroactively for such crimes or to try a foreigner for crimes committed abroad; that the crimes were international crimes entailing individual responsibility; and that their universal character gives every State power to try them. The "act of State" defence was rejected. Why it matters: it is the founding Israeli judgment on universal jurisdiction, and it shows an Israeli court treating customary international law as part of its own law and using art. 38-type reasoning (State practice, Nuremberg, the Genocide Convention) to identify it. [Case summary (Asser Institute)](https://www.internationalcrimesdatabase.org/Case/185)

## How it works in practice

### The source test, in four questions

When you meet a claim that "international law requires X":

1. **Which instrument or rule?** Ask for the article, the treaty or the custom. "International law" is not a citation.
2. **What kind of source?** Treaty (art. 38(1)(a)), custom (b), general principle (c), or only a judgment, report or resolution (d or soft law).
3. **Binding on whom?** A treaty binds its parties only; check whether Israel ratified it (OHCHR and the UN Treaty Collection publish status tables; lesson 19.6). Custom binds all States, except a persistent objector (lesson 19.3).
4. **Binding where?** On the international plane, and/or in an Israeli court? These are different questions in Israel's dualist system (lesson 19.7): a ratified treaty binds Israel internationally but is not, by itself, Israeli law.

### Who speaks for Israel on international law

- **The Foreign Ministry's Legal Adviser** advises the Government on treaties, signs off on ratification proposals and represents Israel's positions in international bodies.
- **The Attorney General**, through the Ministry of Justice's international department, handles extradition, mutual legal assistance and international litigation, and is the central authority for the Hague Abduction Convention (lesson 19.8).
- **The courts** decide what international law means for a case before them, and are not bound by the Government's view, though they give it weight.

Official positions are published on gov.il (⚠️ blocks automated access; check manually) and in Israel's reports to UN treaty bodies, which are public on the OHCHR site.

## Worked example

Noa Ben-David, a high-school teacher in Haifa, reads a social-media post that "international law forbids Israel to extradite anyone to a country with the death penalty". A student, Yusuf Masarwa from Tayibe, asks her whether it is true. She runs the source test.

1. **Which rule?** The post cites nothing. Noa searches and finds that the claim usually rests on the ICCPR (art. 6) as interpreted by the UN Human Rights Committee, and on a European Court of Human Rights judgment.
2. **What kind of source?** The ICCPR is a treaty; the Human Rights Committee's views are treaty-body interpretation (persuasive, soft law); the European judgment binds only Council of Europe States.
3. **Binding on Israel?** Israel ratified the ICCPR in 1991 (lesson 19.6). Whether art. 6 bars extradition to a death-penalty State without assurances is an interpretation the Committee has adopted; States differ on it.
4. **In an Israeli court?** The ICCPR is not incorporated; an extradition court would apply the Extradition Law 1954, read in light of the covenant where possible (lesson 19.7). In practice Israel and many States seek an assurance that the death penalty will not be carried out.

Noa's answer to Yusuf: "Not a clear yes or no. There is a treaty rule on the right to life that Israel accepted, and an influential but non-binding interpretation of it. A court in Israel applies the Extradition Law and reads it in harmony with the treaty." That is a legal answer, not a political one.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One news article, NGO report or official statement that says something is required or forbidden "under international law" (any subject: environment, aviation, refugees, trade, children).
- The ICJ Statute art. 38 (link above) and 35 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Copy the claim (5 min).** Write the exact sentence.
2. **Find the source (15 min).** Trace it to the treaty article, custom, principle, judgment or resolution it rests on. If the text gives none, search for the instrument it probably means.
3. **Classify (5 min).** Mark it (a), (b), (c), (d) or soft law, and note whether a peremptory status is claimed.
4. **Test who is bound (5 min).** For a treaty, check whether Israel (or the State concerned) is a party; for custom, note any objection.
5. **Write two sentences (5 min).** One stating the strongest legal version of the claim; one stating its main weakness.

### Template

None. Keep the result in your module 19 file; the project uses it.

### How you know it worked

- You named an article, a treaty or a specific practice, not "international law" in general.
- You can say whether the source binds Israel, and why.
- Your two sentences would be accepted by a reader who disagrees with the claim's politics.

- [ ] I can explain why an ICJ judgment is not binding precedent (art. 59).
- [ ] I can tell soft law from a binding rule.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The source test is used every time you rely on a treaty in a letter to an authority or in your own court case (lessons 19.7 and 19.8): Israeli courts expect you to name the instrument, its article and Israel's status under it.

### Where and how

- Treaty texts: the UN Treaty Collection (treaties.un.org) and the Israeli treaty series (כתבי אמנה, published by the Foreign Ministry; ⚠️ manual check of current online availability).
- Free academic help: university legal clinics with an international-law or human-rights focus ([where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md)).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your matter turns on the meaning of a treaty in a pending proceeding with a deadline (extradition, abduction, immunity).
- You would need to prove a rule of custom in court; that needs research a self-represented party rarely has time for.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing "international law" without a source | The argument is ignored | Name the article or practice | Run the four-question source test |
| Treating a UN report or resolution as binding | You overstate your case and lose credibility | Present it as soft law or evidence of custom | Check art. 38 and the ILC conclusions |
| Assuming an ICJ judgment binds everyone | Wrong conclusion about third States | Cite it as persuasive (art. 59) | Ask who the parties were |
| Assuming a treaty binds Israel because many States joined | The rule may not bind Israel at all | Check the UN or OHCHR status table | Always check ratification |
| Confusing the international and domestic planes | You argue the treaty "is law" in an Israeli court | Argue interpretation in harmony (lesson 19.7) | Ask "binding where?" |

## Review

- Art. 38(1) of the ICJ Statute lists treaties, custom and general principles as sources; judgments and teachings are subsidiary means.
- There is no strict hierarchy, except that peremptory norms override treaties (VCLT art. 53) and Charter obligations prevail (art. 103).
- ICJ judgments bind only the parties (art. 59); soft law is evidence, not obligation.
- Lotus states the consent-based view; Eichmann shows an Israeli court identifying custom and universal jurisdiction.
- Always ask: which rule, which source, binding on whom, and binding where.
