# 19.4 · States, recognition, jurisdiction and immunity

By the end of this lesson you will be able to analyse whether an entity is a State, which State may legislate for and try a given act, and whether you can sue a foreign State or its agencies in an Israeli court. The last question is the one you are most likely to meet in practice: a dispute with an embassy as landlord or employer, a State-owned airline, or a foreign government's purchase from an Israeli supplier.

## Why it matters

States are the main subjects of international law, and three questions follow from statehood: who counts as a State, how far each State's laws and courts reach, and when a State is exempt from another State's courts. Each has an Israeli dimension. Israel's own admission to the UN in 1949 was a contested recognition question; the status of a Palestinian State remains one of the most disputed recognition questions in the world; Israel recognised Somaliland in December 2025, the first State to do so. Israeli criminal law reaches far beyond Israel's territory (the Eichmann trial, terrorism, offences against Israelis abroad). And since 2008 Israel has a statute on foreign-State immunity, applied in the magistrates', district and labour courts. Lesson [15.1](../module-15/lesson-01.md) taught Israeli jurisdiction and parties; this lesson adds the international layer.

## The law

### What a State is

> The state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: a) a permanent population; b) a defined territory; c) government; and d) capacity to enter into relations with the other states.

> The political existence of the state is independent of recognition by the other states.

*Gist:* The Montevideo criteria (accepted as reflecting custom) are population, territory, effective government and capacity for foreign relations; art. 3 adopts the declaratory theory, that recognition does not create a State. ([Montevideo Convention arts 1, 3](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/intam03.asp))

**The two theories.** On the *declaratory* view a State exists once it meets the criteria and recognition merely acknowledges it; on the *constitutive* view recognition by other States is what makes an entity a State for them. Practice mixes both: recognition is a political act each State decides freely, but there are legal limits (the duty not to recognise situations created by a serious breach of a peremptory norm, and the right of peoples to self-determination, which can support statehood where effective government is weak).

**Recognition and Israel: three examples, with the positions attributed.**

- *Israel 1948–49.* Israel declared independence on 14 May 1948 and was recognised within days by the United States (de facto) and the Soviet Union (de jure); it was admitted to the UN on 11 May 1949 (GA resolution 273). Several States still do not recognise Israel; Israel's position is that its statehood is established by every Montevideo criterion and its UN membership.
- *Palestine.* Palestine has been a UN "non-member observer State" since GA resolution 67/19 (2012), and after recognitions by the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Portugal, France and others in September 2025, roughly three-quarters of UN members recognise it (press counts at the time ranged around 150–157). Israel's official position is that Palestine does not meet the criteria of statehood (in particular effective, unified government and agreed territory), that its status is to be settled in direct negotiations under the Oslo framework, and that recognition outside negotiations is unlawful under those agreements and harmful. The opposing position, of the Palestinian leadership and recognising States, is that the Palestinian people's right to self-determination supports statehood, that the territory is defined by the 1967 lines subject to agreed changes, and that recognition supports, rather than replaces, a negotiated outcome. Several States (including the United States) do not recognise Palestine and favour negotiations first. These are political-legal positions; no court has an authoritative ruling binding all States.
- *Somaliland.* Israel recognised Somaliland on 26 December 2025, establishing diplomatic relations; Somalia, and several States including Egypt and Turkey, condemned the step as a breach of Somalia's territorial integrity. Israel's position is that Somaliland has functioned as an effective State since 1991. It shows the constitutive and declaratory theories in action: Somaliland arguably meets Montevideo, yet almost no State has recognised it.

### Jurisdiction: how far Israeli criminal law reaches

> דיני העונשין של ישראל יחולו על כל עבירת־פנים.

*Gist:* Israeli criminal law applies to every offence committed in Israel (territorial principle). ([Penal Law s. 12](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין))

> (ב) דיני העונשין של ישראל יחולו גם על עבירות־חוץ נגד – (1) חיי אזרח ישראלי, תושב ישראל או עובד הציבור, גופו, בריאותו, חירותו או רכושו, באשר הוא כזה;

*Gist:* Israeli law also applies to offences abroad against the life, body, health, liberty or property of an Israeli citizen, resident or public servant as such (passive personality); s. 13(א) covers offences abroad against State security and institutions (protective principle). ([Penal Law s. 13](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין))

> (א) דיני העונשין של ישראל יחולו על עבירות־חוץ אשר מדינת ישראל התחייבה, באמנות בינלאומיות רב צדדיות ופתוחות להצטרפות, להעניש עליהן; והוא, אף אם נעברו בידי מי שאינו אזרח ישראלי או תושב ישראל, ויהא מקום עשיית העבירה אשר יהא.

*Gist:* Israeli law applies to offences abroad that Israel undertook in open multilateral treaties to punish, whoever committed them and wherever (treaty-based universal jurisdiction). ([Penal Law s. 16](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין)) Section 15 covers offences by Israeli citizens and residents abroad (nationality principle, with a double-criminality condition), and s. 17 lets Israel agree by treaty to try an Israeli resident instead of extraditing him.

International law's limit on these principles is mostly the Lotus framework (lesson 19.1): a State may legislate unless a rule forbids it, but it may *enforce* (arrest, search, serve) on another State's territory only with that State's consent. Prescribing jurisdiction is broad; enforcement jurisdiction is territorial.

### Immunity of foreign States: the 2008 Law

> למדינה זרה תהא חסינות מפני סמכות השיפוט של בתי המשפט בישראל, למעט סמכות השיפוט בעניינים פליליים (בחוק זה – חסינות מפני סמכות שיפוט), בכפוף להוראות חוק זה.

*Gist:* A foreign State is immune from the civil jurisdiction of Israeli courts, subject to the exceptions in the Law. ([Foreign States Immunity Law s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_חסינות_מדינות_זרות)) "Foreign State" includes its government authorities, officials acting in their official capacity, and a "separate entity" (s. 1).

> למדינה זרה לא תהא חסינות מפני סמכות שיפוט בתביעה שעילתה עסקה מסחרית.

*Gist:* No immunity in a claim based on a commercial transaction, defined in s. 1 as a private-law transaction of a commercial nature (sale of goods or services, loans, guarantees) that does not in substance involve the exercise of sovereign authority. ([s. 3](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_חסינות_מדינות_זרות))

The other exceptions:

- **Employment (s. 4):** no immunity in a labour-court claim about work done (wholly or partly) in Israel by an employee who, when the claim arose, was an Israeli citizen or habitually resident in Israel or the Area; but immunity remains if the employee was a national of the foreign State and not an Israeli resident when proceedings began. If s. 4's conditions are not met, immunity applies even if the job was "commercial".
- **Torts (s. 5):** no immunity for a tort causing bodily injury or damage to tangible property, committed in Israel.
- **Property in Israel (s. 6), intellectual property (s. 7), commercial ships (s. 8).**
- **Waiver (ss. 9–10):** express written waiver, notice to the court, or bringing or joining the proceeding.
- **Arbitration (s. 11):** written consent to arbitrate removes immunity for related court proceedings.

> מדינה זרה תעלה טענה לחסינות מפני סמכות שיפוט בהזדמנות הראשונה ולא יאוחר מן המועד שבו היא טוענת לראשונה לגופה של התביעה.

*Gist:* The foreign State must raise immunity at the first opportunity, and no later than its first argument on the merits, or it is treated as having waived it. ([s. 12](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_חסינות_מדינות_זרות))

**Procedure (ss. 13, 14, 19).** A claim against a foreign State, and a default judgment against it, is served through Israel's Foreign Ministry on the foreign State's foreign ministry; the State has an extended period to respond (see [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md)); a default judgment may be given only if the court is satisfied that the State has no immunity; and the Attorney General must be notified whenever immunity is in issue.

**Execution (ss. 15–17).** Even after a judgment, a foreign State's property is immune from execution, except commercial property, property inherited or gifted in Israel, and land in Israel; diplomatic, consular, military and central-bank property is protected. Winning a judgment and collecting it are two separate problems.

**Diplomatic immunity is separate (s. 21).** The Law does not affect diplomatic or consular immunity under any law or custom: an ambassador personally, and embassy premises, are protected under the Vienna Conventions on Diplomatic and Consular Relations, which reflect custom.

## Leading cases

### רע״א 7092/94 מלכות קנדה נ׳ אדלסון (3.6.1997)

Canada leased a house in Israel as its ambassador's residence; the landlords said the lease had ended and sued for eviction. Canada claimed absolute immunity. The Supreme Court (Barak P., Mazza and Strasberg-Cohen JJ. concurring) held that customary international law had moved from absolute to restrictive immunity; that customary international law is "part and parcel of the law of the State of Israel"; and that the test is the legal nature of the act, not its purpose. A lease is a private-law act, so Canada had no immunity, even though the house served a diplomatic purpose. The ambassador's own diplomatic immunity did not help, because Canada, not the ambassador, was the tenant. Why it matters: it is the leading pre-statute ruling and the basis of the 2008 Law's "nature of the transaction" test. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/her-majesty-queen-right-canada-v-edelson)

### Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v. Italy: Greece intervening), ICJ (3.2.2012)

Italian courts allowed claims against Germany for Second World War crimes and enforced Greek judgments against German property in Italy. The ICJ held that Italy breached Germany's immunity: under current custom, acts of armed forces in armed conflict are sovereign acts (*acta jure imperii*), and immunity is a procedural bar that does not depend on how grave the alleged violation is, even of jus cogens. Why it matters: it confirms that the commercial and tort exceptions do not reach sovereign military acts, and that national courts may not create a "human-rights exception" on their own. [Case page](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/143)

### Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (DRC v. Belgium), ICJ (14.2.2002)

Belgium issued an international arrest warrant for the serving Congolese foreign minister on universal-jurisdiction charges. The ICJ held that a serving foreign minister, like a head of State or government, enjoys full immunity from foreign criminal jurisdiction and inviolability while in office, even for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity; the Court distinguished prosecution before international criminal courts with jurisdiction. Why it matters: universal jurisdiction (Eichmann) and immunity of serving senior officials are different questions; a State may have jurisdiction and still be barred by immunity. [Case page](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/121)

## How it works in practice

### Suing a foreign State or its agency in Israel: the checklist

1. **Who is the defendant?** The State, a ministry, an embassy (the State itself), an official in his official capacity, or a "separate entity" (State-owned company, agency). Name the right one.
2. **Which exception?** Commercial transaction (s. 3), Israeli employee (s. 4), tort in Israel (s. 5), property in Israel (s. 6), waiver (ss. 9–11). If none applies, stop: the claim will be dismissed.
3. **Forum.** The ordinary Israeli rules (lesson 15.1): magistrates' court by amount, labour court for employment (exclusive), small-claims court where the rules allow (⚠️ check whether a small-claims court will accept service through the Foreign Ministry; in practice ordinary procedure is safer).
4. **Service.** Through the Foreign Ministry (s. 13(א)); ask the court registry for directions. Expect delay; diary the State's response period ([current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md)).
5. **Default.** If the State does not answer, the court must still be satisfied there is no immunity (s. 14), so your claim must set out the exception and its facts.
6. **Collection.** Identify commercial assets in Israel (s. 16). Embassy bank accounts used for the mission are usually protected.

## Worked example

Fatma Abu-Rabia, a Bedouin resident of Rahat, worked for three years as a driver for a foreign State's embassy in Tel Aviv. Her contract was signed with the embassy; she was dismissed without a hearing or severance pay.

1. **Immunity rule:** the embassy is the foreign State (s. 1), immune unless an exception applies (s. 2).
2. **Employment exception (s. 4):** her claim is within the labour court's exclusive jurisdiction; the work was done in Israel; she was an Israeli citizen when the claim arose; she is not a national of the foreign State. All conditions are met: no immunity.
3. **Not a commercial-transaction case:** s. 4(ג) says employment claims are governed only by s. 4, so she does not argue s. 3.
4. **Procedure:** she files in the Regional Labour Court in her own name; the claim is served through the Foreign Ministry; the Attorney General is notified when immunity is raised (s. 19).
5. **Collection risk:** if she wins and the embassy does not pay, its mission accounts are probably immune from execution (s. 15). The labour court and the Foreign Ministry channel usually produce payment, but she notes the risk before spending money on the case.

Contrast: had her colleague been a citizen of the foreign State posted from abroad and not resident in Israel, s. 4(ב) would preserve immunity.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- A real or invented dispute with a foreign State, an embassy, a State-owned company or a foreign official (a lease, a job, a purchase, a car accident).
- The Foreign States Immunity Law on Wikisource. 40 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Classify the defendant (5 min)** under s. 1: State, authority, official, or separate entity.
2. **Test each exception (15 min):** ss. 3–11, one line each, applied to your facts.
3. **Plan procedure (10 min):** forum, service through the Foreign Ministry, response period, s. 14 default rule, AG notice.
4. **Assess collection (5 min):** what assets in Israel are reachable under s. 16?
5. **Write the immunity paragraph (5 min)** for your statement of claim, in Hebrew: the exception, the facts that meet it, and the request.

### Template

[Statement of claim, magistrates' court](../../templates/statement-of-claim-magistrates.md): add your immunity paragraph after the jurisdiction section.

### How you know it worked

- You named one specific exception and matched each of its conditions to a fact.
- Your plan says how the claim will be served and that the AG is notified.
- You considered execution immunity before deciding to sue.

## Without a lawyer

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Act in your own name only. This is not legal advice — check current rules before filing.

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes, an individual may sue a foreign State in his own name in the competent court, and the labour court allows self-representation ([can I act without a lawyer](../../references/can-i-act-without-a-lawyer.md)). A company must generally act through a lawyer. Service on a foreign State goes through the Foreign Ministry, not by you personally.

### Where and how

- File on Net HaMishpat as usual (lesson [08.8](../module-08/lesson-08.md)), and ask the registry for directions on service under s. 13.
- Fees and response periods: [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).
- Free help: [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md) (workers' rights organisations help embassy employees).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- The foreign State raises immunity and the facts are mixed (sovereign and commercial elements).
- You need to attach assets of a foreign State or its central bank.
- The defendant is a diplomat personally, or an international organisation (separate immunity laws apply).

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suing the ambassador personally for the embassy's debt | Diplomatic immunity; claim dismissed | Sue the State and plead an exception | Identify who contracted (Edelson) |
| Arguing the purpose of the act instead of its nature | The court finds a sovereign purpose | Argue the legal nature of the transaction | Quote s. 1 "עסקה מסחרית" and Edelson |
| Relying on s. 3 for an employment claim | s. 4(ג) excludes it | Plead s. 4 and its conditions | Read s. 4 first for any job dispute |
| Serving the embassy directly | Service defective; default judgment set aside | Re-serve through the Foreign Ministry | Ask the registry for s. 13 directions |
| Suing without a collection plan | A paper judgment | Look for commercial assets (s. 16) | Assess execution immunity before filing |

## Review

- Statehood: population, territory, government, capacity (Montevideo); recognition is political but has legal effects and limits.
- On disputed recognitions (Palestine, Somaliland), state each side's position and the current practice; no single body decides.
- Israeli criminal law reaches offences abroad on protective, passive-personality, nationality and treaty-universal bases (Penal Law ss. 13–17).
- Foreign States are immune from Israeli civil courts except under the 2008 Law's exceptions; the nature of the act decides (Edelson).
- Immunity from execution and diplomatic immunity are separate hurdles; serving officials enjoy personal immunity (Arrest Warrant), and sovereign military acts stay immune (Germany v. Italy).
