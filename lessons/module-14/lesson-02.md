# 14.2 · Discretion: relevant considerations, equality and reasonableness after the 2023 amendment

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take a decision that was within the authority's power and test how the power was used: did the official weigh only relevant considerations, treat equals equally, and reach a result a reasonable authority could reach? You will also know exactly where reasonableness review stands after the 2023 amendment and the January 2024 ruling, and be able to state each side's legal case.

## Why it matters

Most administrative decisions are within power. The legal fight is about discretion (שיקול דעת): the choice an official makes among lawful options. Israeli law developed a set of grounds to review that choice: extraneous considerations, failure to consider relevant ones, discrimination, arbitrariness and unreasonableness. These grounds are what you will use in nearly every letter and petition in this module.

Reasonableness is also the ground at the centre of the 2023–2026 constitutional dispute (lesson [06.6](../module-06/lesson-06.md)). That lesson covered the constitutional question, whether the Court may review a Basic Law. This lesson covers the administrative question: what reasonableness review actually does to a decision, what would remain without it, and how a petitioner should argue in its light.

## The law

There is no statute that lists the grounds for reviewing discretion. They are judge-made and apply in the Administrative Affairs Court through s. 8 of the Administrative Affairs Courts Law ("לפי העילות... שלפיהם דן בית המשפט העליון בשבתו כבית משפט גבוה לצדק"; lesson [08.5](../module-08/lesson-05.md)). The one statutory text on reasonableness is the amendment that tried to remove it.

### The reasonableness amendment (2023), declared void

> על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד זה, מי שבידו סמכות שפיטה על פי דין, לרבות בית המשפט העליון בשבתו כבית משפט גבוה לצדק, לא ידון בעניין סבירות ההחלטה של הממשלה, של ראש הממשלה או של שר אחר, ולא ייתן צו בעניין כאמור; בסעיף זה, ”החלטה“ – כל החלטה, לרבות בענייני מינויים או החלטה להימנע מהפעלת כל סמכות.

*Gist:* No court would hear or order anything on the reasonableness of decisions of the Government, the Prime Minister or ministers, including appointments and refusals to act. It applied only to the elected tier; officials, municipalities and other bodies were never covered. Declared void on 1 January 2024. ([Basic Law: The Judiciary s. 15(ד1), shown struck through](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה))

### The ruling, in the Court's own summary

> בית המשפט העליון הוסיף וקבע בדעת רוב (8 מתוך 15 שופטים) כי יש להכריז על בטלותו של תיקון מס' 3 לחוק־יסוד: השפיטה אשר שלל באופן גורף את הביקורת השיפוטית על סבירות החלטות הממשלה, ראש הממשלה והשרים.

*Gist:* Twelve of fifteen justices held that Basic Laws are reviewable in extreme cases; eight held this amendment void. ([official summary, IDI copy](https://www.idi.org.il/media/22484/summary-of-verdict-of-supreme-court-regarding-reasonableness-bill-january-1-2024.pdf))

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 6163/92 אייזנברג נ׳ שר הבינוי והשיכון (23.3.1993)

The Government appointed a former security-service official as director-general of a ministry. He had been involved in covering up the killing of captured terrorists (the "Bus 300" affair) and in false testimony, for which he had been pardoned without conviction. The Court held that an appointing authority must take into account a candidate's criminal past where there is reasonable evidence of it, even without conviction and despite a pardon, and that this candidate was so unsuitable for a senior civil-service post that the appointment was annulled.

Why it matters: it is the classic case of a relevant consideration (integrity of the public service) given too little weight, and the origin of appointment review that the 2023 amendment targeted. Supporters say it protects the civil service from unfit appointees; critics say it lets judges substitute their view of suitability for the Government's. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/143)

### בג״ץ 6698/95 קעדאן נ׳ מינהל מקרקעי ישראל (8.3.2000)

The Israel Land Administration allocated land to the Jewish Agency, which set up the community of Katzir through a cooperative that admitted only Jews. An Arab couple who wanted to build a home there were refused. The Court held that the State may not allocate land, directly or through a third party, on the basis of religion or nationality, and that the State must reconsider the couple's application on the basis of equality.

Why it matters: an authority cannot escape the duty of equality by acting through an intermediary (compare the third-party point in lesson 14.1). The case remains debated: critics argue it limited the State's ability to pursue settlement policy through the national institutions; supporters see it as a basic statement of civic equality. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/218)

### עע״מ 343/09 הבית הפתוח בירושלים לגאווה ולסובלנות נ׳ עיריית ירושלים (17.3.2010)

An LGBT community centre was refused municipal funding for several years under criteria it argued were designed to exclude it. The Supreme Court held that a local authority must distribute support on clear, transparent and equal criteria, like the State under s. 3א of the Budget Foundations Law; that ignoring a relevant consideration is equivalent to weighing an irrelevant one; and that a decision based on relevant grounds can still be unreasonable if the outcome denies substantive equality, which can be shown by statistics where motives cannot be proved.

Why it matters: it gives you the structure of an equality argument for any funding or allocation decision, and tells you that you may prove discrimination by results. [English translation](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/216)

### בג״ץ 5658/23 התנועה למען איכות השלטון בישראל נ׳ הכנסת (1.1.2024): the administrative core

Lesson 06.6 covered the constitutional holding. The administrative core is how the justices read "reasonableness". The majority read the amendment as barring all reasonableness review of the elected tier, including review of decisions that are absurd or extremely unreasonable, and held that this left whole areas (notably appointments and dismissals of gatekeepers) without effective review. Justices Wilner, Stein and Canfy-Steinitz read it narrowly, as removing only the "balancing" reasonableness developed in בג״ץ 389/80 דפי זהב בע״מ נ׳ רשות השידור (1980), leaving review of absurd decisions and every other ground (authority, extraneous considerations, arbitrariness, bad faith, discrimination, proportionality). Justice Kasher held that the harm, though real, did not reach the threshold for voiding a Basic Law; Justices Sohlberg, Mintz and Elron would not review it at all, Elron adding that the challenge was premature.

Why it matters: the split is an administrative-law debate about what reasonableness adds to the other grounds. Both readings agree that extreme unreasonableness is part of Israeli law. [Official summary](https://www.idi.org.il/media/22484/summary-of-verdict-of-supreme-court-regarding-reasonableness-bill-january-1-2024.pdf); [full judgment](https://www.idi.org.il/media/22483/full-verdict-of-supreme-court-regarding-reasonableness-bill-january-1-2024.pdf)

## How it works in practice

### The discretion checklist

1. **Purpose.** What is the empowering law for? A consideration is relevant if it serves that purpose (Eisenberg: integrity of the service; Solodkin, lesson 14.3: local character).
2. **Extraneous considerations.** Politics, personal ties, punishing a critic, budget saving where the law forbids it. If one extraneous consideration had real influence, the decision is usually void even if relevant ones were also present.
3. **Relevant considerations missing.** List what the authority should have weighed (your hardship, the history, the public interest). Ask for the minutes or reasons (lesson 14.4) to see what was weighed.
4. **Equality.** Who else was treated differently? Is there a relevant difference? Were the criteria published, clear and applied to all? Does the outcome show a pattern (Open House)?
5. **Weight.** Was a relevant consideration given grossly too little or too much weight? This is reasonableness in the balancing sense.
6. **Extreme unreasonableness or arbitrariness.** Is the result one no reasonable authority could reach?

### Reasonableness in October 2026

| Question | Answer as of 17 October 2026 |
|---|---|
| Is s. 15(ד1) in force? | No. Declared void on 1.1.2024 (8–7). |
| Is reasonableness review of ministers available? | Yes, as before July 2023. |
| Was it ever limited for officials, municipalities, committees? | No; the amendment covered only the Government, the Prime Minister and ministers. |
| Any new legislation on reasonableness? | None found on 17.10.2026 (⚠️ check the Knesset database before relying on this; elections are on 27.10.2026). |
| Practical advice | Argue reasonableness, but also every other ground that fits, so that your case does not depend on its scope. |

### Each side's legal argument

*For narrowing review:* "balancing" reasonableness has no fixed standard and lets a court redo the minister's weighing; ministers answer to the Knesset and voters; extraneous considerations, discrimination, proportionality and extreme unreasonableness already catch abuse; other common-law systems use a narrower test. *For keeping it:* Israel has a single chamber controlled by the Government, so judicial review is a main check; some harms (an unfit appointment, an arbitrary refusal to act) are caught only by reasonableness; reasonableness works mainly in advance, as the standard officials and legal advisers apply when they decide. Both sides accept review for absurd decisions.

## Worked example

Rabbi Shmuel Katz runs a Haredi girls' after-school programme in Beit Shemesh. The municipality's sports department publishes criteria for supporting youth activities: "programmes open to the general public, with at least 40% of activity in competitive sport". His application is rejected.

1. **Authority.** The municipality may support institutions (lesson 14.1); no issue.
2. **Relevant consideration.** Encouraging youth activity is the purpose. Competitive sport may be relevant to a sports budget, but is the 40% rule tied to the purpose or designed to exclude?
3. **Equality.** He asks under the FOI Law (lesson 14.7) for the list of supported bodies. Of 30 supported groups, none serves Haredi girls, though they are a large share of the city's children. Following Open House, the statistical outcome supports a claim of indirect discrimination.
4. **Reasonableness.** The criteria ignore a relevant consideration (the population's needs and its religious constraints on mixed competition) and so produce an unequal outcome.
5. **Remedy he asks for.** Not money for this year alone, but reconsideration of the criteria and his application, which is what courts usually order.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- A decision that allocated something (a grant, a place, a permit, a benefit) to some and not to you, or Rabbi Katz's case.
- The authority's published criteria if any.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Purpose (10 min).** Write the purpose of the empowering law or budget item in one sentence.
2. **Considerations table (15 min).** Two columns: considerations the authority used; considerations it should have used. Mark each relevant or extraneous, with a reason.
3. **Equality test (15 min).** Who was treated better? Is there a relevant difference? Are criteria published and equal? What does the outcome show?
4. **Paragraph (10 min).** Draft the "discretion" paragraph of your demand: the strongest two grounds, with one case each. Then add one sentence on extreme unreasonableness in case balancing review is contested.

### Template

[Letter to a public authority](../../templates/letter-to-authority.md) for the demand; [argument from principle](../../templates/argument-from-principle.md) to structure the equality argument.

### How you know it worked

- Each consideration is tied to the law's purpose.
- Your equality argument names a comparator group and a criterion.
- Your paragraph would survive even under the narrow reading of reasonableness.

## Without a lawyer

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Act in your own name only. This is not legal advice — check current rules before filing.

### Can you do this yourself?

Yes. Writing to the authority, asking for its criteria and the list of recipients, and filing an FOI request are all things you may do yourself. A petition in your own name is possible (lesson 14.9).

### Where and how

- Ask for the criteria and the decision minutes in writing; use the FOI Law for data on other recipients (lesson 14.7).
- Funding and support decisions of a municipality go to the Administrative Affairs Court (First Schedule, local authorities); Government-level decisions to the HCJ (lesson [06.8](../module-06/lesson-08.md)).
- Fees and deadlines: [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your claim is discrimination on a large scale (a whole community or sector): an NGO or clinic with experience is better placed.
- The authority argues the decision is policy at Government level, where petitions are heard in the HCJ.
- You need to cross-examine officials to prove motive.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relying on "unreasonable" alone | The case turns on the contested scope of one ground | Add extraneous considerations, equality, arbitrariness | Use the six-step checklist |
| Claiming discrimination with no comparator | The court finds no unequal treatment | Get data on recipients by FOI | Name who was treated better and why that matters |
| Saying the Court "abolished" or "saved" reasonableness | Inaccurate; it voided one amendment | State: s. 15(ד1) void, review as before | Use the status table |
| Asking the court to grant the benefit itself | Usually refused; the court sends it back | Ask for reconsideration on proper criteria | Frame the remedy as a new decision |
| Treating your political view as a legal argument | The court ignores it; credibility drops | Rewrite as a consideration tied to the law's purpose | Ask: which statute makes this relevant? |

## Review

- Discretion is reviewed for extraneous or missing considerations, discrimination, arbitrariness and unreasonableness; the grounds are judge-made and apply in the Administrative Affairs Court through s. 8.
- Eisenberg: relevant considerations include integrity; Ka'adan: no discrimination through intermediaries; Open House: clear, equal criteria, and discrimination can be shown by outcomes.
- The 2023 amendment barred reasonableness review of the Government and ministers only; it was declared void on 1.1.2024, so review continues as before.
- The minority's narrow reading would have removed only "balancing" reasonableness; all justices accept review of absurd decisions.
- Argue every ground that fits, so that your case does not depend on the scope of one.
