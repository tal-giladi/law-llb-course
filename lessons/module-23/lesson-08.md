# 23.8 · Confessions and corroboration in criminal cases

A confession was once called the "queen of evidence": who would admit a crime he did not commit? Experience with false confessions taught otherwise, and Israeli law now surrounds confessions with two safeguards: a confession is admissible only if it was free and voluntary, and a conviction based on it needs "something extra". This lesson covers those safeguards, the wider family of corroboration requirements (strengthening for accomplices and recanting witnesses, full corroboration for state witnesses and child-investigator evidence), the rules on the accused's silence, and the civil-law counterpart in s. 54. It completes the criminal side of evidence law before module 24 takes up criminal procedure.

## Why it matters

> [!WARNING]
> This lesson explains how courts treat confessions and silence. It is not advice on what to say to the police. If you are summoned for questioning or arrested, you have the right to remain silent and to consult a lawyer: use them, and see module 24.

Confessions decide many criminal cases, and false confessions have caused some of Israel's most debated miscarriages of justice. In 1994 the Minister of Justice set up a committee headed by Justice Eliezer Goldberg to examine conviction on the basis of a confession alone and the grounds for a retrial; its retrial recommendations were enacted in the Courts Law, and its work on confessions shaped the debate on the "something extra" requirement ([Hebrew Wikipedia, ועדת גולדברג](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/הוועדה_לעניין_הרשעה_על_סמך_הודאה_בלבד_ולעניין_העילות_למשפט_חוזר), secondary).

The tension is real on both sides. Investigators and victims point out that many guilty people confess, that a true confession may be the only evidence in crimes without witnesses, and that excessive suspicion of confessions lets offenders go free. Defence lawyers and scholars point to the pressure of interrogation, to vulnerable suspects (minors, people with disabilities) and to cases where a confession contained details the suspect could have learned from investigators. Courts have answered with procedural safeguards (recorded interrogations, the free-and-voluntary test, the Yissacharov doctrine) and evidential ones (the additions required for conviction).

## The law

### Admissibility of a confession: s. 12

> (א) עדות על הודיית הנאשם כי עבר עבירה, תהא קבילה רק אם הביא התובע עדות בדבר הנסיבות שבהן ניתנה ההודיה ובית המשפט ראה שההודיה היתה חפשית ומרצון.

*Gist:* Evidence of the accused's confession is admissible only if the prosecution brings evidence of the circumstances in which it was given and the court finds it was free and voluntary. ([s. 12(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הראיות#סעיף_12))

The person who took the confession may prove the circumstances by affidavit instead of testimony if the represented accused's lawyer waives cross-examination, or, for a misdemeanour or contravention, if the unrepresented accused, after the court explains his right, confirms he read the confession, does not deny it was free and voluntary and waives cross-examination (s. 12(ב)). The statement itself is proved under s. 11 by whoever heard it or was present when it was signed (lesson [23.3](lesson-03.md)).

When the defence disputes admissibility, the court holds a **trial within a trial** (משפט זוטא): the prosecution proves how the confession was obtained, the defence may call evidence, and the court rules before deciding guilt. Since Yissacharov the court asks two questions: was the confession free and voluntary under s. 12, and, separately, should it be excluded under the general doctrine now in s. 56א (lesson [23.5](lesson-05.md))?

### Recording interrogations

Interrogations of suspects in listed serious offences at a police station must be recorded on video, with exceptions for technical failure or a stated practical need; other interrogations may be recorded by video, audio or in writing ([Interrogation of Suspects Law ss. 4, 7](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_סדר_הדין_הפלילי_(חקירת_חשודים))). The recording is often decisive in the trial within a trial.

### The three levels of additional evidence

| Level | What it is | Required for | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Something extra (דבר מה נוסף) | Any evidence, even slight, that confirms the confession's truth; the more reliable the confession, the less is needed | Conviction on the accused's out-of-court confession | Case law |
| Strengthening (חיזוק) | Evidence from another source that confirms the testimony on a point; it need not implicate the accused directly | An accomplice's sole testimony (s. 54א(א)); a recanting witness's statement (s. 10א(ד)); a young child's sole testimony (s. 55(ב)) | Evidence Ordinance |
| Corroboration (סיוע) | Independent evidence that implicates the accused on a point in dispute | A state witness (s. 54א(א)); child-investigator evidence (Child Protection Law s. 11) | Statute |

> בית המשפט לא ירשיע נאשם על סמך עדותו היחידה של שותפו לעבירה, אלא אם מצא בחומר הראיות דבר לחיזוקה; ואולם אם היה השותף עד מדינה – טעונה עדותו סיוע…

*Gist:* No conviction on an accomplice's sole testimony without something to strengthen it; a state witness (an accomplice testifying for the prosecution after a benefit was given or promised) needs corroboration. ([s. 54א(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הראיות#סעיף_54א))

In sexual offences, a conviction on the victim's sole testimony is allowed, but the court must state in the verdict what led it to rely on that testimony alone (s. 54א(ב)).

### The accused's silence: Criminal Procedure Law ss. 152, 161, 162

The accused need not answer the charge, but silence in reply to the indictment or the court's questions may strengthen the prosecution's evidence, and the court must explain this (s. 152(ב)). At the end of the prosecution case the accused may testify (and be cross-examined) or not testify (s. 161).

> (א) הימנעות הנאשם מהעיד עשויה לשמש חיזוק למשקל הראיות של התביעה וכן סיוע לראיות התביעה במקום שדרוש להן סיוע…

*Gist:* An accused's refusal to testify may strengthen the prosecution's evidence and serve as corroboration where corroboration is needed, but not for child-investigator evidence or evidence under the law on investigating people with disabilities; and it may not count against an accused whose disability, shown by an expert opinion, caused him not to testify. ([s. 162](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_סדר_הדין_הפלילי#סעיף_162))

A fact the accused admits is proved against him unless the court declines the admission or he withdraws it (s. 154). If the prosecution has not proved guilt even prima facie, the court acquits at the end of its case (s. 158).

### Civil counterpart: s. 54

> פסק בית משפט במשפט אזרחי באחד המקרים שלהלן על פי עדות יחידה שאין לה סיוע, והעדות אינה הודיית בעל דין, יפרט בהחלטתו מה הניע אותו להסתפק בעדות זו…

*Gist:* A civil court may decide on a single uncorroborated witness, but must give reasons when the witness is a child under 14, a party or a close relative of a party, a person interested in the result, or when the claim is against an estate, a minor, a person with a mental illness or an absentee. ([s. 54](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_הראיות#סעיף_54)) In a claim against an estate, your own word about an oral loan to the deceased will need strong support.

## Leading cases

### רע״פ 4142/04 מילשטיין נ׳ התובע הצבאי הראשי (14.12.2006)

A soldier confessed to drug use in his police interrogation, then pleaded not guilty and exercised his right not to testify; the military courts treated his silence as the "something extra" for the confession. The Supreme Court (Justice Arbel and President Emeritus Barak for the majority) held that, as a rule, an accused's silence at trial should not be "something extra" for his out-of-court confession. The court has a narrow discretion to treat it as such only if three conditions are met: the confession is logical, consistent, clear and detailed; the court can rule out that the accused confessed falsely because of internal pressure; and his silence at trial is not the result of internal or external pressure or an innocent motive. Justice Levy, in the minority, would have allowed it mainly where the interrogation was video-recorded.

**Why it matters:** silence can corroborate under s. 162, but courts are cautious about letting silence prop up a confession, because the two together may hide a false confession. English translation: [Versa / LARC](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/260/).

### ע״פ 5121/98 יששכרוב נ׳ התובע הצבאי הראשי (on s. 12)

Besides creating the general exclusion doctrine, the court held that failure to warn of the right to a lawyer does not automatically make a confession involuntary under s. 12; a confession is excluded under s. 12 when the improper means seriously and significantly harmed the suspect's autonomy of will and free choice. Where s. 12 is not met, the general doctrine may still exclude it. English translation: [Versa / LARC](https://larc.cardozo.yu.edu/iscp-opinions/411/); s. 12 discussion summarised in [Hebrew Wikipedia](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/פסק_דין_יששכרוב_נגד_התובע_הצבאי_הראשי) (secondary).

## How it works in practice

### Testing a confession, step by step

1. **Was it proved?** Who heard or recorded it (s. 11)? Is the recording complete?
2. **Free and voluntary?** Threats, promises, deprivation of sleep or food, exploitation of a vulnerable suspect (s. 12).
3. **Excluded anyway?** Breach of the right to a lawyer, of the warning, of recording duties; the s. 56א factors.
4. **Weight.** Internal test: is it coherent, detailed, consistent with known facts? External test: does it contain details only the offender would know?
5. **Something extra.** Is there independent evidence confirming it? The more doubt about the confession, the more is needed.

### For a victim or witness

If you are a complainant, your testimony may need strengthening or corroboration depending on who you are and the offence; the prosecution plans this. Preserve anything that confirms your account at the time: messages you sent right after the event, a doctor's record, people you told (lesson [23.3](lesson-03.md) on spontaneous statements).

## Worked example

Two young men, Noam (20) and Adi (21), are charged with breaking into a school in Afula. Noam confessed at the station after four hours of questioning late at night; Adi denies everything. Noam later turns state witness against Adi in exchange for a plea deal.

1. **Noam's confession against himself.** In a trial within a trial the defence argues it was not free and voluntary: long questioning, late at night, a tired suspect. The video recording shows calm questioning and Noam offering details (the broken window at the back, the stolen projector's brand). The court admits it under s. 12 and finds no ground under s. 56א.
2. **Something extra.** The projector was found in Noam's room: more than enough.
3. **Noam's testimony against Adi.** Noam is a state witness (a benefit was promised), so his testimony needs **corroboration** (s. 54א(א)): independent evidence implicating Adi.
4. **Corroboration found?** CCTV from a petrol station shows Adi and Noam walking towards the school ten minutes before the alarm. That implicates Adi on a disputed point.
5. **Adi's silence.** Adi does not testify. Under s. 162 his silence may also serve as corroboration, since the prosecution's evidence calls for an answer.
6. **Result.** Both convicted; the court explains each addition it relied on.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The worked example, or a reported criminal case summary you find on Versa / LARC.
- Evidence Ordinance ss. 10א, 12, 54א, 55 and Criminal Procedure Law ss. 152, 161, 162 open.
- About 35 minutes.

### Steps

1. **Map (10 min).** List every item of prosecution evidence and its source: confession, accomplice, state witness, child, complainant, physical evidence.
2. **Requirement (10 min).** For each testimonial item, write which addition is needed (none, something extra, strengthening, corroboration) and the section or rule.
3. **Match (10 min).** For each requirement, identify the evidence that meets it, or write "missing".
4. **Silence (5 min).** Write two sentences on whether the accused's silence could be used, and how Milstein limits it.

### Template

None.

### How you know it worked

- Every testimonial item has the right level of addition and its source.
- You can explain the difference between strengthening and corroboration with an example.
- You can state the three Milstein conditions.

### Self-check

- [ ] I know a confession is admissible only if proved free and voluntary (s. 12).
- [ ] I know a conviction on a confession needs "something extra".
- [ ] I know a state witness needs corroboration, an ordinary accomplice only strengthening.
- [ ] I know when a civil court must explain reliance on a single witness (s. 54).

## Without a lawyer

> [!WARNING]
> Never decide alone whether to give a statement, to confess or to stay silent in a criminal investigation. Ask for a lawyer; if you cannot afford one, ask the court about the Public Defender.

### Can you do this yourself?

No, in practice, for confessions. An accused may represent himself in principle, but a trial within a trial over a confession is one of the most technical parts of criminal procedure, and many accused are entitled to a public defender (module 24). As a complainant or witness, you do not need a lawyer to testify, and you have rights under the Crime Victims' Rights Law (lesson [05.7](../module-05/lesson-07.md)). In a civil claim against an estate (s. 54), you may act for yourself.

- You have been questioned, or summoned for questioning, as a suspect.
- Someone has given a statement implicating you.
- You are a minor or a parent of a minor being questioned.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treating a confession as conclusive | Missing the s. 12 and s. 56א challenges | Request the recording and challenge in a trial within a trial | Always test admissibility and weight |
| Confusing strengthening with corroboration | Wrong analysis of a state witness's evidence | Look for independent evidence implicating the accused | Use the three-level table |
| Assuming silence at trial is always harmless | Silence may corroborate (s. 162) | Discuss the choice with a lawyer before the defence case | Understand s. 161–162 explanations |
| Suing an estate on an oral promise with no other evidence | Court requires reasons, often rejects (s. 54(4)) | Find documents, bank transfers or witnesses | Put loans in writing |
| A complainant deleting messages sent right after the event | Lose strengthening evidence | Ask the recipients for copies | Preserve everything from the time |

## Review

- A confession is admissible only if the prosecution proves it free and voluntary (s. 12), decided in a trial within a trial; Yissacharov's general doctrine (s. 56א) applies too.
- Conviction on a confession needs "something extra"; the more doubtful the confession, the more is needed.
- Strengthening for accomplices, recanting witnesses and young children (ss. 54א(א), 10א(ד), 55(ב)); corroboration for state witnesses and child-investigator evidence (s. 54א(א); Child Protection Law s. 11).
- Silence in reply to the charge or at trial may strengthen or corroborate (Criminal Procedure Law ss. 152(ב), 162), but Milstein limits silence as "something extra" for a confession.
- In civil cases a single uncorroborated witness of a listed kind requires written reasons (s. 54).
