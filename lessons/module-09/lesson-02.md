# 09.2 · Legal positivism: Austin, Kelsen, Hart

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain the positivist answer to the question "what makes a rule law?", compare the three classic versions (Austin's commands of a sovereign, Kelsen's hierarchy of norms resting on a basic norm, and Hart's union of primary and secondary rules), and use them to trace why an Israeli by-law, regulation or statute is valid. You will see that the Supreme Court used Kelsen and Hart by name in Bank Mizrahi, the case that founded judicial review of Knesset laws.

## Why it matters

Legal positivism is the working theory of most lawyers, even those who have never heard the word. When a lawyer asks "is this rule valid?", the questions are positivist ones: who made it, under what power, published how, and does it conflict with a higher norm? The answer does not depend on whether the rule is wise or just.

The core of positivism is the **separation thesis**: what the law *is* and what it *ought to be* are different questions. A positivist can think a law is terrible and still say it is the law; indeed, positivists argue that this separation is what makes clear moral criticism of law possible.

Israel's legal system is easy to describe in positivist terms. Every norm can be traced upward: a municipal by-law to the Municipalities Ordinance, a regulation to its enabling statute, a statute to the Knesset's power, the Basic Laws to the Knesset's constituent authority, and that authority to the founding acts of 1948. Lesson 06.1 showed that the last step is disputed. This lesson gives you the theory behind the dispute.

## The law

### Austin: commands of a sovereign

John Austin's lectures (1832) are public domain. He defined law by its source:

> "Every law or rule (taken with the largest signification which can be given to the term properly) is a command."

*Gist:* A law is a general command, backed by a threat of a sanction, issued by a sovereign: a person or body that the bulk of society habitually obeys and that obeys no one else. [Austin, The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, Lecture I (1861 ed.)](https://archive.org/details/sc_0000348991_00000000363937)

And he stated the separation thesis in the form every textbook quotes:

> "The existence of a law is one thing: its merits or demerits are another thing."

*Gist:* Whether a law exists is one inquiry; whether it is good is a different one. [Lecture V](https://archive.org/details/sc_0000348991_00000000363937)

### Kelsen: a hierarchy of norms

Hans Kelsen (Pure Theory of Law, 1934, revised 1960) rejected Austin's psychology of fear and habit. In paraphrase: a legal norm is valid not because someone is obeyed, but because a higher norm authorised its creation. A regulation is valid because a statute empowers the minister; the statute is valid because the constitution empowers the legislature; and the historically first constitution is valid because we presuppose a **basic norm** (Grundnorm): "one ought to behave as the first constitution prescribes". The basic norm is not enacted by anyone; it is the assumption that makes it possible to read a system of coercive orders as law. Kelsen's theory is "pure" because it excludes morality, politics and sociology from the question of validity.

### Hart: primary and secondary rules

H. L. A. Hart (The Concept of Law, 1961) argued that Austin's model fits a gunman's orders better than a legal system. In paraphrase:

- Law is a union of **primary rules** (rules that impose duties: do not steal, pay your tax) and **secondary rules** (rules about the rules).
- There are three kinds of secondary rule: the **rule of recognition**, which tells officials how to identify valid law; **rules of change**, which say how laws are made and repealed; and **rules of adjudication**, which empower courts to decide disputes.
- The rule of recognition exists as a social fact: it is the practice of judges and officials who accept it from the **internal point of view**, treating it as a standard they must follow, not merely a habit.
- Legal language has an **open texture**. Every rule has a core of clear cases and a penumbra of doubtful ones. In the penumbra the rule runs out and the judge must exercise discretion, in effect making new law.

### The Israeli chain of validity in statutes

> המשפט שהיה קיים בארץ־ישראל ביום ה׳ באייר תש״ח (14 במאי 1948) יעמוד בתקפו, עד כמה שאין בו משום סתירה לפקודה זו או לחוקים האחרים שיינתנו על ידי מועצת המדינה הזמנית או על פיה, ובשינויים הנובעים מתוך הקמת המדינה ורשויותיה.

*Gist:* The law in force on 14 May 1948 stays in force unless it conflicts with the Ordinance or with laws of the Provisional Council of State. This is a rule of change and continuity: it is why some Ottoman and Mandate law is still valid. [Law and Administration Ordinance 1948, s. 11](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_סדרי_השלטון_והמשפט)

> מועצה רשאית להתקין חוקי עזר כדי לאפשר לעיריה ביצוע הדברים שהיא נדרשת או מוסמכת לעשותם על פי הפקודה או כל דין אחר [...]

*Gist:* A municipal council may make by-laws to carry out what the Ordinance or another law requires or empowers it to do. A by-law outside that power is invalid however sensible it is. [Municipalities Ordinance, s. 250](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_העיריות)

> בענייני שפיטה אין מרות על מי שבידו סמכות שפיטה, זולת מרותו של הדין.

*Gist:* In judicial matters a judge is subject to no authority but the law. [Basic Law: The Judiciary, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה)

> (ב) הלכה שנפסקה בבית המשפט העליון מחייבת כל בית משפט, זולת בית המשפט העליון.

*Gist:* A Supreme Court holding binds every court except the Supreme Court itself. This is a rule of adjudication and part of the rule of recognition: it tells you which judicial statements count as law. [Basic Law: The Judiciary, s. 20(ב)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה)

### Positivising a value

The sequel to Yeredor (lesson 09.1) is a positivist move. In 1985 the Knesset enacted what is now s. 7א of Basic Law: The Knesset:

> רשימת מועמדים לא תשתתף בבחירות לכנסת ולא יהיה אדם מועמד בבחירות לכנסת, אם יש במטרותיה או במעשיה של הרשימה או במעשיו של האדם [...] אחד מאלה: (1) שלילת קיומה של מדינת ישראל כמדינה יהודית ודמוקרטית; (2) הסתה לגזענות; (3) תמיכה במאבק מזוין [...]

*Gist:* A list or candidate whose aims or acts deny Israel's existence as a Jewish and democratic state, incite to racism, or support armed struggle against Israel may not run. A ground that a justice once found in "supra-constitutional rules" is now written law. [Basic Law: The Knesset, s. 7א(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הכנסת)

## Leading cases

### ע״א 6821/93 בנק המזרחי המאוחד בע״מ נ׳ מגדל כפר שיתופי, פ״ד מט(4) 221 (1995): the theory behind the result

Lesson 06.1 covered the holding: Basic Laws are supreme over ordinary statutes. Here, read how the justices justified it, because they used positivist theory openly.

- **President Barak** offered three models for the Knesset's constituent authority. The first, *constitutional continuity*, is Kelsenian: he cited Kelsen's Pure Theory and said Israel's basic norm is that the Provisional Council of State is the supreme authority, from which constituent power passed to the Knesset. The second is Hartian: he cited The Concept of Law on primary and secondary norms and the pre-eminent place of the rule of recognition, and held that Israel's rule of recognition, reflecting the community's views, is that the Knesset has both constituent and legislative authority. He stressed that this is an objective finding about the system, not a judge's personal preference. The third model is "the best explanation" of Israel's constitutional history, which looks ahead to Dworkin (lesson 09.3).
- **Former President Shamgar** also cited Kelsen, for the point that one organ can perform both ordinary and constituent functions, and Hart, for the point that a parliament able to bind itself expresses sovereignty as well as one that cannot.
- **Justice Cheshin** disagreed on the theory: in his view, the facts of Israeli practice did not show a shared understanding that the Knesset had adopted a constitution.

Why it matters: Israel's most important constitutional case turned on a question of legal philosophy, namely what Israel's rule of recognition is, and the justices disagreed about a social fact, not only about values. [English translation, including all opinions](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/united-mizrahi-bank-v-migdal-cooperative-village)

### ע״ב 1/65 ירדור נ׳ יושב־ראש ועדת הבחירות המרכזית לכנסת השישית (1965): the positivist dissent

Read Justice Cohn's dissent (lesson 09.1) again as a positivist text. He did not say that the al-Ard list was good or that the State had no right to defend itself. He said that the Committee's power came from the Elections Law, which did not include the power claimed, and that a restriction on a fundamental right needs a statute. That is the separation thesis applied: the question was what the law authorised, not what morality required. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/yeredor-v-chairman-central-elections-committee-sixth-knesset)

## How it works in practice

### The three theories side by side

| Question | Austin | Kelsen | Hart |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is law? | Commands of a sovereign backed by sanctions | A system of norms, each authorised by a higher norm | A union of primary and secondary rules |
| Where does the chain end? | The sovereign, habitually obeyed and obeying no one | The presupposed basic norm | The rule of recognition, a social practice of officials |
| Role of morality | None in validity | None ("pure" theory) | None necessary, though a system may include moral criteria (soft positivism, Hart's postscript) |
| Hard cases | Not addressed in detail | The judge creates a lower norm within the frame of the higher one | In the penumbra the judge has discretion |
| Main weakness | Cannot explain power-conferring rules, or the continuity of law when the sovereign changes | The basic norm seems to be an assumption without content | Critics say judges do not experience hard cases as free discretion (Dworkin) |

### Using positivism in a real dispute

The positivist questions are the first questions in almost every Israeli case against an authority:

1. **Source.** What norm is being applied to me: statute, regulation, by-law, procedure, internal guideline?
2. **Power.** What higher norm authorised it, and did the maker stay within that power?
3. **Procedure.** Was it made and published as required (Reshumot, Knesset committee approval where needed)?
4. **Hierarchy.** Does it conflict with a higher norm: a Basic Law, a statute, a binding precedent?
5. **Text.** Is my case in the core of the rule or in its penumbra?

A by-law that fails step 2 is void even if it is reasonable. An internal guideline is not law at all for the public, although an authority must follow its own guidelines and may be bound by them when people rely on them.

### Positivism and the 2023–2026 debate

Both sides of the dispute over review of Basic Laws (lesson 06.6) use positivist arguments. The majority in HCJ 5658/23 argued that Israel's rule of recognition, as shown by decades of practice, places limits on constituent power. Justices Sohlberg and Mintz replied that no written source gives the Court that power, and that a court cannot create its own authority. The disagreement is partly about what the rule of recognition actually is, which is exactly the question Hart said is answered by looking at official practice, and practice was itself in dispute.

## Worked example

Natalia Petrenko, an engineer in Ashdod, rides an electric bicycle along the beach promenade. A municipal inspector fines her under a by-law (invented for this example) that says "no vehicle may be driven on the promenade". She wants to contest the fine in the local-affairs court and asks what a positivist analysis would say.

1. **Source and power.** The by-law was made by the city council. Under s. 250 of the Municipalities Ordinance the council may make by-laws to carry out its powers; regulating public spaces is one of them. Assume it was published in Reshumot. The by-law is valid.
2. **Hierarchy.** Natalia checks whether a national statute or regulation on electric bicycles covers promenades and overrides municipal rules. If the national rules permit riding there and leave no room for local rules, the by-law could be invalid to that extent. That is a question of higher norms, not fairness.
3. **Open texture.** Is an electric bicycle a "vehicle"? A car is in the core; a child's scooter is probably outside; an electric bicycle is in the penumbra. On Hart's view the by-law does not settle it and the judge must decide, guided by the by-law's purpose (protecting pedestrians from speed and weight).
4. **Austin and Kelsen.** Austin would ask only whether the sovereign, through its delegate, commanded it. Kelsen would say the judge, by deciding, creates an individual norm within the frame the by-law leaves open.
5. **Her argument.** Natalia's best positivist arguments are about the higher norm (step 2) and the penumbra (step 3). "The fine is unfair" is not one of them.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One rule that has actually been applied to you: a fine, a fee, a condition in a permit, a benefit rule.
- Access to Wikisource or the Knesset legislation database to find its source.
- 45 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write down the exact rule and where you found it (document, section, date).
2. Climb the chain: for each level, write the norm and the section that authorises the level below it. Stop when you reach a Basic Law or the Knesset's legislative power.
3. Mark the step you think is weakest (power, procedure, conflict with a higher norm).
4. Find one word or phrase in the rule that has an open texture for your facts. Write one sentence placing your case in the core and one placing it in the penumbra.
5. Write a 200-word positivist argument for your position, using only steps 2–4. Do not argue that the rule is unfair.
6. In two sentences, say what Hart and Kelsen would each say the judge is doing when deciding your penumbral question.

### Template

None. A simple table with columns "level", "norm", "authorising section", "weak point?" is enough.

### How you know it worked

- Every level in your chain names a real section that you opened and read.
- Your argument would still make sense to a judge who thinks the rule is a good one.
- You can say whether your case is in the core or the penumbra and why.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Positivist analysis is the most practical skill in this module: most successful challenges by self-represented people to fines, fees and administrative conditions win on source, power or procedure, not on fairness. You may raise these arguments in your own name in any proceeding where you are a party.

### Where and how

- Find the source norm on Wikisource or the Knesset legislation database; by-laws are published in Reshumot (Kovetz Hukei Ezer) and usually on the municipality's site.
- In your statement or appeal, set out the chain briefly: "The fine was imposed under by-law X, s. Y, made under s. 250 of the Municipalities Ordinance. Section Y does not apply to my case because…".
- Contest a municipal fine in the way and within the time printed on the notice (a request to cancel it, or a request to be tried in the local-affairs court). Keep proof of the date you sent it.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your argument is that a regulation or by-law exceeds its enabling statute in a way that affects many people: that can become a class action or an administrative petition with costs risk.
- The rule is criminal (an offence with imprisonment) rather than an administrative fine.
- The authority answers with a detailed legal opinion you cannot evaluate.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arguing that a rule is invalid because it is unfair | The court applies it anyway | Find a source, power or procedure defect, or an open-textured term | Run the five positivist questions first |
| Treating an internal procedure (נוהל) as if it were a statute | You misjudge what binds you and the authority | Check whether it was made under a statutory power and published | Ask the authority for the legal source of the procedure |
| Saying positivists approve of the laws they describe | Misstating the theory | Explain that separation is meant to make criticism clearer | Remember Austin's merits-and-demerits sentence |
| Confusing Kelsen's basic norm with the Basic Laws | Muddled exam answer | The basic norm is presupposed, not enacted; Basic Laws are enacted | Ask "who made it?" for each |
| Thinking Hart's rule of recognition is written down somewhere | You look for a text that does not exist | It is the practice of officials; texts such as s. 20 are evidence of it | Describe it as a social fact |

## Review

- Positivism separates the existence of law from its merit; validity depends on source, not content.
- Austin: commands of a habitually obeyed sovereign. Kelsen: a hierarchy of norms resting on a presupposed basic norm. Hart: primary and secondary rules, with a rule of recognition practised by officials.
- Israeli norms can be traced up a chain (by-law, statute, Basic Law, 1948 founding acts); Basic Law: The Judiciary ss. 2 and 20 are pieces of Israel's rule of recognition.
- In Bank Mizrahi, Barak used Kelsen's basic norm and Hart's rule of recognition to ground the Knesset's constituent authority; Cheshin disputed the social facts.
- For self-represented people, source, power, procedure and open texture are the strongest arguments.
