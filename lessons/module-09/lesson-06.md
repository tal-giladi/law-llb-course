# 09.6 · Practice lab: read one Israeli ruling through four theories

In this lab you will read the further hearing in Nahmani v. Nahmani (1996), one of the most philosophically rich judgments in Israeli law, and analyse it four times: as a natural lawyer, as a positivist, as a Dworkinian and as a realist or critical scholar. You will finish with a written four-theory analysis and a one-page argument from principle of the kind a party could have filed. The same method works for any hard case you meet later in the course or in your own affairs.

## Why it matters

Reading a ruling through several theories is the skill a theory course is meant to build. It shows you why judges disagree, which arguments persuade which kind of judge, and how to frame your own case so it fits the way the court reasons. Nahmani is ideal: no statute governed the dispute; eleven justices wrote opinions that appeal openly to justice, rights, consent, reliance and the absence of law; and the result reversed an earlier Supreme Court judgment on the same facts.

## The law

> ראה בית המשפט שאלה משפטית הטעונה הכרעה, ולא מצא לה תשובה בדבר חקיקה, בהלכה פסוקה או בדרך של היקש, יכריע בה לאור עקרונות החירות, הצדק, היושר והשלום של המשפט העברי ומורשת ישראל.

*Gist:* The gap-filling rule (at the time without the words "Jewish law", added in 2018). Several justices discussed whether the case was a true gap. [Foundations of Law Act, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט)

> בקיום של חיוב הנובע מחוזה יש לנהוג בדרך מקובלת ובתום לב; והוא הדין לגבי השימוש בזכות הנובעת מחוזה.

*Gist:* Good faith in performing obligations and exercising rights, which the appeal judgment applied to the couple's agreement. [Contracts (General Part) Law, s. 39](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_(חלק_כללי))

The Embryo Carrying Agreements Law was enacted in 1996, after the appeal and before the further-hearing judgment. It regulates surrogacy agreements approved in advance by a statutory committee and did not govern the Nahmanis' dispute. [Embryo Carrying Agreements Law 1996](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הסכמים_לנשיאת_עוברים_(אישור_הסכם_ומעמד_היילוד))

## Leading cases

### דנ״א 2401/95 נחמני נ׳ נחמני, פ״ד נ(4) 661 (1996)

**Facts.** Ruth and Daniel Nahmani married in 1984. An operation left Ruth unable to carry a pregnancy. With a consent judgment of the High Court of Justice permitting the IVF in Israel, her ova were fertilised with Daniel's sperm at Assuta Hospital and frozen, for implantation in a surrogate in the United States. In 1992 Daniel left home and lived with another woman, who gave birth to his daughter in 1993. Ruth asked the hospital to release the embryos; Daniel objected and the hospital refused.

**Procedure.** The Haifa District Court (1993) ordered release to Ruth. The Supreme Court on appeal (CA 5587/93, 1995) reversed, 4–1 (Strasberg-Cohen, Barak, Levin and Zamir; Tal dissenting). On a further hearing before eleven justices (1996), the Court ruled 7–4 for Ruth (Tal, Dorner, Goldberg, Kedmi, Türkel, Bach and Mazza; Strasberg-Cohen, Or, Zamir and Barak dissenting).

**Main lines of reasoning.**

- **Tal:** Daniel is estopped: he consented, Ruth relied on his consent, and she changed her position irreversibly when the ova were fertilised. Jewish heritage values having children.
- **Dorner:** the interest in not becoming a parent against one's will gives way to the right to be a parent; courts weigh the stage of the procedure, representations, reliance and whether alternatives to parenthood exist.
- **Goldberg, Bach:** with no normative arrangement or statute, the case is decided by justice; the just result is the lesser evil, and the possibility of bringing a person into life tips the scale.
- **Türkel:** the right to be a parent carries far greater ethical weight than the right not to be one.
- **Kedmi:** before fertilisation either spouse may withdraw; after it, the spouse who wants to complete the process prevails.
- **Strasberg-Cohen (minority):** both are liberties, and parenthood requires the other's cooperation; consent is needed at each stage; justice is neutral here; refusing to force parenthood is itself a principled legal decision. She cited Dworkin and used Hohfeld's classification of rights.
- **Barak (minority):** continuing consent is required at every stage; public policy forbids waiving it in advance; equality between spouses supports this; withdrawing consent after the relationship ended is not bad faith.

[CFH 2401/95, English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/nahmani-v-nahmani-0); [CA 5587/93, English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/nahmani-v-nahmani)

## How it works in practice

### A worksheet for any ruling

For each theory, ask one question of each opinion:

| Theory | Question to ask of each opinion | Typical signs in the text |
|---|---|---|
| Natural law | Does the judge appeal to justice, the value of life or human nature as a standard above or behind positive law? | "justice", "ethical", "the value of life", "conscience" |
| Positivism | Does the judge locate a source (statute, precedent, contract) and treat its absence as a gap, with discretion? | "no statute governs", "the legislature has not decided", "discretion" |
| Dworkin | Does the judge treat principles (consent, reliance, equality, good faith) as already part of the law and weigh them? | "principle", "weight", "the system's values", "balance" |
| Realism / critical | What facts or values seem to drive the result, and how might the panel's make-up or social assumptions about gender and family matter? | Emphasis on sympathetic facts; disagreement that tracks values; a reversal by a larger panel |

Use the [four-theories worksheet](../../templates/four-theories-worksheet.md) for the full version.

## Worked example

To model the method, here is the analysis of one opinion, Justice Goldberg's, in the four columns.

1. **Natural law.** Goldberg decides by "the basic value of justice" when there is no normative arrangement. This is the closest opinion to a natural-law method: justice as the standard behind the law.
2. **Positivism.** Goldberg also begins positivistically: he finds no statute and no binding rule. A Hartian would describe what follows as discretion in a gap; Foundations of Law Act s. 1 would point to the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace.
3. **Dworkin.** A Dworkinian would ask whether "the lesser evil" is a principle that fits Israeli law. Goldberg's reliance on not undermining reliance retroactively ties his reasoning to recognised principles (estoppel, good faith), which improves its fit.
4. **Realism / critical.** His result matches the facts that make Ruth's position sympathetic: her lack of alternatives and her irreversible reliance. A feminist reading could stress that the burden of IVF fell on her body; another could stress that forcing genetic parenthood on a man who left raises equal-autonomy concerns. Both readings appear in the opinions.
5. **Verdict.** On fit and justification, Goldberg's opinion is persuasive only if one accepts that reliance outweighs continuing consent; Barak's dissent shows the principled case on the other side.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The English translation of CFH 2401/95 (link above). Allow 90 minutes for reading: the summaries at the top of the page, then the opinions of Strasberg-Cohen, Tal, Dorner, Goldberg and Barak.
- The [four-theories worksheet](../../templates/four-theories-worksheet.md) and the [argument-from-principle template](../../templates/argument-from-principle.md).
- 150 minutes in total. If you prefer, use Shakdiel (lesson 09.3) or Yekutieli (lesson 09.5) instead; the steps are the same.

### Steps

1. **Brief the ruling (20 minutes).** Facts, procedure, the question, the result and the vote, on one page (use the case-brief method from lesson 02.3).
2. **Map the opinions (30 minutes).** For at least five opinions (three majority, two minority), fill the worksheet's row: the opinion's key reason in one sentence, and one quotation of no more than 15 words with its paragraph.
3. **Four columns (40 minutes).** For each opinion, write one or two sentences in each of the four theory columns, as in the worked example.
4. **Four verdicts (20 minutes).** Write one paragraph per theory: how would a committed natural lawyer, positivist, Dworkinian and realist each assess the majority's reasoning? Give the strongest objection to that assessment.
5. **Your own view (15 minutes).** In 150 words, say which opinion you find most persuasive and which theory best explains why. Then state the strongest argument for the opposite result.
6. **Argument from principle (25 minutes).** Write a one-page argument, using the template, that one of the parties could have filed at the further hearing. Choose the party you did *not* favour in step 5.

### Template

[Four-theories worksheet (ניתוח פסק דין בארבע תיאוריות)](../../templates/four-theories-worksheet.md) and [argument from principle (טיעון מן העקרונות)](../../templates/argument-from-principle.md).

### How you know it worked

- Every opinion in your map is attributed correctly to the majority or minority, with the right vote (7–4).
- Each theory column contains something specific to that opinion, not a generic definition.
- Your one-page argument for the side you disagree with would be fair enough for its supporters to sign.
- Your quotations are short, attributed and accurate.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The method applies whenever you face a hard question in your own case: map how judges reason about it, find the principle that fits the law best, and argue in that language. In family matters such as Nahmani, any person may act for themselves in the family court, but the stakes and the emotional cost are high.

### Where and how

- Before arguing a hard question in your own case, read the leading ruling the way you did here: count votes, separate reasons, and note which principles the majority weighed.
- When you write to the court, use the principle the binding majority relied on (s. 20 of Basic Law: The Judiciary); a minority opinion can support an argument but is not the law.
- Free help in family matters: the family court's assistance unit (יחידת הסיוע) and Legal Aid for eligible people; see [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your own case involves a dispute over embryos, surrogacy, parenthood or custody. These turn on fast-changing law, expert evidence and strict procedure.
- You would need to ask the Supreme Court to depart from a precedent or to hold a further hearing.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing the 1995 appeal as the law | The precedent was reversed at the further hearing | Cite CFH 2401/95 for the result | Check the procedural history first |
| Writing generic definitions in the theory columns | The analysis shows no reading of the case | Tie every entry to a passage in the opinion | Fill the "quotation" column first |
| Quoting long passages from the translation | Copyright and readability problems | Paraphrase; quote 15 words or fewer | Use paragraph references |
| Treating the theory you prefer as the only reading | One-sided analysis | Write the strongest objection to each verdict | Do step 6 for the side you disagree with |
| Calling the result "what the law requires" without saying under which theory | Unclear claim | Say "on a Dworkinian view…" or "on a positivist view…" | Label every verdict |

## Review

- Nahmani CFH 2401/95 reversed the 4–1 appeal by 7–4; no statute governed; justices reasoned from justice, reliance, consent and rights.
- A natural lawyer sees the appeal to justice; a positivist sees a gap and discretion; a Dworkinian sees principles with weight; a realist sees outcome dependence on the panel and on value judgments.
- The four-column worksheet works for any hard case.
- Arguing the side you disagree with is the best test of whether you understand a ruling.
