# 09.3 · Dworkin: principles and law as integrity

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain Ronald Dworkin's challenge to legal positivism: that law contains not only rules but principles, that judges in hard cases are bound by those principles rather than free to legislate, and that the best account of law is "law as integrity", the reading that best fits and justifies a community's legal practice. You will see the theory at work in two Israeli rulings that found binding law where no statute spoke, and you will write an argument from principle of the kind judges actually adopt.

## Why it matters

Hart said that when a rule runs out, the judge has discretion (lesson 09.2). Dworkin, Hart's successor at Oxford, replied that this misdescribes what judges do. In a hard case, lawyers on both sides argue that the law already requires their result, and the judge writes as if bound, citing principles such as "no one may profit from his own wrong", "equality", "good faith" or "freedom of expression". If those principles are part of the law, there is no gap, and the judge is not legislating.

Israeli law is a natural test bed. Israel had no written bill of rights until 1992, yet the Supreme Court protected freedom of expression from 1953 and equality from the 1950s onward, by treating them as principles of the system. The Contracts Law makes "good faith" a binding standard. President Barak's purposive interpretation (lesson 02.4) and his talk of the "values of the system" are close to Dworkin's view, and in Bank Mizrahi his third model for constituent authority was the "best explanation" of the constitutional history, a Dworkinian idea.

For a self-represented person, Dworkin's account explains why the best arguments in a hard case are arguments from principle anchored in existing law: they show the judge that ruling for you is not inventing law but applying it.

## The law

### Dworkin's theory in brief

Dworkin's works are modern and in copyright; the summary below is a paraphrase, checked against the SEP entries in the sources.

- **Rules and principles ("The Model of Rules", 1967).** A rule applies in an all-or-nothing way: if its conditions are met, it settles the case, unless it has an exception. A principle states a reason that argues in one direction but does not necessitate a result. Principles have a dimension of **weight**: when two conflict, the judge weighs them, and the losing principle remains valid law. Positivism's single test of source (a rule of recognition) cannot capture principles, which count as law because of their content and their fit with the institutional history, not because someone enacted them.
- **The classic example: Riggs v. Palmer (New York, 1889).** A grandson murdered his grandfather to inherit under his will. The wills statute, read literally, gave him the estate. The court refused, relying on the principle that no one shall profit from his own wrong. For Dworkin the court did not fill a gap with its own morality: it applied a principle that was already part of the law.
- **Law as integrity (Law's Empire, 1986).** Legal propositions are true if they follow from the principles that provide the best constructive interpretation of the community's legal practice. Interpretation has two dimensions: **fit** (the reading must account for most of the settled materials: statutes, precedents) and **justification** (among readings that fit, the judge chooses the one that shows the practice in its best moral light). Dworkin compared judges to authors of a **chain novel**, each writing a new chapter that must continue the story already written. His ideal judge, Hercules, has unlimited time to construct that interpretation.
- **The right-answer thesis.** Even in hard cases there is usually one right answer, the one the best interpretation supports, although judges may disagree about which it is.

Hart's reply, published after his death (the "Postscript", 1994), accepted that a rule of recognition may include moral principles (soft or inclusive positivism), but maintained that where the law is indeterminate judges still have discretion.

### Principles written into Israeli statutes

> בקיום של חיוב הנובע מחוזה יש לנהוג בדרך מקובלת ובתום לב; והוא הדין לגבי השימוש בזכות הנובעת מחוזה.

*Gist:* Contractual obligations must be performed in a customary way and in good faith, and the same applies to exercising a contractual right. Good faith is a textbook Dworkinian principle: it never settles a case alone but weighs against the literal use of a right. [Contracts (General Part) Law, s. 39](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החוזים_(חלק_כללי))

> אלה פסולים לרשת את המוריש: (1) מי שגרם, במתכוון או באדישות, למותו של המוריש;

*Gist:* A person who caused the deceased's death intentionally or with indifference may not inherit. Israel turned the Riggs principle into a rule; the 2024 amendment widened it from intent to indifference. Once a principle becomes a rule, it applies in an all-or-nothing way. [Succession Law, s. 5(א)(1)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הירושה)

> חוק זה מטרתו לקבוע עקרונות להבטחת שוויון מלא בין האישה לבין האיש, ברוח העקרונות שבהכרזה על הקמת מדינת ישראל.

*Gist:* The purpose of the Women's Equal Rights Law is to set principles ensuring full equality between women and men, in the spirit of the Declaration's principles. The Knesset itself calls these "principles". [Women's Equal Rights Law, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_שיווי_זכויות_האשה)

## Leading cases

### בג״ץ 73/53 חברת "קול העם" בע״מ נ׳ שר הפנים, פ״ד ז 871 (1953)

The Minister of the Interior suspended two Communist newspapers under the Mandatory Press Ordinance, which allowed suspension of a publication "likely to endanger the public peace". Justice Agranat, for the Court, held that the Minister may suspend only if it is *probable* that the publication will endanger the public peace, not merely possible, and that he must weigh the gravity of the danger against the value of free expression. There was no bill of rights. Agranat derived freedom of expression from Israel's democratic character and the Declaration of Independence, holding that "the law of a people must be studied in the light of its national way of life", and read the Ordinance accordingly.

Why it matters: the textbook Israeli example of principles with weight. Free expression and public peace are both principles; neither is a trump; the "probability" test is the court's account of how they weigh against each other. And the court treated freedom of expression as already part of Israeli law, not as a new rule it was inventing. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/kol-haam-co-ltd-v-minister-interior)

### בג״ץ 153/87 שקדיאל נ׳ השר לענייני דתות, פ״ד מב(2) 221 (1988)

Leah Shakdiel, a teacher from Yerucham, was nominated to the local religious council. The ministerial committee refused to approve her because she was a woman, citing forty years of practice. No statute barred women from religious councils, and none expressly required their inclusion. Justice Elon, for the Court, held that the religious council is an administrative body providing services, not a body ruling on religious law, and that the general principle against discrimination on grounds of sex governs it. He relied on the Women's Equal Rights Law, read broadly, and on the Declaration. Long practice, the local rabbi's unexplained objection and fears of disruption did not outweigh equality. The refusal was set aside.

Why it matters: the court found a binding answer where the written rules were silent by asking which principles best fit and justify the legal system as a whole, and it gave tradition weight but not decisive weight. Note too that Justice Elon examined Jewish-law sources and found no halakhic bar, which strengthened the "fit" of his reading. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/shakdiel-v-minister-religious-affairs)

### A Dworkinian citation in Nahmani (1996)

In the further hearing on the Nahmani frozen-embryos case, Justice Strasberg-Cohen, writing for the minority, cited Dworkin on the point that justice cannot be left to convention and anecdote, and argued that refusing to force parenthood is itself a principled legal decision. The majority reached the opposite result, also by appeal to justice. The case shows that integrity does not tell you which side wins; it tells you what kind of argument both sides must make. Lesson 09.6 reads the whole case. [CFH 2401/95, English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/nahmani-v-nahmani-0)

## How it works in practice

### Rules, principles and policies

| | Rule | Principle | Policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it applies | All or nothing | Has weight; can be outweighed and survive | A collective goal (economy, security) |
| Israeli example | A fixed deadline in a statute; the disqualification in Succession Law s. 5(א)(1) | Good faith (s. 39); equality; free expression | Encouraging housing construction |
| Dworkin's view of its role in court | Applies as written unless a principle justifies an exception | Binds the judge; part of the law | Mainly for the legislature; courts decide on principle |

### The objections

- **Positivists (Hart, Raz).** Principles are law only because the rule of recognition points to them (for example, s. 39 is law because the Knesset enacted it). Where principles conflict and the law is indeterminate, the judge still chooses.
- **Realists and critical scholars (lesson 09.4).** "Best moral light" is whatever the judge already believes; integrity hides choice behind confident language.
- **Democratic critics.** In Israel, opponents of an expansive judiciary argue that "the values of the system" allowed the court to read principles into statutes that the Knesset did not enact. Supporters reply that every legal system relies on principles to resolve hard cases, and that the alternative, unconstrained discretion, gives judges more power, not less. Lesson 06.6 sets out the current state of the debate.

### Writing an argument from principle

An argument from principle that a judge can adopt has four parts:

1. **The rule and its limit.** Quote the rule and show that its words do not clearly decide your case, or that applying them literally defeats their own purpose.
2. **The principle and its anchor.** Name the principle and show where the law already recognises it: a statute (s. 39), a Basic Law, a leading case.
3. **Fit.** Show that ruling for you fits the existing cases and statutes; distinguish the ones that seem to go the other way.
4. **Weight.** Admit the competing principle the other side relies on and explain why, on these facts, yours weighs more.

## Worked example

Ahmad Khatib from Sakhnin rents a small shop from a private landlord. The lease lets the landlord end it "at any time, with 60 days' notice". In June Ahmad complained to the municipality that the landlord had blocked the building's fire exit. Two weeks later he received a termination notice. He wants to argue in the magistrates' court that the termination is invalid.

1. **Rule.** The termination clause is clear, and the landlord gave the notice it requires. On a rules-only view, Ahmad loses.
2. **Principle.** Section 39 of the Contracts Law requires a contractual right to be exercised in good faith. Using a termination right to punish a tenant for reporting a safety hazard is a strong candidate for bad-faith exercise.
3. **Fit.** The argument does not ask the court to ignore the clause or rewrite the lease. It asks it to apply a principle the Knesset enacted for every contract, and it fits the general approach of Israeli contract law that rights are not absolute.
4. **Weight.** The competing principle is freedom of contract and the landlord's right to decide who uses his property. Ahmad answers that on these facts (timing, no other reason given, a public-safety complaint) good faith weighs more, and that he is not claiming a permanent tenancy, only that this notice, given for this reason, is not a valid exercise of the right.
5. **Hart's view.** A Hartian judge would say the clause is clear and s. 39 gives the court discretion. A Dworkinian judge would say s. 39 is part of the law, so the right answer may already be that the notice is ineffective.

Ahmad's letter and statement of claim use the four-part structure. Whether he wins depends on the evidence of retaliation, which he must prove.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- A situation in your own life where a rule seems to point one way and fairness the other: a contract clause, an employer's policy, an authority's rule.
- The text of the rule and of s. 39 of the Contracts Law or another statute that states a principle.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. Quote the rule. Write one sentence on how it applies to you if read literally.
2. List every principle that might weigh against the literal result. For each, find a legal anchor (statute, Basic Law, a ruling you have read).
3. Choose the strongest principle. Write a "fit" paragraph: which existing law your reading is consistent with, and which you must distinguish.
4. Write a "weight" paragraph that states the other side's principle at its strongest and explains why yours weighs more on your facts.
5. Assemble a 300-word argument from principle using the [argument-from-principle template](../../templates/argument-from-principle.md).
6. Write two sentences on how a Hartian judge and a Dworkinian judge would each describe what they are doing if they rule for you.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. Arguing from principle is one of the most effective tools a self-represented party has, especially in the small-claims court, the magistrates' court and labour tribunals, where judges often decide on fairness within the law. You may make these arguments in your own name in any proceeding where you are a party.

### Where and how

- Put the principle argument in your pre-action letter as well as in court. A landlord, employer or company that sees a well-anchored good-faith argument often settles.
- In court, cite one statute and one ruling for the principle; more is rarely better. Quote the ruling's holding, not its general rhetoric.
- Use the template's four parts as headings in a written argument (טיעון בכתב) if the judge allows one.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- The other side relies on a clear statutory rule and your only answer is a principle: you are asking the court to create an exception, which is a hard argument with costs risk if you lose.
- Your argument from principle depends on proving motive (retaliation, bad faith) and the evidence is mainly the other side's documents.
- The case is a High Court petition that asks the court to read a principle into a statute against its words.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naming a principle with no legal anchor ("fairness requires…") | The judge treats it as a plea for sympathy | Tie it to a statute, Basic Law or ruling | Do step 2 before writing |
| Treating a principle as a trump that wins automatically | You ignore the other side's principle and lose the weighing | Address the competing principle head on | Always write the "weight" paragraph |
| Using good faith to rewrite a clear contract | The court refuses to remake the bargain | Ask only that a right not be exercised in bad faith on these facts | Ask for the narrowest remedy that solves your problem |
| Claiming Dworkin shows there is always one right answer that favours you | Overconfidence; integrity cuts both ways (Nahmani) | Argue fit and weight, not certainty | Read both sides of a split decision |
| Confusing principle with policy | You argue economic consequences the court will leave to the Knesset | Recast the argument as a right or principle | Ask "whose right is this?" |

## Review

- Dworkin: law includes principles, which have weight, not only rules, which apply all or nothing; principles bind judges, so hard cases are not gaps.
- Law as integrity: the right legal answer is the one that best fits and justifies the community's legal practice, like the next chapter of a chain novel.
- Hart's reply: principles are law if the rule of recognition includes them, but where the law is indeterminate judges still have discretion.
- Kol Ha'am (1953) and Shakdiel (1988) show Israeli courts finding binding principles where no express rule spoke; s. 39 of the Contracts Law is a principle in statutory form.
- In your own case, build arguments from principle in four steps: rule, anchored principle, fit, weight.
