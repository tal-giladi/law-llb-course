# 09.4 · Legal realism and critical theories

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain the realist view that law is best understood as a prediction of what courts will actually do, and the critical theories that grew from it: critical legal studies, feminist legal theory and critical race theory. You will use realism as a practical planning tool (reading a sample of recent decisions to predict an outcome) and read three Israeli rulings through a critical lens, stating each side's strongest argument. You will also learn where these theories stop being useful in a courtroom.

## Why it matters

Natural lawyers, positivists and Dworkin all ask what the law *is*. Realists ask a different question: what do courts *do*, and why? Their answer is that the written rules matter less than lawyers like to admit. Outcomes depend on the facts as the judge sees them, on the judge's sense of fairness, on institutional habits and sometimes on who sits on the panel.

Israel offers vivid material. The same Nahmani dispute was decided 4–1 one way on appeal and 7–4 the other way at the further hearing. The President of the Supreme Court decides panel size and composition. Debates about the court since the 1990s, from left and right, have often been realist arguments: critics on one side say judges' values shaped decisions on religion and state or security; critics on the other say the court's rhetoric of equality hid outcomes that left historical inequalities in place.

For a self-represented person realism is the most practical theory in this module. Before you file, you want to know what this kind of court usually does with this kind of case. Holmes called it looking at the law as a "bad man" does: not asking what is right, but what will happen.

## The law

### Holmes and the American realists

Oliver Wendell Holmes's 1897 lecture is public domain and short enough to read in an evening:

> "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law."

*Gist:* For a practising lawyer, law is a prediction of official behaviour. [Holmes, The Path of the Law (1897)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Path_of_the_Law)

> "If you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict [...]"

*Gist:* To see law apart from morality, take the view of someone who cares only about consequences. [same source](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Path_of_the_Law)

Holmes also warned against "the notion that the only force at work in the development of the law is logic". The realists of the 1920s and 1930s (Karl Llewellyn, Jerome Frank and others) built on this. In paraphrase of the SEP account: legal rules and precedents are often indeterminate, so they do not fix outcomes; judges respond mainly to the facts, deciding what is fair or commercially sensible and then finding legal reasons; and the useful work for lawyers is to study the patterns in how courts react to types of facts. Frank stressed that fact-finding at trial is itself uncertain, so prediction must include how a court will see the facts. Scandinavian realists (Alf Ross) gave a more philosophical version: legal concepts like "right" and "ownership" have no meaning beyond the predictions and behaviour they summarise.

### The critical theories

These are modern and in copyright; the summaries are paraphrases.

- **Critical legal studies (1970s onward: Duncan Kennedy, Roberto Unger and others).** Took realist indeterminacy further: legal doctrine is full of contradictory principles (freedom of contract against fairness, rules against standards), so a skilled judge can justify almost any result. Law therefore cannot be neutral; it is politics in a technical language, and it tends to make existing hierarchies look natural.
- **Feminist legal theory.** Asks how law built around male experience treats women. Versions differ: liberal feminists seek equal treatment; others (Catharine MacKinnon) argue that treating unequals alike preserves domination and that law must address subordination; relational or "care" approaches question the model of the isolated rights-holder. A key idea is that discrimination can be **structural**, operating without any intent.
- **Critical race theory (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw and others).** Argues that formally neutral law can entrench racial hierarchy, that advances for minorities often come when they suit majority interests, and that people at the intersection of several groups (for example, women of a minority) can fall between anti-discrimination categories ("intersectionality").

In Israel, critical scholarship has examined, among other things, the place of Arab citizens, Mizrahi Jews, Ethiopian Israelis, women and Haredim in the legal system. Critiques of the court from conservative and religious perspectives also rely on a realist premise: that judicial "values" reflect the views of a particular group.

### Where Israeli statutes acknowledge realist and critical insights

> בית המשפט העליון ידון בשלושה, אולם – (1) נשיא בית המשפט העליון או המשנה לנשיא רשאי להורות, לפני תחילת הדיון בענין מסויים, שהדיון בו יהיה לפני מספר בלתי זוגי גדול יותר של שופטים;

*Gist:* The Supreme Court sits in panels of three, but the President or Deputy President may order a larger odd-numbered panel. Who decides the panel's size and make-up is a question realists take seriously. [Courts Law, s. 26](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בתי_המשפט)

> (א) בהרכב דירקטוריון של חברה ממשלתית יינתן ביטוי הולם לייצוגם של בני שני המינים. (ב) עד להשגת ביטוי הולם לייצוג כאמור, ימנו השרים, ככל שניתן בנסיבות הענין, דירקטורים בני המין שאינו מיוצג באופן הולם [...]

*Gist:* Boards of government companies must give appropriate representation to both sexes, and until they do, ministers must, as far as circumstances allow, appoint directors of the under-represented sex. This is a statutory move from formal to substantive equality. [Government Companies Law, s. 18א](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_החברות_הממשלתיות)

## Leading cases

### ע״א 5587/93 ודנ״א 2401/95 נחמני נ׳ נחמני, פ״ד מט(1) 485 (1995); פ״ד נ(4) 661 (1996)

Ruth and Daniel Nahmani froze embryos during IVF; after they separated, Ruth wanted to use them through a surrogate abroad and Daniel refused. No statute decided the question. On appeal (1995) a five-justice panel ruled 4–1 for Daniel: parenthood cannot be imposed, and his consent did not extend to the new circumstances. At the further hearing (1996) an eleven-justice panel ruled 7–4 for Ruth, relying on reliance, estoppel and the greater weight of the wish to be a parent. Several majority justices said openly that, without a governing statute, they decided by their sense of justice.

Why it matters, through a realist lens: the same facts and the same legal materials produced opposite results depending on the panel. Counting both Supreme Court rounds, the judicial votes split 8–8, yet Ruth won because the larger panel had the last word. A formalist answer is that the larger panel's decision is the law (s. 20 of Basic Law: The Judiciary). A realist answer is that this is exactly the point: "the law" was what the second panel did. [CA 5587/93](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/nahmani-v-nahmani); [CFH 2401/95](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/nahmani-v-nahmani-0)

### בג״ץ 453/94 שדולת הנשים בישראל נ׳ ממשלת ישראל (1994)

After s. 18א was added, ministers appointed men to the boards of two government companies that had no women directors. Justice Mazza, for the majority, held that the section imposes a real duty: ministers must actively search for qualified women, and the "as far as circumstances allow" exception is narrow. He treated affirmative action as part of the principle of equality rather than an exception to it, and accepted that equal opportunity is not enough when groups start from unequal positions; discrimination can be systemic without any intent. The appointments were set aside. Justice Kedmi dissented in part: qualifications remain the main criterion and the appointees should not be harmed.

Why it matters: core feminist ideas (substantive equality, structural discrimination) became Israeli doctrine through a statute and its interpretation. Critics of affirmative action argue it sacrifices individual merit; supporters answer that "merit" is measured in conditions shaped by past exclusion. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/israel-women%E2%80%99s-network-v-government-israel)

### בג״ץ 6698/95 קעדאן נ׳ מינהל מקרקעי ישראל (2000)

The Ka'adan family, Arab citizens of Israel, sought to build a home in Katzir, a community established on state land allocated through the Jewish Agency, which admitted only Jews. President Barak, for the majority, held that the State may not discriminate on grounds of nationality in allocating land, directly or through a third party, and that Israel's values as a Jewish and democratic state do not justify such discrimination. The remedy was limited: the State had to reconsider the request on the basis of equality, "with appropriate speed". Barak described the ruling as a first step on a difficult and sensitive path, looking forward only. Justice Kedmi agreed in principle but would have given only a declaration.

Why it matters, through a critical lens: the case can be read in opposing ways, and both readings are made by serious scholars. One reading stresses the limits: a declaration of formal equality with a cautious remedy, applying only to the future, leaves earlier allocation patterns untouched. Another reading, from those who see Jewish settlement as a legitimate national purpose, argues the ruling went too far by treating that purpose as irrelevant to state land policy. The Court's own view is that it applied equality, a principle of the system, case by case. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ka%E2%80%99adan-v-israel-land-administration)

## How it works in practice

### Realism as a planning tool

Realism gives you a method that lawyers use every day without naming it:

1. **Define the type of case** narrowly by its facts, not by the legal label: "flight cancelled shortly before departure, airline claims extraordinary circumstances".
2. **Collect recent decisions** of the same court level on that type of case. For small claims and magistrates' courts, recent decisions are often online on the Judiciary's site and on free databases (module 2).
3. **Tabulate** facts, result and amount awarded, and note the judge.
4. **Predict** the likely range of outcomes and what facts move the result.
5. **Decide**: sue, settle or drop, and which facts to prove carefully.

This is not cynicism. A rule tells you what a court should do; a sample tells you what courts like this one actually do, including how they see facts like yours.

### What realism and critical theory cannot do in court

- You cannot ask a judge to rule for you because "law is politics" or because the system is structurally biased. The judge must give legal reasons.
- You can use the insights: point to the purpose of a rule, show how a neutral-seeming practice falls unequally on a group you belong to, and rely on doctrines that already recognise substantive equality (Israel Women's Network) or indirect discrimination (Ka'adan).

### The strongest objections

- **To realism.** Hart: judges treat rules as binding standards, not just predictors; a judge deciding a case is not predicting her own behaviour. Most cases are easy and decided by rules; realists generalise from appellate hard cases.
- **To critical legal studies.** If doctrine can justify anything, why are most outcomes predictable? And if law is only politics, the critique undermines the rule-of-law protections that weaker parties rely on.
- **To feminist and critical race theory.** Critics argue that group-based analysis can neglect individual rights and merit and can itself become a contested political programme; supporters reply that formal neutrality also embodies choices, only less visibly.

## Worked example

Yossi Mizrahi, an administrator at a yeshiva in Elad, bought tickets for his family to a wedding abroad. The airline cancelled the flight nine days before departure, blaming "operational reasons", and offered only a voucher. He wants to know whether to file a small claim for compensation under the Aviation Services Law.

1. **The rule.** The statute grants compensation for cancellations close to departure unless an exception applies. He reads the current text and its compensation schedule on Wikisource (the clinic module covers the claim itself).
2. **Realist sample.** He searches recent small-claims decisions for "ביטול טיסה" and "נסיבות מיוחדות" from his local court and two others, and reads twelve from the past two years.
3. **Table.** In nine of twelve the passenger won; airlines that blamed vague "operational reasons" without documents usually lost; awards often included compensation but rarely large sums for distress; two judges awarded costs to passengers who had written to the airline first.
4. **Prediction.** A good chance of compensation if he documents the cancellation date and his written demand; a modest award for distress.
5. **Critical check.** He notices that passengers who wrote clearly and attached documents did better, which tells him something about how small-claims judges see self-represented parties: organisation matters.
6. **Decision.** He sends a written demand first, then files if the airline refuses.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- A dispute in your own life, or the airline example above.
- Access to free case-law search (the Judiciary's decisions search or another free database, module 2).
- A spreadsheet or table. 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Describe your type of case in one sentence using facts, not legal labels.
2. Find at least six recent decisions of the court that would hear your case, on similar facts. Record the case number, date and judge.
3. Build a table: key facts, result, amount, reasons given in one line.
4. Mark the facts that seem to move the result. Write your prediction in three sentences, with a range.
5. Write one paragraph reading the same sample through a critical lens: who usually wins, who has better documents or representation, and whether any group seems to fare differently.
6. Write one paragraph stating what you will do (sue, settle, drop) and which facts you must prove. Note which of your findings you may say to the judge (legal reasons, purpose, indirect discrimination) and which you may not ("the system favours airlines").

### Template

None. Use a table with columns "case", "date", "court and judge", "key facts", "result", "amount", "reason".

### How you know it worked

- Your prediction is a range tied to facts, not a hope.
- You found at least one decision that went against your side and can explain the difference.
- Your plan says what evidence to collect before filing.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The realist method in this lesson is how you decide whether to file at all, and it is something any party may do for their own case. The critical insights become legal arguments only through recognised doctrines: substantive equality, indirect discrimination, the purpose of a rule.

### Where and how

- Search recent decisions on the Judiciary's free decisions search and other free databases (lessons 02.2 and 01.5). Prefer your own court and the last two or three years.
- If you rely on a decision in court, bring a copy and cite it fully; lower-court decisions do not bind but can persuade.
- For discrimination claims, free advice is available from equality bodies and clinics; see [where to get help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md).

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your sample shows that cases like yours usually fail and the stakes are high.
- Your case rests on proving systemic or indirect discrimination: these claims need data, expert evidence and often an organisation behind them.
- The other side is a large company or the State and the decisions in your sample turn on detailed evidence you do not have.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telling a judge "law is just politics" | The argument has no legal weight and may irritate the court | Recast as purpose, equality or indirect discrimination | Separate planning insights from courtroom arguments |
| Predicting from one remembered case | Overconfidence and a surprise loss | Build a sample of at least six recent decisions | Use the table in the steps |
| Reading only decisions your side won | A biased prediction | Search for losses deliberately | Search with the other side's key terms too |
| Treating a lower-court decision as binding | Misstating the law | Present it as an example that persuades | Check s. 20 of Basic Law: The Judiciary (lesson 01.5) |
| Presenting one critical reading of a case as the truth | A one-sided analysis | State the opposing reading at its strongest | Write both readings before concluding |

## Review

- Realism: law is, for practical purposes, a prediction of what courts will do; outcomes respond to facts and judges' sense of fairness, not only to rules.
- Critical legal studies: doctrine is indeterminate and law is politics in technical language; feminist and critical race theory examine how neutral law can entrench hierarchy and recognise structural discrimination.
- Nahmani shows panel-dependence; Israel Women's Network brings substantive equality into doctrine; Ka'adan invites opposing critical readings.
- Realism is a planning tool (sample recent decisions, predict a range); in court, critical insights must be expressed through recognised doctrines.
- Each theory has strong objections: most cases are easy, and the rule of law protects weaker parties.
