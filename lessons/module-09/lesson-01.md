# 09.1 · Natural law

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain what natural-law theorists mean when they say that law must answer to justice, tell the strong version of that claim apart from the moderate ones that most modern scholars defend, and find the places where Israeli law itself refers to justice, freedom and the values of the Declaration of Independence. You will read Yeredor, the 1965 case in which a Supreme Court justice invoked "natural law" to bar a party from the elections and a dissenting colleague replied that Israel's ways "are the ways of law".

## Why it matters

Every legal system eventually meets a law, an order or a gap that seems deeply unjust. The question legal theory has asked for two thousand years is whether such a rule is still law. Natural-law theory answers, in its various forms, that law is not just whatever the powerful command: it is a rational standard aimed at the common good, and a rule that betrays that aim is defective as law.

This is not an antique debate in Israel:

- The **Foundations of Law Act 1980** tells judges to fill gaps in the law "in light of the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace" of Jewish law and Israel's heritage. A moral standard is written into the rule of decision.
- The **Penal Law** denies the defence of obedience to an order that is "manifestly unlawful". The test grew out of the Kafr Qasim trial (1957–58), where the military court held that some orders are so plainly wrong that the law expects a soldier to refuse.
- The **Nazis and Nazi Collaborators (Punishment) Law 1950** punishes acts done before Israel existed. Its retroactive reach is defended on the ground that those acts were crimes against humanity whatever the law of Germany said.
- Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty builds the rights it protects on "the value of the human being, the sanctity of human life and the principle that all persons are free", which is classic natural-rights language.

For a self-represented person the practical lesson is narrower but useful: an Israeli judge will not refuse to apply a statute because you say it is unjust, but will listen carefully when you show that a legal text, read in light of the system's own values, leads to a just result.

## The law

### The classical texts

Natural-law writing is public domain for most of its history, so you can read the originals.

Cicero (1st century BCE) described a law higher than any statute:

> "There is indeed a law, right reason, which is in accordance with nature; existing in all, unchangeable, eternal."

*Gist:* True law is reason in agreement with nature, the same everywhere, and no senate or people can release us from it. [Cicero, The Republic, book III, Featherstonhaugh translation, 1829](https://archive.org/details/republicofcicero00cicerich)

Thomas Aquinas (13th century) gave the theory its classic structure. Human law binds because, and to the extent that, it is derived from natural law:

> "Every human law has just so much of the nature of law, as it is derived from the law of nature. But if in any point it deflects from the law of nature, it is no longer a law but a perversion of law."

*Gist:* A human law that departs from natural law is not a full law but a corruption of law. [Summa Theologiae I-II, Q. 95, a. 2](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2095.htm)

Aquinas then gave three tests of a just law: its **end** (the common good, not the ruler's vanity), its **author** (within the power given to the lawgiver) and its **form** (burdens shared in proportion). A law that fails them is, quoting Augustine, "a law that is not just, seems to be no law at all", yet he added that one may still have to obey it "in order to avoid scandal or disturbance". [Summa Theologiae I-II, Q. 96, a. 4](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2096.htm)

Note the subtlety, which students often miss: Aquinas did not say that an unjust law has no effect at all. He said it does not bind *in conscience*, while prudence may still counsel obedience. Natural-law theory is a theory about the moral authority of law, not a licence to ignore every rule one dislikes.

### The modern versions

The SEP entry in the sources sets out the modern positions. In brief, and in paraphrase:

- **Gustav Radbruch (1946).** A German jurist who had been a positivist before the Nazi period. After the war he argued that statutes should normally be applied even when unjust, for the sake of legal certainty, but that a statute whose injustice reaches an "intolerable" degree, or which does not even attempt equality, loses its character as law. German courts used this "Radbruch formula" in cases about Nazi-era and East German border laws.
- **Lon Fuller (1964).** Law has an "inner morality": rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, consistent, possible to obey, stable, and actually applied as written. A system that fails these eight requirements badly enough is not a legal system at all. This procedural natural law returns in lesson 09.5 on the rule of law.
- **John Finnis (1980).** Natural law identifies basic human goods (life, knowledge, friendship, practical reasonableness and others). Law is justified as a way of coordinating the pursuit of the common good. An unjust law is law in a "weak" or secondary sense: it is legally valid but its moral claim to obedience is defeated.

### Israeli statutes that point to justice

> ראה בית המשפט שאלה משפטית הטעונה הכרעה, ולא מצא לה תשובה בדבר חקיקה, בהלכה פסוקה או בדרך של היקש, יכריע בה לאור עקרונות החירות, הצדק, היושר והשלום של המשפט העברי ומורשת ישראל.

*Gist:* Where legislation, precedent and analogy give no answer, the court decides by the principles of freedom, justice, equity and peace of Jewish law and Israel's heritage (the words "Jewish law" were added in 2018). [Foundations of Law Act, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_יסודות_המשפט)

The Act is a natural-law clause with a positivist gate: moral principles become the rule of decision only after the written sources run out. Module 10 looks at how rarely courts have found such a gap.

> לא יישא אדם באחריות פלילית למעשה שעשה באחד מאלה: [...] (2) עשהו על־פי צו של רשות מוסמכת שהיה חייב לפי דין לציית לה, זולת אם הצו הוא בעליל שלא כדין;

*Gist:* Acting under an order one must obey is a justification, unless the order is manifestly unlawful. [Penal Law, s. 34יג(2)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין)

> תהא מושתתה על יסודות החירות, הצדק והשלום לאור חזונם של נביאי ישראל; תקיים שויון זכויות חברתי ומדיני גמור לכל אזרחיה בלי הבדל דת, גזע ומין;

*Gist:* The State will be based on freedom, justice and peace as envisaged by the prophets of Israel, and will ensure complete equality of social and political rights to all its citizens. [Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/הכרזה_על_הקמת_מדינת_ישראל)

> זכויות היסוד של האדם בישראל מושתתות על ההכרה בערך האדם, בקדושת חייו ובהיותו בן־חורין, והן יכובדו ברוח העקרונות שבהכרזה על הקמת מדינת ישראל.

*Gist:* Basic human rights in Israel rest on the value of the person, the sanctity of life and human freedom, and are to be honoured in the spirit of the Declaration. [Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו)

### The hardest case: retroactive punishment

> אין עבירה ואין עונש עליה אלא אם כן נקבעו בחוק או על־פיו.

*Gist:* There is no offence and no punishment unless set by or under a statute. [Penal Law, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_העונשין); s. 3 forbids applying a new offence to earlier acts.

Yet the Nazis and Nazi Collaborators (Punishment) Law 1950 makes punishable, in s. 1, crimes against the Jewish people, crimes against humanity and war crimes committed "during the period of the Nazi regime, in an enemy country". [The 1950 Law, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_לעשיית_דין_בנאצים_ובעוזריהם) The courts that tried Adolf Eichmann (District Court 1961; Supreme Court, CrimA 336/61, 1962) rejected his argument that this was punishment under a retroactive law, reasoning that the acts were crimes under the law of nations and universally recognised as such when committed. Here a natural-law intuition (some acts are wrong whatever the local statute says) and a positivist safeguard (no punishment without prior law) meet head on.

## Leading cases

### ע״ב 1/65 ירדור נ׳ יושב־ראש ועדת הבחירות המרכזית לכנסת השישית (1965)

The Central Elections Committee refused to approve the "Socialists' List", whose candidates came from the al-Ard movement, which the authorities had declared unlawful as rejecting the existence of the State. No statute then allowed a list to be disqualified for its aims. The Supreme Court, 2–1, upheld the refusal.

- **President Agranat** relied on the "constitutional factor" of the State: Israel is a permanent state, and the Knesset Elections Law must be read in light of the Declaration of Independence, so that no list may run in order to destroy the State.
- **Justice Sussman** went further. He spoke of fundamental, supra-constitutional rules that amount to the right of an organised society to defend itself, and wrote that whether one calls them "natural law" or by another name, experience forbids repeating the mistake of democracies that let their enemies use elections to destroy them.
- **Justice Cohn**, dissenting, answered in the positivist voice. Some countries, he wrote, have invented a "natural law" that stands above any legislation and overrides it when need be; "These are not the ways of the State of Israel. Its ways are the ways of law", and law is made by the Knesset or with its explicit authorisation. The right to stand for election can be restricted only by statute.

Why it matters: it is the clearest Israeli judicial debate between natural-law reasoning and legal positivism, and its sequel is instructive. In 1985 the Knesset wrote disqualification grounds into s. 7א of Basic Law: The Knesset, turning a judge-made, value-based limit into positive law; later cases (Neiman, 1984, on the eve of that amendment) held that without such a statute the Yeredor exception must stay very narrow. [English translation](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/yeredor-v-chairman-central-elections-committee-sixth-knesset)

### מ/3/57 התובע הצבאי נ׳ מלינקי (פרשת כפר קאסם, 1958)

On 29 October 1956, border police killed 48 residents of Kafr Qasim (49 counting an unborn child), among them women and children, who were returning from work unaware of a curfew. The military court convicted the officers and rejected their defence of superior orders. It held that an order to kill civilians was "manifestly unlawful": its illegality is not a matter of legal fine print but flies like a black flag above the order, recognisable to any person's conscience, and a soldier must refuse it.

Why it matters: the "black flag" test makes ordinary moral perception part of the legal standard, which is a natural-law idea, yet it is now applied through a positive statute (Penal Law s. 34יג(2)). The full judgment is not freely available online; a free scholarly summary is the Hebrew University record of the article ["Black flag at a crossroads: the Kafr Qasim political trial"](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/black-flag-at-a-crossroads-the-kafr-qasim-political-trial-1957-58-2/). Read it as background, not as a source you would cite to a court.

## How it works in practice

### Three ways natural-law ideas enter an Israeli courtroom

| Channel | What the court does | Example | How strong the argument is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interpretation in light of values | Reads an open text so that it fits justice and the Declaration | Kol Ha'am (1953), Yeredor (1965) | Strong when the text is open; weak against clear words |
| Gap-filling | Applies Foundations of Law Act s. 1 after legislation, precedent and analogy fail | Rare; courts usually find an analogy first | Narrow |
| Constitutional review | Tests a law against Basic Law rights grounded in dignity and freedom | The limitation clause cases (module 6) | Strong, but it is review under positive law, not natural law |

Israeli courts almost never say "this law is void because it is unjust". When they reach a just result against the apparent words of a law, they do it through one of the three channels above. That is why the useful skill is translation: turning a sense of injustice into an argument about purpose, values or rights.

### The strongest objections to natural law

- **Whose justice?** In a society divided on religion, nationality and politics, "natural law" may simply be the judge's own morality. Cohn's dissent in Yeredor makes this point.
- **Certainty.** If every citizen can disregard laws they judge unjust, the law cannot guide anyone. Even Aquinas and Radbruch accepted that ordinary injustice does not cancel a law.
- **Democracy.** Values that override statutes are applied by unelected judges. The answer of natural lawyers is that some limits (no murder of civilians, no destruction of democracy itself) protect democracy rather than undermine it.

## Worked example

Tsega Mekonen, a social worker in Netanya, is preparing for a public meeting about a municipal by-law (invented for this example) that fines anyone who sleeps in a public park overnight. Several of her clients are homeless. She wants to explain how different natural-law theorists, and then Israeli law, would treat the by-law.

1. **Aquinas.** End: the by-law aims at public order and safety in parks, a common good. Author: a municipality has power to regulate parks. Form: the burden falls almost entirely on people who have nowhere else to sleep. On the third test the by-law is at least doubtful: the burden is not shared "according to an equality of proportion".
2. **Radbruch.** The injustice is real but not "intolerable" in his sense; the by-law remains law and should be applied, while the council is pressed to change it.
3. **Fuller.** The by-law is public, general, prospective and clear. It passes the inner morality of law. Fuller's test is about form, so it does not condemn it.
4. **Finnis.** The by-law is valid, but if it fines people for being unable to find shelter it may be law in the weak sense: its claim on conscience is weakened.
5. **Israeli law.** No court will strike it down for being unjust. But Tsega can argue (a) that a by-law must be read in light of human dignity (Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, ss. 1–2), (b) that a by-law infringing dignity must meet the limitation clause, and (c) that the municipality must offer shelter alternatives before enforcement is proportionate. Each argument carries the natural-law intuition through a positive legal channel.

Her conclusion for the meeting: the strongest course is to ask the council to amend the by-law and, if a client is fined, to argue proportionality and dignity, not "this law is not a law".

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The texts of Aquinas Q. 96 a. 4 and the Foundations of Law Act s. 1 (links in Sources).
- One Israeli law, regulation or by-law that you think is unjust in some respect. It may affect you or not.
- 45 minutes and a word processor.

### Steps

1. Write the rule in one sentence and quote its source (statute, section, date).
2. Apply Aquinas's three tests (end, author, form) in one short paragraph each. Say which test, if any, it fails.
3. Apply Radbruch: is the injustice ordinary or intolerable? Give one reason.
4. Apply Fuller's eight requirements as a checklist. Mark any that fail.
5. Write a 250-word argument addressed to an Israeli judge that carries your concern through one of the three channels (interpretation, gap-filling, constitutional review). Do not use the words "natural law" or "unjust law"; use the language of purpose, values, dignity or proportionality.
6. Add one paragraph stating the strongest objection to your argument (certainty, democracy or "whose justice?") and your answer to it.

### Template

None. Use the steps as headings.

### How you know it worked

- Your argument would make sense to a judge who rejects natural-law theory, because it rests on Israeli texts.
- You separated the moral question (is the rule just?) from the legal one (is it valid, and how should it be read?).
- A reader who supports the rule would accept your summary of its purpose as fair.

## Without a lawyer

### Can you do this yourself?

Not a filing lesson. The idea helps whenever you argue your own case: judges respond to arguments that show the just result is also the legally correct one. A small-claims judge, a labour-court judge or an appeals committee will rarely accept "the rule is unfair", but will often accept "the rule's purpose is X, and applying it to my facts defeats that purpose" or "read this way, the rule offends dignity without need".

### Where and how

- In a statement of claim or a letter to an authority, put the value argument after the text argument, not instead of it: first what the rule says, then how its purpose and the values of the system point to your reading.
- Quote the Foundations of Law Act only if you can show a real gap: no statute, no precedent and no analogy. Otherwise it signals that you have not done the research.
- Free help on the underlying rights: Kol-Zchut explains benefits and rights in plain Hebrew; the court information centres explain procedure, not merits.

### Stop and get a lawyer when

- Your argument is that a statute itself is invalid. That is a constitutional claim, usually for the High Court of Justice (module 6).
- You are facing a criminal charge and want to argue that an order you obeyed, or a law you broke, was unjust. The legal tests (manifest unlawfulness, necessity) are narrow and the stakes are high.
- The other side is a public body represented by the State Attorney and the case turns on values rather than facts.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telling a judge a law "is not law because it is unjust" | The argument is dismissed and your credibility drops | Re-frame as interpretation, purpose or proportionality | Translate moral claims into a legal channel before you write |
| Treating Aquinas as saying unjust laws may always be disobeyed | Misstating the theory in an exam or debate | Add his point about avoiding scandal and disturbance | Read Q. 96 a. 4 in full |
| Invoking the Foundations of Law Act when a statute or analogy covers the issue | The court notes there is no gap | Find the closest statute or precedent and argue from it | Check legislation, precedent and analogy first, in that order |
| Citing Sussman in Yeredor as "the Court's holding" | Overstating the precedent | Say the result was 2–1 and that Agranat's reasoning differed | Count opinions and reasons separately |
| Assuming natural law always favours individual rights | Missing that Yeredor used it to restrict a right to stand for election | Ask whose interests the "higher law" protects in each case | Read each case's outcome, not only its rhetoric |

## Review

- Natural-law theory holds that law answers to reason and justice; the strong form (Augustine, Aquinas) says an unjust law is not fully law, while modern forms (Radbruch, Fuller, Finnis) limit the claim to extreme injustice, to the form of law, or to its moral force.
- Even Aquinas separated the duty in conscience from prudent obedience.
- Israeli law imports moral standards through positive texts: Foundations of Law Act s. 1, the Declaration, Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty s. 1, and the "manifestly unlawful order" in Penal Law s. 34יג(2).
- Yeredor (1965) shows the debate in a courtroom: Sussman's supra-constitutional rules against Cohn's "the ways of law"; the Knesset later legislated the grounds in s. 7א.
- In your own case, carry a sense of injustice through interpretation, purpose and rights, not through a claim that a law is void.
