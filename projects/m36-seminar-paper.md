# Module 36 project: Seminar paper: presentation, defence and submission

Take the full draft you wrote in lab 36.6 through a recorded presentation and defence, a final revision and a complete submission package: the 30-page paper, its appendices, a response memo and a short defence note. Time: about 6 hours.

## Brief

Your paper answers one research question on Israeli law in about 30 pages of text plus footnotes, in Hebrew or English, footnoted under the uniform citation rules (third edition, 2021), and makes an original contribution stated as a gap on page 1. At least one chapter rests on primary sources read systematically (legislative history, coded court or registry data, or foreign primary sources), with the method, its limits and an appendix. Use your own question or one of the supplied questions in lesson [36.6](../lessons/module-36/lesson-06.md):

- **A.** Do recent class-action applications against mobile operators serve the access-to-court purpose of the Class Actions Law, or mainly its enforcement purpose?
- **B.** Does the legislative history of a section of a law that governs your own affairs support the reading that courts now give it?

You need an audience of at least one for the presentation (a study partner, a classmate or a recording you watch back after a day) and a reviewer who has read the full draft (round 2 of the lab).

## Steps

1. **Prepare the presentation (60 min).** Nine slides at most, following lesson [36.5](../lessons/module-36/lesson-05.md): hook, question and thesis, the gap, method and one limit, two or three findings, the strongest objection, an open question.
2. **Prepare the defence (30 min).** Write the five hardest questions about your paper, at least one on the method and one on the contribution; for each, a concede, cite, distinguish or plan answer.
3. **Present and record (30 min).** 12–15 minutes plus questions. Write down every comment.
4. **Response memo (30 min).** Every comment from the presentation and from round 2 of peer review, with a decision and a reason.
5. **Final revision (120 min).** The changes that go to the thesis and the contribution first; then structure (reverse outline); then sentences and footnotes ([36.4](../lessons/module-36/lesson-04.md)).
6. **Final checks (45 min).** The integrity check and AI declaration ([paper review checklist](../templates/paper-review-checklist.md), part 1); every primary-source citation reopened at its file address; rescore the 14-line seminar rubric from lesson 36.6.
7. **Package (25 min).** Title page (course, supervisor, date, name, word count including footnotes if your rules count them); the paper; appendices (coding sheet or [legislative-history worksheet](../templates/legislative-history-worksheet.md), search log); source list; AI declaration; response memo.
8. **Defence note (20 min).** One page: your contribution in three sentences, the strongest objection and your answer, and the one limit a reader should keep in mind.

## Self-assessment rubric

| Criterion | Weak (1) | Adequate (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution | A survey; no gap stated | Gap stated but not checked against the literature | Gap names the closest work; the paper delivers what it promises |
| Question and thesis | Topic only, or thesis only at the end | Present but vague | One-sentence question and contestable thesis on page 1; conclusion answers it |
| Method | Not stated | Named; limits missing | Designed, repeatable, limits stated, appendix attached |
| Primary sources | Explanatory notes or a few rulings only | Several primary sources, read selectively | Systematic: all relevant documents listed, the key ones read in full, weighed by kind |
| Structure over 30 pages | Chapters drift from the thesis | Most chapters advance it | Every chapter on the argument map; headings state claims; within the length rules |
| Counter-arguments and neutrality | Absent or straw man | Present, weakly answered | Strongest objections, cited and fairly stated, answered; contested issues presented from each side |
| Footnotes and integrity | Inconsistent; unchecked sources | Mostly correct | Uniform rules throughout; pinpoints; every source opened; declaration accurate |
| Presentation and defence | Read from a script; defensive | Clear talk, some answers evasive | On time; answers concede, cite, distinguish or plan; comments recorded |
| Use of comments | Ignored | Small edits only | Response memo with reasons; main comments reflected in the text |

A strong project scores at least 23 of 27, with no criterion at 1.

**Supplied question A: defence note and response memo (extracts).** The counts and findings below are illustrative, to show the form; they are not results of real coding, and your own data will differ.

**Defence note.**

*Contribution.* Earlier empirical work counted all class-action applications up to the early 2010s; this paper codes every application in the registry against two mobile operators opened in 2024–2025 (64 entries) and reads them against the 21 committee sessions on the 2006 bill. It finds that most applications concern small uniform charges and that settlements in the sample rarely paid members directly. It argues that the access-to-court purpose in s. 1(1) needs its own mechanism in settlement approval.

*Strongest objection.* Deterrence benefits members too, and Tnuva accepts relief for the public where members cannot be identified. Answer: s. 1 lists access, enforcement and relief as separate purposes; the paper does not say these applications are illegitimate, only that they serve enforcement more than access, and it proposes a reasoning requirement, not a ban.

*Limit.* Two companies over two years are not the market, and the registry does not show disputes settled before filing.
