# Project: a theory-based argument file

Take one hard question in your own affairs, where a rule seems to point one way and fairness or principle the other, and build the file a careful person prepares before arguing it: the rule and its chain of validity, a four-theory analysis, a realist prediction from recent decisions, and a one-page argument from principle you could put before a judge or an authority. You will reuse the method in every later module where the law is unsettled.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Act in your own name only. This is not legal advice — check current rules before filing. This project is a dry run: you may send a letter about your own matter if you choose, but nothing is filed in court.

## Brief

Choose a question from your own life: a contract clause used against you, a condition an authority imposed, a rule that does not fit your situation, a gap where no rule seems to apply. If you have nothing suitable, use the supplied case and say so.

**Supplied case (invented).** Igor Volkov, 58, from Karmiel, buried his mother in a cemetery run by a local burial society. The society provides burial services as part of a public service and under the arrangements that govern burial in Israel. When Igor submitted the tombstone design, which shows his mother's name in Hebrew and Russian and her dates in both the Hebrew and the Gregorian calendar, the society refused: "according to the society's custom, only Hebrew letters and Hebrew dates are permitted". Neither the burial agreement Igor signed nor any published rules of the society mention inscriptions. The society says it is a matter of religious custom and of the cemetery's uniform appearance. Igor wants to write to the society and, if it refuses again, to consider a claim in the magistrates' court.

Time: about 5 hours.

## Steps

1. **Facts and documents (30 minutes).** A dated, numbered chronology; the rule or decision in full; every document as a numbered attachment.
2. **The rule and its source (30 minutes).** State exactly what rule is being applied to you and climb its chain of validity: what authorises it, was it published, does a higher norm conflict with it (lesson 09.2). If there is no written rule, say so: that is itself a finding.
3. **Core or penumbra (15 minutes).** Is your case clearly inside the rule, clearly outside it, or in its open texture (lesson 09.2)?
4. **Four-theory analysis (60 minutes).** Fill the [four-theories worksheet](../templates/four-theories-worksheet.md) for your question rather than for a ruling: what would a natural lawyer, a positivist, a Dworkinian and a realist or critical scholar each say the right outcome is, and why (lessons 09.1–09.4)?
5. **Rights and rule of law (30 minutes).** A Hohfeld analysis of your position (claim, liberty, power, immunity, and who holds the correlative); Fuller's eight questions applied to the rule (lesson 09.5).
6. **Realist prediction (45 minutes).** Find at least five recent decisions on similar questions, tabulate them and predict a range of outcomes (lesson 09.4).
7. **Argument from principle (60 minutes).** One page, using the [argument-from-principle template](../templates/argument-from-principle.md): rule, anchored principle, fit, weight, remedy (lesson 09.3).
8. **The other side (20 minutes).** One paragraph stating the other party's strongest argument as its supporters would, and your answer.
9. **Decision and stop signals (10 minutes).** What you will do (write, negotiate, file, drop) and the red flags that would send you to a lawyer.

## Self-assessment rubric

Score each criterion 0–2. A total of 14 or more (out of 18) means the project meets the standard.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facts | Vague, undated | Mostly dated | Dated, numbered, every document attached |
| Source and validity | Not considered | Source named | Full chain, publication and conflicts checked |
| Open texture | Not considered | Asserted | Core and penumbra reasoned with the rule's purpose |
| Four theories | One theory only | All four, generic | All four, specific to the facts, each with an objection |
| Rights and rule of law | Missing | "My rights" without analysis | Hohfeld positions and Fuller's questions applied |
| Realist prediction | None | Fewer than five decisions or no table | Five or more decisions, table, range |
| Argument from principle | Missing or a plea for sympathy | Principle without anchor or weight | Rule, anchored principle, fit, weight, narrow remedy |
| Other side | Missing or a straw man | Stated weakly | Stated so its supporters would accept it |
| Decision | None | A decision without reasons | A reasoned decision with stop signals |

## Model answer

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

This model uses the supplied case.

**Source and validity.** The burial agreement says nothing about inscriptions, and the society has no published rule. The "custom" is not a written norm. A positivist notes at once that the society is applying something that is not shown to be law or a term of the contract. Because it provides a public service, it is also bound by duties of equality and reasonableness that apply to bodies performing public functions (a point to check against the case law before relying on it).

**Open texture.** There is no rule to interpret, so the question is what terms the agreement implies and whether the society's practice is a permissible implied term. That is a gap case.

**Four theories.**
- *Natural law:* respect for the dead and for the family's mourning is a basic human good; forbidding the language in which the mother lived and the calendar the family uses touches dignity. Objection: the cemetery's religious character is also a value that matters to others buried there.
- *Positivism:* no written rule, so the society's refusal has no clear legal source; the judge has discretion to fill the gap, guided by the contract law's implied terms and by good faith. Objection: long custom can be part of a contract between the parties.
- *Dworkin:* the principles that fit Israeli law best include human dignity (Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, ss. 1–2, 4), freedom of conscience and good faith in exercising contractual rights (Contracts Law s. 39); the competing principle is the religious autonomy and uniformity of a religious cemetery. On these facts (a public service with no alternative cemetery in town, a design that keeps Hebrew and the Hebrew date), dignity weighs more. Objection: a court may give more weight to the community's religious practice.
- *Realism / critical:* a sample of recent decisions on burial and inscriptions would show how courts weigh these interests; a critical reading notes that a "uniform" custom may fall hardest on immigrant families whose language and calendar differ.

**Rights and rule of law.** Igor has no claim to any design he likes, but he has a claim that the society decide by published, relevant and equal criteria and exercise its contractual rights in good faith; the society bears the correlative duties. Fuller: the "custom" was not published (fails publicity) and was announced after the agreement (doubtful prospectivity).

**Argument from principle (summary).** Rule: the burial agreement, silent on inscriptions. Principle: dignity of the deceased and her family, anchored in Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty and in s. 39. Fit: no written rule supports the refusal; the design keeps Hebrew and the Hebrew date, so it respects the cemetery's character. Weight: uniformity and religious practice are acknowledged; a bilingual inscription that keeps the Hebrew elements limits the harm to them. Remedy: approve the design as submitted, or as modified with Russian letters smaller than the Hebrew.

**Other side.** The society's strongest argument: it runs a Jewish cemetery whose character matters to the many families buried there; uniform Hebrew inscriptions are part of that character; allowing exceptions will erode it; a family that wants a different style may choose a civil cemetery where one is available.

**Decision.** Write to the society with the argument from principle and a request for its written rules; if refused, consider a claim in the magistrates' court. Stop signals: the society relies on written rules Igor has not seen; the society is represented and the case becomes a test case; no civil burial alternative is available and the dispute widens into a constitutional question.

</details>
